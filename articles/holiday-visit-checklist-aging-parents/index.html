Talking With Your Parent

Holiday Visit Checklist: What to Notice About Your Aging Parents

By Ellen Carter. Published October 31, 2026. Publisher: Family Caregiver Practical Guide.

The Thanksgiving I remember most wasn't the one with the burned pie. It was the year I opened my mother's refrigerator to get the cream and found almost nothing in it: a few condiments, half a loaf of bread, and a casserole dish I'd seen there in August. She'd told me on the phone every week that she was "eating fine."

Holidays are often the only time adult children spend several days in a parent's home. You see things that phone calls hide. But holidays are also emotional, crowded, and short, which makes them a tricky time to raise worries. I've learned to treat the holiday visit as a time to notice and to plan, not to fix everything at the dinner table.

This checklist will help you see what's really going on, gently and without turning the visit into an inspection.

Please note: This post is general information, not medical, legal, or financial advice. Changes you notice may have many causes. If something worries you, encourage your parent to talk with their doctor. If you see an emergency, call 911.

Key takeaways

  • Holidays are a chance to observe daily life in person, not to have every hard conversation.
  • Look at the home, the fridge, the mail, and how your parent is getting around.
  • Compare what you see with what you hear on the phone.
  • Write notes, then share them calmly with siblings after the visit.
  • Pick one small next step rather than a big plan.

Why Holidays Are a Good Time to Notice

You'll be in the house for more than a quick lunch. You'll see mornings and evenings, meals and mail, how your parent handles stairs and guests. Changes that happen slowly are often invisible to the people closest to them and obvious to someone who hasn't visited in months.

The goal is to gather information, not to diagnose anything. Most changes have ordinary explanations. A few may be worth a follow-up.

The Holiday Visit Checklist

Keep this on your phone and jot notes privately. You don't need to tick every box.

Around the House

  • ☐ Is the home about as tidy as usual, or noticeably more cluttered?
  • ☐ Are there piles of unopened mail, bills, or notices?
  • ☐ Are there scorch marks on pots, or signs the stove was left on?
  • ☐ Are plants, pets, and the yard being cared for as usual?
  • ☐ Is the house a comfortable temperature?
  • ☐ Are there new tripping hazards, poor lighting, or loose rugs? (Our home safety checklist goes room by room.)

Kitchen and Food

  • ☐ Is there fresh food in the fridge, or mostly expired items?
  • ☐ Are there several of the same items, as if they were bought and forgotten?
  • ☐ Is your parent cooking the dishes they usually enjoy making?

Getting Around

  • ☐ How does your parent manage stairs, getting up from chairs, and walking outside?
  • ☐ Are they holding onto furniture or walls as they move around?
  • ☐ Any new dents or scrapes on the car? (See our guide on talking with a parent about driving.)

Daily Routines

  • ☐ Are they dressed and groomed as usual?
  • ☐ Are medications organized, or are there bottles scattered or unopened? (Don't change anything; just note it for a conversation with their doctor or pharmacist.)
  • ☐ Are they sleeping much more or less than usual?

Money and Paperwork

  • ☐ Any overdue bills or collection notices?
  • ☐ Unusual piles of catalogs, sweepstakes letters, or charity requests?
  • ☐ Have they mentioned a new "friend" asking for money, or calls about prizes? These can be signs of a scam.

Mood and Connection

  • ☐ Do they seem like themselves?
  • ☐ Are they still seeing friends and going to their usual activities?
  • ☐ Do they repeat stories or questions more than usual within the same conversation?
  • ☐ Do they seem withdrawn, anxious, or down?

Phone vs. Reality

A simple way to make sense of your notes is to compare what you've heard on the phone with what you're seeing.

What you heard on the phone What you see in person Possible next step
"I'm eating fine." Fridge nearly empty Offer grocery delivery or meal help
"The bills are all handled." Late notices on the counter Offer to sit down with the bills together
"I'm getting out plenty." No plans on the calendar Ask about friends, clubs, or rides
"The house is no trouble." Clutter, burned-out bulbs Offer a weekend of small fixes
"I'm driving just fine." New dents on the car Ride along; start the driving conversation later

How to Raise a Concern During the Visit

Not every concern needs to be raised at the holiday table. Some tips:

  • Choose a quiet one-on-one moment. A walk, washing dishes together, or a drive.
  • Lead with care, not a list. "I love being here. I noticed the stairs seem harder this year. How are they for you?"
  • Ask, don't tell. "What's the hardest part of the day right now?"
  • Pick one thing. One small next step is better than a long list.
  • Save bigger talks for later. "Can we talk more about this when I call next week?"

When to Act Right Away

Some situations can't wait until after the holidays:

  • Your parent seems in immediate danger or has a medical emergency: call 911.
  • Signs of serious self-neglect or exploitation: contact your parent's local Adult Protective Services, or ask the Eldercare Locator (eldercare.acl.gov, 1-800-677-1116) where to turn.
  • Sudden changes in confusion, speech, or movement: seek medical help promptly.

After the Visit

  1. Write up your notes while they're fresh.
  2. Share them calmly with siblings. Stick to what you saw, not conclusions. Our guide to splitting caregiving responsibilities among siblings can help you decide who does what.
  3. Choose one next step. A grocery delivery trial, a doctor's appointment, a home safety weekend.
  4. Set up your check-in rhythm. If you live far away, our long-distance caregiving guide explains how to stay connected.
  5. Get documents in order. If you haven't yet, our documents checklist is a good next project.

And Enjoy the Holiday

It's easy to spend the whole visit on alert. Make room for the reasons you came: the stories, the meal, the old photo albums. The time together matters too.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should I look for when visiting elderly parents?

Look at the home, the fridge, the mail, and how your parent gets around. Notice their mood, grooming, and routines. Compare what you see with what you've heard on the phone, and write notes privately.

Should I bring up my concerns during the holidays?

Gently, and one-on-one, if it feels right. Choose a quiet moment, ask open questions, and suggest one small step. Bigger discussions can often wait for a calmer time after the holidays.

What are signs an elderly parent needs more help at home?

Possible signs include an empty or spoiled fridge, unpaid bills, a cluttered or neglected home, trouble getting around, and withdrawal from friends. These have many possible causes, so talk with your parent and, if needed, their doctor.

What should I do after noticing problems on a visit?

Write down what you saw, share it calmly with siblings, choose one next step with your parent, and set up regular check-ins. Act right away only if there's an emergency or serious safety risk.
