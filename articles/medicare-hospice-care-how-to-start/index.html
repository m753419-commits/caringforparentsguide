Medicare Hospice Care: Who Qualifies, What It Covers, and How to Start

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 3, 2026

US programs only.

Please note: This post is general information, not legal, financial, or medical advice.

Ask your parent’s doctor and the hospice team about your parent’s situation, and confirm coverage with Medicare.gov or 1-800-MEDICARE (Medicare’s official phone line, 1-800-633-4227) (full disclaimer).

Who qualifies for Medicare hospice care?

Your parent must have Part A: Hospice care (comfort-focused care and support for someone who is terminally ill) is covered when the hospice doctor and regular doctor (if any) certify your parent is terminally ill, your parent accepts comfort care (palliative care) instead of care to cure the illness, and your parent signs a statement choosing hospice (Medicare.gov).

  1. Terminally ill means a medical prognosis that life expectancy is 6 months or less if the illness runs its normal course (42 CFR 418.3).
  2. The election statement (the form your parent signs to choose hospice) names the hospice and the attending physician your parent chose, and confirms your parent understands hospice is palliative rather than curative (42 CFR 418.24).
  3. Benefit periods (a benefit period starts the day your parent begins hospice care and ends when the 90-day or 60-day period ends): an initial 90-day period, a second 90-day period, then an unlimited number of 60-day periods (Medicare Hospice Benefits, March 2026; 42 CFR 418.21).
  4. After 6 months, hospice can continue as long as the hospice medical director or hospice doctor recertifies, after a face-to-face meeting, that your parent is still terminally ill; the face-to-face meeting is required before the 3rd benefit period and each one after (Medicare.gov; 42 CFR 418.22).
  5. Medicare Advantage (a Medicare plan from a private company): once hospice starts, Original Medicare covers everything related to the terminal illness, and your parent can stay in the plan as long as they keep paying its premiums (Medicare Hospice Benefits, March 2026).

What does Medicare hospice cover?

Most care for the terminal illness: The hospice plan of care can include doctor services, nursing care, medical equipment and supplies, drugs for pain and symptom control, hospice aide and homemaker services, therapies, social worker services, dietary counseling, grief and loss counseling, short-term inpatient care, and short-term respite care (Medicare Hospice Benefits, March 2026).

  1. Not covered: treatment intended to cure the terminal illness, room and board (the cost of living in a home or facility) at home, in a nursing home, or in a hospice inpatient facility, and hospital or ambulance care unless the hospice team arranges it or it’s unrelated to the terminal illness (Medicare.gov; Medicare Hospice Benefits, March 2026).
  2. Other health problems: Original Medicare still pays for covered benefits unrelated to the terminal illness, but your parent owes any deductible and coinsurance (their share of the cost) that apply (Medicare.gov).
  3. Ask for the non-covered list: the hospice must give the list of items, services, and drugs it decided aren’t related, with the reasons, within 3–5 days of your request (Medicare.gov).
  4. For hospice elections beginning on or after October 1, 2026, the hospice must provide an election statement addendum titled “Patient Notification of Hospice Non-Covered Items, Services, and Drugs,” within the first 5 days of the election, and update it within 3 days when the plan changes (42 CFR 418.24; 42 CFR 418.202).

What does hospice cost?

Usually little: Your parent pays nothing for hospice care from a Medicare-approved hospice, except a copayment of up to $5 for each outpatient prescription for pain and symptom management and possibly 5% for inpatient respite care, as listed on Medicare.gov (checked November 2026) (Medicare.gov).

  1. Copayment (an amount you may be required to pay as your share of the cost, usually a set amount rather than a percentage) (Medicare Hospice Benefits, March 2026).
  2. Respite: your parent may pay 5% of the Medicare-approved amount (the payment Medicare sets for a service) for inpatient respite care, and the copayment can’t be more than the inpatient deductible amount (Medicare.gov).
  3. Room and board: Medicare doesn’t cover it at home or in a nursing home, so ask the hospice and the nursing home who will pay for it and how much (Medicare.gov).
  4. 2027 amounts: Medicare has not published 2027 hospice amounts as of November 2026 (Medicare.gov).

Can family caregivers get a break during hospice?

Yes: If the usual caregiver needs rest, your parent can get inpatient respite care in a Medicare-approved facility that the hospice arranges, for up to 5 days each time, more than once but only on an occasional basis (Medicare Hospice Benefits, March 2026).

  1. Respite care is short-term inpatient care provided only when necessary to relieve the family members or others caring for your parent, and it isn’t paid for more than five consecutive days at a time (42 CFR 418.204).
  2. Four levels of care: Medicare pays hospices for routine home care, continuous home care, inpatient respite care, and general inpatient care for symptoms that can’t be managed elsewhere (CMS).
  3. Grief support: bereavement counseling (support before and after a death to help with grief, loss, and adjustment) is available, and hospices must make bereavement services available to the family for up to 1 year after the death (42 CFR 418.3, 418.64).
  4. More on respite: see our respite care guide.

Where is hospice care given, and how do we choose a provider?

Usually at home: Hospice care is usually given at home but may also be covered in an inpatient facility, such as a hospice inpatient facility, hospital, or skilled nursing facility (a nursing home with nurses and therapists on staff), and a patient’s home might be a skilled nursing facility or an assisted living facility (Medicare Hospice Benefits, March 2026; CMS).

  1. Compare hospices: Care Compare finds Medicare-certified hospices that serve your area and compares them based on the quality of care they provide (Medicare Care Compare).
  2. Switching: your parent has the right to change hospice providers once during each benefit period (Medicare.gov; 42 CFR 418.30).
  3. Ask the doctor and hospice: which services they provide at your parent’s home or facility, and who to call at night.
  4. Plan the move home: see our hospital discharge guide.

What is the difference between hospice and palliative care?

Hospice includes palliative care: Medicare says palliative care is the part of hospice care that focuses on helping people who are terminally ill and their families maintain their quality of life (Medicare Hospice Benefits, March 2026).

  1. Palliative care (care that eases pain and other symptoms and supports quality of life): federal rules define it as patient and family-centered care that optimizes quality of life by anticipating, preventing, and treating suffering (42 CFR 418.3).
  2. In hospice, the focus changes to comfort care for pain relief and symptom management instead of care to cure the illness (CMS).
  3. If your parent isn’t ready for hospice, talk with the doctor about comfort-focused care and ask what your parent’s coverage pays.

Can my parent stop hospice, and what if we have a complaint?

Yes, at any time: Your parent or their representative may revoke (cancel) the hospice election at any time, and Medicare coverage for the benefits given up resumes (42 CFR 418.28).

  1. Going back: if your parent is eligible, they can go back to hospice care at any time (Medicare Hospice Benefits, March 2026).
  2. Disagree with a decision? If your parent’s care is ending and you disagree, you can appeal (Medicare.gov fast appeals).
  3. Quality complaints: you can file with your state’s survey agency, or with the BFCC-QIO (Medicare’s quality improvement organization for patients and families) by visiting Livanta or Acentra or calling 1-800-MEDICARE (Medicare Hospice Benefits, March 2026).
  4. The hospice must tell you about the BFCC-QIO, including the right to immediate advocacy and its contact information (42 CFR 418.24).
