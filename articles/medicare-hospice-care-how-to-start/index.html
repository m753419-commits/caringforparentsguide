Medicare Hospice Care: Who Qualifies, What It Covers, and How to Start

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 7, 2026

US programs only.

Please note: This post is general information, not legal, financial, or medical advice.

Ask your parent’s doctor and the hospice team about your parent’s situation. Confirm coverage with Medicare (the federal health insurance program for people 65 and older and some younger people with disabilities). You can use Medicare.gov or call 1-800-MEDICARE. That is Medicare’s official phone line, 1-800-633-4227 (full disclaimer).

In plain words: Hospice is comfort care for someone who is terminally ill. Medicare is U.S. government health insurance for older people and some younger people with disabilities. It covers most care for the terminal illness, and your parent usually pays little. Your parent can stop at any time, so start by talking with the doctor and the hospice team.

Who qualifies for Medicare hospice care?

Your parent must have Part A (Medicare’s hospital insurance), accept comfort care (palliative care) instead of care to cure the illness, and sign a statement choosing hospice. The hospice doctor and your parent’s regular doctor, if any, must certify a life expectancy of 6 months or less if the illness runs its normal course (Medicare.gov). Medicare then covers hospice care (comfort-focused care and support for someone who is terminally ill) (Medicare.gov).

  1. The rules. Here’s what has to happen. The hospice doctor and your parent’s regular doctor (if any) certify your parent is terminally ill. That means a life expectancy of 6 months or less. This assumes the illness runs its normal course. Your parent also accepts comfort care (palliative care) instead of care to cure the illness. And your parent signs a statement choosing hospice. The hospice must get this in writing for each benefit period. A benefit period starts the day your parent begins hospice care. It ends when the 90-day or 60-day period ends (42 CFR 418.22; 42 CFR 418.3).
  2. The election statement is the form your parent signs to choose hospice. Choosing hospice is called an election. The form names the hospice and the attending physician your parent chose. That is the doctor with the main role in your parent’s medical care. The form also confirms your parent understands this. Hospice is palliative (for comfort) rather than curative (meant to cure) (42 CFR 418.24).
  3. Benefit periods. Hospice time is counted in periods. First comes a 90-day period. Then comes a second 90-day period. After that, there’s no limit on the number of 60-day periods (Medicare Hospice Benefits, March 2026; 42 CFR 418.21).
  4. After 6 months, hospice can continue. It goes on as long as the hospice medical director or hospice doctor recertifies your parent. Recertify means they confirm again that your parent is still terminally ill. They do this after a face-to-face meeting. The meeting is required before the 3rd benefit period and each one after (Medicare.gov; 42 CFR 418.22).
  5. Medicare Advantage is a Medicare plan from a private company. Once hospice starts, Original Medicare (regular Medicare) covers everything related to the terminal illness. Your parent can stay in the plan as long as they keep paying its premiums. Premiums are the regular payments for the plan (Medicare Hospice Benefits, March 2026).

What does Medicare hospice cover?

It covers most care for the terminal illness. The hospice team’s written plan of care can include the services below (Medicare Hospice Benefits, March 2026).

  1. Services. Here’s the list. The plan can include doctor services, nursing care, and medical equipment and supplies. It can include drugs for pain and symptom control. It can also include hospice aide and homemaker services. Therapies and social worker services can be part of it too. So can dietary counseling, and grief and loss counseling. So can short-term inpatient care. Inpatient means as a patient admitted to a facility. And so can short-term respite care (short-term inpatient care to give family caregivers a rest) (Medicare Hospice Benefits, March 2026).
  2. Not covered. Some things aren’t paid for. Treatment meant to cure the terminal illness isn’t covered. Neither is room and board (the cost of living in a home or facility). That’s true at home, in a nursing home, or in a hospice inpatient facility. Hospital or ambulance care isn’t covered either. The exceptions: the hospice team arranges it, or it’s unrelated to the terminal illness (Medicare.gov; Medicare Hospice Benefits, March 2026).
  3. Other health problems. Original Medicare still pays for covered benefits that aren’t related to the terminal illness. But your parent owes any deductible and coinsurance that apply. A deductible is the amount paid before Medicare starts to pay. Coinsurance is their share of the cost (Medicare.gov).
  4. Ask for the non-covered list. The hospice must give the list of items, services, and drugs it decided aren’t related. It must give the reasons too. It’s due within 3–5 days of your request (Medicare.gov).
  5. For hospice elections beginning on or after October 1, 2026, there’s an extra form. It’s an election statement addendum (an added page) titled “Patient Notification of Hospice Non-Covered Items, Services, and Drugs.” The hospice must provide it within the first 5 days of the election. It must also update it within 3 days when the plan changes (42 CFR 418.24; 42 CFR 418.202).

What does hospice cost?

Your parent usually pays nothing for hospice care from a Medicare-approved hospice, except up to $5 per outpatient prescription for pain and symptom management (Medicare.gov). Possibly, your parent pays 5% for inpatient respite care, as listed on Medicare.gov (checked November 2026) (Medicare.gov).

  1. Copayment. The first is a copayment of up to $5. A copayment is a set amount your parent pays as their share. It’s for each outpatient prescription for pain and symptom management. Outpatient means not admitted to a facility. Medicare defines a copayment as an amount you may be required to pay as your share of the cost. It’s usually a set amount rather than a percentage (Medicare Hospice Benefits, March 2026).
  2. Respite. The second is for inpatient respite care. Your parent may pay 5% of the Medicare-approved amount. That’s the payment Medicare sets for a service. This copayment can’t be more than the inpatient deductible amount (Medicare.gov).
  3. Room and board. Medicare doesn’t cover it at home or in a nursing home. So ask the hospice and the nursing home who will pay. Also ask how much (Medicare.gov).
  4. 2027 amounts. Medicare hasn’t published 2027 hospice amounts as of November 2026 (Medicare.gov).

Can family caregivers get a break during hospice?

Yes, if the usual caregiver needs rest. Your parent can get inpatient respite care for up to 5 days each time, in a Medicare-approved facility the hospice arranges, and only on an occasional basis (Medicare Hospice Benefits, March 2026).

  1. Where and how often. You need rest too. The hospice arranges this care in a Medicare-approved facility. Your parent can get it more than once, but only on an occasional basis. It’s given only when needed to relieve the family or others caring for your parent. It isn’t paid for more than five consecutive days (days in a row) at a time (42 CFR 418.204).
  2. Four levels of care. CMS is the federal agency that runs Medicare. It says Medicare pays hospices for routine home care, continuous home care, and inpatient respite care. It also pays for general inpatient care for symptoms that can’t be managed elsewhere (CMS).
  3. Grief support. Help with grief is part of hospice. Bereavement counseling is available. It’s support before and after a death to help with grief, loss, and adjustment. Hospices must make bereavement services available to the family for up to 1 year after the death (42 CFR 418.3, 418.64).
  4. More on respite. See our respite care guide.

Where is hospice care given, and how do we choose a provider?

Usually at home, but it may also be covered in an inpatient facility. Examples are a hospice inpatient facility, hospital, or skilled nursing facility (a nursing home with nurses and therapists on staff) (Medicare Hospice Benefits, March 2026; CMS).

  1. Home can be a facility. A patient’s home might be a skilled nursing facility or an assisted living facility. Assisted living is housing with help for daily needs.
  2. Compare hospices. Care Compare is Medicare’s tool for comparing providers. It finds Medicare-certified (approved by Medicare) hospices that serve your area. It compares them on the quality of care they give (Medicare Care Compare).
  3. Switching. This is allowed. Your parent has the right to change hospice providers once during each benefit period (Medicare.gov; 42 CFR 418.30).
  4. Ask the doctor and hospice. Ask which services they provide at your parent’s home or facility. Also ask who to call at night.
  5. Plan the move home. See our hospital discharge guide.

What is the difference between hospice and palliative care?

Hospice includes palliative care. Medicare says this part of hospice care focuses on helping people who are terminally ill and their families maintain their quality of life (Medicare Hospice Benefits, March 2026).

  1. Palliative care is care that eases pain and other symptoms and supports quality of life. Federal rules define it as patient and family-centered care. It optimizes (makes the best of) quality of life by anticipating, preventing, and treating suffering (42 CFR 418.3).
  2. In hospice, the focus changes to comfort care. That means pain relief and symptom management, instead of care to cure the illness (CMS).
  3. If your parent isn’t ready for hospice, talk with the doctor about comfort-focused care. Ask what your parent’s coverage pays.

Can my parent stop hospice, and what if we have a complaint?

Yes, at any time. Your parent or their representative may revoke (cancel) the hospice election at any time (42 CFR 418.28).

  1. Going back. After the election is revoked, Medicare coverage for the benefits given up resumes (starts again). If your parent is eligible, they can go back to hospice care at any time (Medicare Hospice Benefits, March 2026).
  2. Disagree with a decision? If your parent’s care is ending and you disagree, you can appeal (Medicare.gov fast appeals).
  3. Quality complaints. You can file with your state’s survey agency. That’s the state office that inspects health care providers. Or you can file with the BFCC-QIO. That’s Medicare’s quality improvement organization for patients and families. Commence or Acentra runs it, depending on the state. Find the one for your parent’s state in the CMS BFCC directory. You can also call 1-800-MEDICARE (Medicare Hospice Benefits, March 2026).
  4. The hospice must tell you about the BFCC-QIO. That includes how to contact it. It also includes the right to immediate advocacy. That’s a fast, informal way to settle a complaint (42 CFR 418.24).
