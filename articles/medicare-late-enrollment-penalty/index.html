Medicare Late Enrollment Penalties (Part B & D), Explained for Families

What is the penalty for not having Medicare Part B when you are eligible?

An extra 10% of the standard Part B premium for each full 12 months your parent could have had Part B but didn't, and it generally lasts as long as they have Part B (Medicare.gov).

  1. Count the full 12-month periods your parent went without Part B (or job-based coverage) after they could have signed up.
  2. Multiply by 10% and apply it to the standard premium, which is $202.90 a month in 2026 (CMS).
  3. Example from Medicare.gov: 2 full years (24 months) late = 20% penalty; $202.90 + 20% = $243.48, rounded to $243.50 a month in 2026.
  4. Check the exceptions. Generally there's no Part B penalty if your parent qualifies for a Special Enrollment Period (a window to sign up outside the usual times, for example within 8 months after job-based coverage ends) or enrolls in a Medicare Savings Program (state help paying Medicare costs for people with limited income).

What happens if I don't sign up for Medicare at 65?

If your parent has no job-based coverage and misses their first sign-up window, they may have to wait for the General Enrollment Period (January 1–March 31), go without coverage until it starts, and pay a lifetime Part B penalty (Medicare.gov).

  1. Find the Initial Enrollment Period: generally 3 months before the month your parent turns 65, the birthday month, and 3 months after (Medicare.gov).
  2. Sign up in that window unless your parent (or spouse) is still working and covered by that employer's group health plan.
  3. If the window was missed, sign up between January 1 and March 31. Coverage starts the month after sign-up.
  4. Ask SHIP (State Health Insurance Assistance Program: free, unbiased Medicare counseling) whether a Special Enrollment Period applies: shiphelp.org or 1-877-839-2675.

Should I enroll in Part D just to avoid late enrollment penalty?

If your parent has no other creditable drug coverage, joining when first eligible avoids a penalty that is added for as long as they have Part D (Medicare.gov). Creditable drug coverage means drug coverage expected to pay, on average, at least as much as standard Part D.

  1. Check for creditable coverage. Coverage from a current or former employer or union, TRICARE, and some other plans can count. The plan must tell your parent in writing whether it is creditable; keep that notice.
  2. Know the trigger: going 63 days or more in a row without Part D or creditable coverage after the Initial Enrollment Period.
  3. Know the math: 1% of the national base beneficiary premium (a figure CMS sets each year) for each full month without coverage. The base is $38.99 in 2026 (Medicare.gov) and $41.33 in 2027 (CMS). Medicare.gov's example: 14 months late = 14% × $38.99 = $5.46, rounded to $5.50 a month in 2026. At the 2027 base the same 14% is $5.79, rounded to $5.80 (our calculation).
  4. Check Extra Help. Generally there is no Part D penalty if your parent qualifies for Extra Help (the program that helps people with limited income pay drug costs).

Can I delay Medicare if I am still working?

Often yes, if your parent (or their spouse) is still working and has job-based group health coverage; they then get an 8-month Special Enrollment Period to sign up after the job or coverage ends (Medicare.gov).

  1. Ask the employer's benefits office whether your parent needs Part A and Part B at 65 for the job-based plan to pay.
  2. Confirm it's group health plan coverage from current work. Medicare.gov warns that coverage not from current employment, such as retiree coverage, doesn't count; sign up at 65 to avoid the Part B penalty.
  3. Mark the 8-month window. It starts when the job ends or the coverage ends, whichever comes first.
  4. Keep proof of coverage from the employer. Keep it with your parent's Medicare papers.

Does COBRA protect against the Part B penalty?

No. COBRA (the federal law that may let your parent keep an employer's health plan for a limited time after the job ends) doesn't extend the Part B deadline: the 8-month window starts when work stops, whether or not they choose COBRA (Medicare.gov).

  1. Write down the date work (or coverage) ended.
  2. Sign up for Part B within 8 months of that date, even if COBRA is still active.
  3. If the 8 months pass, the next chance is January 1–March 31, which may mean a gap in coverage and a lifetime Part B penalty.

Is there a penalty for buying Part A late?

Only if your parent has to buy Part A: the premium may go up 10%, for twice the number of years they could have had it but didn't sign up (Medicare.gov). About 99% of people with Medicare pay no Part A premium (CMS).

  1. Check work credits. 40 or more quarters of coverage generally means premium-free Part A.
  2. Know the 2026 premium if buying: $311 a month with 30–39 quarters, $565 a month with fewer than 30 (CMS).
  3. Example from Medicare.gov: eligible for 2 years without signing up = the higher premium for 4 years.

Can a late enrollment penalty be appealed?

Yes. For Part D, your parent can ask for a "reconsideration" within 60 days of the date on the letter telling them about the penalty; for Part B, ask Social Security or SHIP whether a Special Enrollment Period applies (Medicare.gov publication 11222).

  1. Part D: fill out the reconsideration form sent with the plan's letter and return it within 60 days. A Medicare contractor not connected with the plan decides, generally within 90 days (CMS). Keep paying the penalty while waiting (Medicare.gov publication 11222).
  2. Gather proof of creditable drug coverage (the yearly notices) or Extra Help.
  3. Part B: generally there's no penalty with a Special Enrollment Period or a Medicare Savings Program (Medicare.gov). In limited cases where a federal government error caused the delay, there may be other relief; ask Social Security or SHIP.
  4. Get free help from SHIP (1-877-839-2675) or Medicare (1-800-633-4227).

Does the penalty go away at some point?

Usually not. Medicare.gov says late enrollment penalties are usually charged for as long as your parent has that type of coverage (for most people, a lifetime penalty); the Part A penalty is different (Medicare.gov).

  1. Part A: the higher premium lasts for twice the number of years your parent didn't sign up (for example, 2 years late means 4 years of the penalty).
  2. Part D and Extra Help: Medicare says people who qualify for Extra Help won't be charged a late enrollment penalty while they're getting Extra Help, and months without coverage before they qualified don't count toward a penalty (Medicare.gov publication 11222).
  3. Part B and Medicare Savings Programs: Medicare.gov says there's generally no Part B penalty for people who enroll in a Medicare Savings Program; ask your parent's state Medicaid office whether they qualify.
  4. If you think the penalty is wrong, ask for a review (see the previous question), or get free help from SHIP (1-877-839-2675).

My parent has Medicare Advantage. Can there still be a penalty?

Yes. Your parent still pays the monthly Part B premium in a Medicare Advantage Plan, so a Part B penalty can still apply (Medicare.gov), and a Part D penalty can apply if the plan includes drug coverage (Medicare.gov).

  1. Check what your parent pays for Part B each month. Medicare.gov says late enrollment penalties are added to the monthly premium.
  2. Return the plan's form on time. If a plan with drug coverage finds a possible break of 63 or more days in a row without creditable drug coverage, it sends a notice and a form asking about prior drug coverage; complete, sign, and return it by the deadline on the form (Medicare.gov, publication 11222).
  3. Don't expect switching plans to remove it. Medicare.gov says the Part D penalty is added for as long as your parent has Medicare drug coverage, even if they switch plans.
  4. If your parent's income and resources are limited, apply for Extra Help (apply through Social Security) and, through your parent's state, for a Medicare Savings Program.
