Early Signs of Dementia in a Parent: What to Do Next and What Memory Care Costs

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 4, 2026

US programs only.

Please note: This post is general information, not legal, financial, or medical advice. It is not a diagnosis; ask a doctor about any changes in your parent's memory or health.

Only a doctor can find the cause of memory changes: the CDC (the U.S. public health agency) says people with warning signs should see a doctor to find the cause (CDC; full disclaimer).

What are the early signs of dementia?

Watch for changes that disrupt daily life: NIA (the National Institute on Aging, a federal research agency) lists memory loss that disrupts daily life, poor judgment, trouble handling money and paying bills, and wandering and getting lost among the signs of mild Alzheimer's disease (the most common form of dementia, a loss of thinking, remembering, and reasoning that gets in the way of daily life) (NIA).

  1. More signs NIA lists: losing track of dates or knowing current location, taking longer to complete normal daily tasks, repeating questions or forgetting recently learned information, losing things or misplacing them in odd places, mood and personality changes, and increased anxiety and/or aggression (NIA).
  2. Signs the CDC lists for dementia: getting lost in a familiar neighborhood, using unusual words to refer to familiar objects, forgetting the name of a close family member or friend, forgetting old memories, and not being able to complete common tasks on your own (CDC).
  3. Normal aging vs. dementia: NIA contrasts making a bad decision once in a while or missing a monthly payment (age-related forgetfulness) with making poor judgments and decisions a lot of the time or problems taking care of monthly bills (signs of dementia) (NIA).
  4. Mild cognitive impairment (MCI: more memory or thinking problems than other people the same age, but not dementia): NIA estimates 10 to 20% of people age 65 or older with MCI develop dementia over a one-year period, but not everyone with MCI does, and symptoms may stay the same or even improve (NIA, content reviewed 2021; NIA).

Could memory problems be caused by something else?

Yes, sometimes: NIA says medical conditions such as depression or blood clots can cause memory problems, and these problems usually go away once the condition is successfully treated (NIA).

  1. Medicines: medication side effects are on NIA's list of factors that may cause memory problems (NIA).
  2. Body and brain conditions: head injury, blood clots, tumors, or infections in the brain, and thyroid, kidney, or liver problems (NIA).
  3. Mood, sleep, and diet: depression and anxiety, alcohol or drug misuse, sleep problems, low levels of nutrients such as vitamin B12, and not eating enough healthy foods (NIA).
  4. Tell the doctor about all of these so they can check for causes that can be treated (NIA).

What should I do if I notice signs in my parent?

Help your parent see a doctor: NIA says to talk with a doctor about noticeable changes in memory, such as asking the same questions over and over or getting lost in places you used to know well (NIA).

  1. Write down what you notice, using NIA's signs that it may be time to see a doctor, such as trouble following recipes or directions, more confusion about time, people, and places, or not taking care of themselves (NIA).
  2. What to bring: insurance cards, names and phone numbers of other doctors, medical records if the doctor doesn't have them, and a list of all medicines, vitamins, herbal remedies, over-the-counter drugs, and supplements with names, dosage, and schedule (NIA).
  3. Yearly wellness visit: Medicare (the federal health insurance program for people 65 and older) covers an Annual Wellness Visit (a yearly prevention checkup), and in it the provider will do a cognitive assessment (a check of thinking abilities like memory and decision-making) to look for signs of dementia; your parent pays nothing if the provider accepts assignment (agrees to Medicare's approved amount as full payment), and the deductible (the yearly amount paid before coverage starts) for Part B (Medicare's medical insurance) doesn't apply, though extra tests in the same visit may cost more (Medicare.gov, current page as of November 2026; Medicare.gov).
  4. Separate cognitive visit: Medicare Part B covers a separate visit with a doctor or other health care provider to fully review thinking, confirm a diagnosis like dementia, and make a care plan; your parent pays 20% of the Medicare-approved amount after the Part B deductible (Medicare.gov).

How is dementia diagnosed?

With several checks, not one test: NIA says doctors first look for an underlying, potentially treatable condition, using a physical exam, lab tests, a review of medical and family history, and cognitive and neurological tests (tests of memory, problem solving, language, balance, and reflexes) (NIA).

  1. Who diagnoses: a primary care doctor is often the first step, and neurologists (doctors who specialize in disorders of the brain and nervous system) are often consulted; geriatric psychiatrists (mental health doctors for older adults), neuropsychologists (specialists in testing thinking and memory), and geriatricians (doctors who specialize in older adults) may also be able to diagnose dementia (NIA).
  2. Brain scans: CT, MRI, or PET scans (pictures of the brain) can support an Alzheimer's diagnosis or rule out other causes (NIA).
  3. Blood tests: NIA says blood test results alone should not be used to diagnose dementia but may be considered along with other tests, and availability is still limited (NIA).
  4. Ask about the cognitive assessment visit described above so the doctor can make a care plan (Medicare.gov).

What legal and money steps should we take early?

Plan while your parent can still sign: NIA's Alzheimers.gov says financial planning documents must be created while the person still has the legal capacity (the ability, under the law, to understand and make a decision) to make decisions (Alzheimers.gov).

  1. Durable power of attorney for finances: names someone who will make financial decisions for your parent when they are not able (Alzheimers.gov).
  2. Advance directives (legal documents with instructions for medical care that only take effect if your parent can't communicate their wishes): the two most common are the living will (written instructions about medical care) and the durable power of attorney for health care, which names a proxy (a person who makes medical decisions for them) (NIA).
  3. Guard against scams: the CFPB (the federal consumer financial protection agency) warns that losing money to scams, fraud, and exploitation can be especially devastating to older adults (CFPB).
  4. Compare the documents: see our guardianship vs. power of attorney guide and documents checklist.

How do I keep a parent with memory problems safe?

Plan for wandering: NIA says many people with Alzheimer's disease wander away from their home or caregiver, so caregivers need to know how to limit wandering and prevent the person from becoming lost (NIA).

  1. ID and photo: make sure your parent carries ID or wears a medical bracelet with their name, address, and your phone number, and keep a recent photo or video to help police (NIA).
  2. Doors and yard: NIA suggests keeping doors locked, a keyed deadbolt or an extra lock placed up high or down low, a smart doorbell or alarm that chimes when a door opens, and a fenced yard with a locked gate (NIA).
  3. Tell neighbors and local police that your parent tends to wander; NIA notes many GPS systems can track a person's location (see our GPS tracker guide) (NIA).
  4. Home and car: see our home safety checklist and driving conversation guide; NIA says not to leave a person with a history of wandering unattended (NIA).

What is memory care, and how much does it cost?

Costs are not published by official sources as of November 2026: memory care (a care unit or home that specializes in people living with dementia) has no federal definition, state rules differ, and NIA says assisted living (housing with help for daily care, but less than a nursing home) is almost always paid for out of pocket (NIA).

  1. What NIA says: some long-term care facilities offer special programs for people with Alzheimer's disease and other types of dementia, and assisted living tends to cost more than living independently but less than a nursing home (NIA).
  2. Florida example: a facility that advertises special care for persons with Alzheimer's disease must meet standards such as awake staff on duty at all hours (17 or more residents), activities designed for people who are cognitively impaired, and trained staff (Fla. Stat. 429.178; Florida Senate).
  3. California example: a residential care facility for the elderly (a California licensing category) that advertises special care for persons with dementia must disclose its special features, such as staff training and physical environment, to the state (Cal. Health & Safety Code 1569.627; California Legislature); Texas HHS (Texas Health and Human Services, the state agency) lists an Alzheimer's certification for assisted living facilities (Texas HHS).
  4. For cost ranges, see our assisted living cost section.

Does Medicare or Medicaid pay for memory care?

Medicare doesn't, and Medicaid may help in part: Medicare doesn't pay for long-term care, also called custodial care (help with everyday tasks like bathing and dressing), and NIA says Medicaid (a joint federal and state program that helps with health costs for people with limited income and resources) may cover some aspects of assisted living depending on the state (Medicare.gov; NIA).

  1. Medicare: NIA states that Medicare does not pay for assisted living, and Medicare.gov says you pay all costs of long-term care (NIA; Medicare.gov).
  2. Medicaid: states can set up HCBS waivers (Medicaid programs that pay for long-term care services at home or in the community instead of in an institution) (Medicaid.gov).
  3. Room and board isn't covered: federal rules bar federal Medicaid funding for HCBS room and board (rent and meals), with narrow exceptions such as respite care (short-term care that gives a caregiver a break) in a state-approved facility (42 CFR 441.310; eCFR).
  4. Veterans: the VA (the federal agency for veterans' benefits) Aid and Attendance benefit adds monthly payments to a VA pension (a monthly VA payment) for qualified veterans, for example those who need help with bathing, feeding, and dressing; for December 1, 2025, through November 30, 2026, the maximum annual pension for a veteran with no dependents who qualifies is $29,093; see the linked VA rates page for the latest amount (VA; VA).
  5. Nursing home Medicaid: see our guide to Medicaid and nursing homes.

Where can families get dementia help and support?

Start with two phone lines: the Eldercare Locator (a federal service connecting you to local help for older adults) is at 1-800-677-1116, and NIA's ADEAR Center (NIA's dementia information service) is at 800-438-4380 (Eldercare Locator; NIA).

  1. Eldercare Locator: call or text 1-800-677-1116 or chat online with trained staff (Eldercare Locator).
  2. ADEAR Center: 800-438-4380, English and Spanish, 8:30 a.m.–5:00 p.m. ET, Monday–Friday (NIA).
  3. Alzheimer's Association: NIA points families to the Alzheimer's Association (a private dementia organization) for local support groups; its free 24/7 helpline is 800-272-3900 (NIA; Alzheimer's Association).
  4. Take care of yourself: see our guides to respite care and caregiver burnout.
