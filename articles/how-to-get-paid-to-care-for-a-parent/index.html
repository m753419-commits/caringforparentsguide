Can I get paid for taking care of my elderly mother?

Possibly. The most common route is a Medicaid home care program that lets your mother hire you; VA programs, long-term care insurance, or a written agreement paid from her own money are the other routes (KFF; Medicaid.gov, Self-Directed Services).

  1. Call her state Medicaid office or Area Agency on Aging (find it through the Eldercare Locator) and ask: "Do you have a self-directed home care program, and can an adult child be the paid caregiver?" Details are in the Medicaid question below.
  2. If she is a veteran, ask VA about the Program of Comprehensive Assistance for Family Caregivers (PCAFC). VA says the Veteran must have a VA disability rating of 70% or higher, need at least 6 months of continuous, in-person personal care, and be enrolled in VA health care; the caregiver must be at least 18 and a family member (including a son or daughter) or live full time with the Veteran. A Primary Family Caregiver may get a monthly stipend, health coverage through CHAMPVA if not otherwise covered, and at least 30 days of respite care a year. Apply together with VA Form 10-10CG; VA says it assigns a caregiver no later than 90 days after it gets the application. Questions: VA Caregiver Support Line, 1-855-260-3274.
  3. Ask about VA's other options. In Veteran-Directed Care, the Veteran gets help developing a spending plan and hiring workers, which VA says might include a family member. Aid and Attendance or Housebound benefits are monthly payments added to a VA pension, paid to your parent, not to you. For December 1, 2025, to November 30, 2026, VA lists a yearly limit (Maximum Annual Pension Rate) of $29,093 with Aid and Attendance for a Veteran with no dependents; VA pays the difference between that limit and the Veteran's countable income, and the net worth limit (assets plus yearly income) is $163,699 (VA pension rates).
  4. Check private options. Some long-term care insurance policies may pay a family member, depending on the policy (NIA); call the insurer. Or use a written caregiver agreement (your parent pays you from their own money for specific care); payments can affect later Medicaid eligibility and taxes, so have an elder law attorney draft or review it.
  5. If you work, check leave. The federal Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) gives eligible employees up to 12 workweeks of unpaid, job-protected leave in a 12-month period, including to care for a parent with a serious health condition (US Department of Labor). Ask your HR office and your state labor department whether paid family leave is available.

Does Medicare pay family caregivers?

Generally no. Medicare doesn't pay for long-term care, also called custodial care (help with bathing, dressing, eating, and using the bathroom), and it has no program that pays a family member to be the caregiver (Medicare.gov).

  1. Use Medicare for what it does cover: part-time skilled home health care for a homebound patient, given by a Medicare-certified agency (Medicare.gov, home health services).
  2. Turn to Medicaid or VA for paid family caregiving (see the other questions on this page).
  3. Get free help sorting out coverage from your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP: free, unbiased Medicare counseling) at shiphelp.org or 1-877-839-2675.

Does Medicaid pay for family members to be a caregiver?

Yes, in some circumstances in every state that answered KFF's 2024 survey (48 states including D.C.), usually through home care programs your parent can self-direct. Your parent must qualify for Medicaid and the program (KFF).

Self-directed means your parent (or their representative) chooses, hires, trains, and supervises their own caregiver (Medicaid.gov). Home- and community-based services (HCBS) are Medicaid services that help people get care at home instead of in a nursing home; many are offered through 1915(c) waivers.

  1. Check your parent's Medicaid eligibility with the state Medicaid office: income and asset limits, and, for most waivers, a required level of care.
  2. Ask which program fits your parent's age and needs, whether it allows self-direction, and whether an adult child can be paid. KFF found all responding states except Alaska allow self-direction in at least some programs.
  3. Ask about a waiting list and get on it early; waiver programs can limit how many people they serve.
  4. Ask about structured family caregiving. In this model Medicaid pays a daily rate and the family caregiver gets paid plus support. KFF reported it for seniors and people with disabilities in 7 states: Connecticut, Georgia, Indiana, Louisiana, North Carolina, North Dakota, and South Dakota.
  5. Ask about training, background checks, and payroll. Many programs use a fiscal intermediary (a company that handles payroll and taxes).

Can VA help me care for a parent who is a veteran?

Possibly. VA has two programs for family caregivers: the Program of Comprehensive Assistance for Family Caregivers (PCAFC), which can include a monthly stipend (payment) for the main caregiver, and the Program of General Caregiver Support Services (PGCSS), which offers training, coaching, and peer support; separately, Aid and Attendance adds money to your parent's VA pension if they need help with daily activities (VA.gov, checked November 2026).

  1. Make sure your parent is enrolled in VA health care. Both caregiver programs require it. If your parent isn't enrolled yet, they apply for VA health care first.
  2. Apply for PCAFC if your parent qualifies. VA says the Veteran needs a VA disability rating (the percentage VA assigns to a Veteran's disability) of 70% or higher and at least 6 months of continuous, in-person personal care (help with things like bathing, feeding, and dressing). You and your parent apply together on VA Form 10-10CG (online, by mail, or in person), and you both sign it. A Primary Family Caregiver may get a monthly stipend, health coverage through CHAMPVA (VA's health insurance for certain family members) if not covered by another plan, and at least 30 days of respite care (a break from caregiving) a year (VA PCAFC).
  3. If PCAFC doesn't fit, ask about PGCSS. Contact the caregiver support team at your parent's nearest VA facility (VA caregiver support teams) or ask your parent's VA health care team for a referral. You don't need to be a relative or live with your parent; your parent must agree and list you as their caregiver in their VA health record. Services include peer support mentoring, skills training, coaching, telephone support, online programs, and referrals (VA PGCSS).
  4. If your parent needs help with daily activities, apply for Aid and Attendance. It's paid to your parent, not to you, on top of a VA pension. The pension is for wartime Veterans without a dishonorable discharge who are 65 or older (or meet another condition, such as a permanent and total disability) and whose income and net worth are within limits (VA pension eligibility). For December 1, 2025, to November 30, 2026, the yearly limit with Aid and Attendance for a Veteran with no dependents is $29,093, and the net worth limit is $163,699 (VA pension rates). A medical examiner fills out part of VA Form 21-2680; if your parent is in a nursing home, add VA Form 21-0779 (VA Aid and Attendance).
  5. Get free help. Call the VA Caregiver Support Line at 1-855-260-3274 (Monday to Friday, 8:00 a.m. to 8:00 p.m. ET). For pension claims, an accredited Veterans Service Organization (VSO) representative helps for free; VA notes that accredited attorneys and claims agents can charge fees (VA accredited representatives).

How can my wife become my paid caregiver?

It's harder for a spouse. KFF says payment rules are stricter for "legally responsible relatives," which include spouses; for waiver programs, states may pay them only for "extraordinary care" beyond what a spouse would normally provide (KFF).

  1. Ask the state Medicaid office whether any program pays legally responsible relatives, and under what conditions.
  2. If you are a veteran, a spouse can apply as a family caregiver under VA's PCAFC (see the first question).
  3. Ask about respite care either way; KFF found 47 responding states cover it for family caregivers.

How much do family caregivers get paid?

It depends on the program and state. KFF says that in most cases family caregivers paid through Medicaid get hourly wages like other employees; in structured family caregiving programs, which pay a per diem (daily) rate, the examples KFF cites are around $40 or $50 per day (KFF).

  1. Ask the state Medicaid office or case manager what hourly rate the program pays and how many hours your parent's care plan allows.
  2. Ask whether the state has structured family caregiving and what its daily rate is.
  3. For VA's PCAFC, the Primary Family Caregiver may get a monthly stipend; VA explains how it's set on its PCAFC page.
  4. For a caregiver agreement paid from your parent's own money, the amount is whatever the written agreement says; have an elder law attorney review it (see the first question).

Do I have to pay taxes on caregiver payments?

Possibly; it depends on the program and your situation. The IRS says certain Medicaid waiver payments for caring for someone who lives in your home may be excludable from income under Notice 2014-7 (IRS).

  1. Find out where the money comes from: a state Medicaid home- and community-based services (HCBS) waiver program, VA, or your parent's own funds.
  2. If it's a Medicaid waiver payment and your parent lives in your home under their plan of care, read the IRS questions and answers on Notice 2014-7.
  3. Know that excluded doesn't always mean no tax: the IRS says payments to employees can be excludable from income but generally still count as wages for Social Security and Medicare tax.
  4. For any other payments, including a caregiver agreement, ask a tax professional.

Can my parent get help paying for prescription drugs (Extra Help)?

Yes, if your parent has Medicare and limited income and resources (savings and things they own). Extra Help is a Medicare program that helps pay Medicare drug coverage (Part D) premiums, deductibles, and copays. 2026 limits for one person: income $23,940 a year and resources $18,090; for a married couple: income $32,460 a year and resources $36,100 (Medicare.gov, 2026).

Extra Help: 2026 limits (Medicare.gov)
Who2026 yearly income limit2026 resource limit
One person$23,940$18,090
Married couple$32,460$36,100
  1. Check whether your parent already has it. Medicare.gov says people who get full Medicaid, a Medicare Savings Program (see the next question), or Supplemental Security Income (SSI) get Extra Help automatically and receive a notice in the mail.
  2. Gather documents for your parent and their spouse: bank statements and tax returns; IRA or 401(k) balances; and statements for pensions, veterans' benefits, annuities, and Railroad Retirement Board benefits (Social Security).
  3. Apply with Social Security online, or call 1-800-772-1213 (TTY 1-800-325-0778) and ask for an appointment to apply for Part D Extra Help. Your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP: free Medicare counseling) can help you apply at shiphelp.org.
  4. Let the same application start a Medicare Savings Program. Social Security sends the information to your parent's state to start that application unless you say no on the Extra Help form (Medicare.gov).
  5. Know what your parent will pay in 2027. Medicare.gov says that in 2027, people with Extra Help pay a $0 plan premium and a $0 deductible; in 2027, generic drugs cost up to $5.80 each and brand-name drugs up to $14.40 each at plan pharmacies. If your parent doesn't qualify now, they can reapply any time their income or resources change.

Can my parent get help paying Medicare premiums (Medicare Savings Programs)?

Possibly. Medicare Savings Programs are state programs that help pay Medicare Part B (medical insurance) premiums and, in the QMB program, also Part A (hospital insurance) premiums, deductibles, and coinsurance; Medicare.gov says to apply even if you don't think your parent qualifies, because some states allow more income or resources (Medicare.gov, 2026).

  1. Find your parent's state Medicaid office on Medicaid.gov's state contact list, which lists a Medicare Savings Program contact for each state, and apply there. The state decides which program your parent qualifies for.
  2. Or start with Extra Help (see the question above); Social Security will pass the information to the state unless you say no.
  3. Ask the state which proof it needs of income and resources, and have records ready, such as Social Security benefit letters, pension statements, and bank statements.
  4. If your parent is in QMB, have them show both their Medicare card and their Medicaid or QMB card each time they get care. Medicare providers aren't allowed to bill them for Medicare-covered services, including deductibles, coinsurance, and copays.
  5. If your parent is in QI, reapply every year. States approve QI applications first come, first served, with priority for people who had QI the year before.

Can my parent get a monthly check if they have little income (SSI)?

Possibly. Supplemental Security Income (SSI) is a monthly payment from Social Security for people who are 65 or older, blind, or have a disability and have little income and resources; the 2026 maximum is $994 a month for one person or $1,491 for a couple, the 2027 maximum is $1,029 or $1,544, and the resource limit in both 2026 and 2027 is $2,000 for one person or $3,000 for a couple (SSA, 2026 and 2027).

SSI: federal amounts for 2026 and 2027 (SSA)
Who2026 maximum monthly payment2027 maximum monthly payment2026 and 2027 resource limit
One person$994$1,029$2,000
Couple$1,491$1,544$3,000
  1. Check the rules on SSA's Who can get SSI page. SSA counts income from work and other sources, such as pensions, and says citizens 65 or older don't need a disability to get SSI. Noncitizens must meet extra rules.
  2. Make an appointment soon. Call 1-800-772-1213 (TTY 1-800-325-0778); you can call for your parent. SSA can't pay for months before the application date, and if your parent keeps the appointment and files, SSA may use the date of the call as the filing date (SSA).
  3. Gather documents (SSA list): Social Security card or number; proof of age, such as a birth record; proof of citizenship or immigration status, such as a U.S. passport or Permanent Resident Card (Form I-551); proof of income, such as award letters and bank statements; proof of resources, such as bank statements, life insurance policies, burial contracts, and vehicle titles; and proof of living arrangements, such as a lease or rent receipt and the household's rent and utility costs. SSA needs originals or certified copies, not photocopies, and returns them.
  4. Read the written decision. SSA sends a notice for every decision, and most can be appealed. If your parent gets SSI, Medicare.gov says they also get Extra Help automatically.

Can my parent get help buying food (SNAP)?

Possibly. SNAP (the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program) loads money for groceries onto an EBT card (it works like a debit card) each month. From October 1, 2026, to September 30, 2027, a household with someone 60 or older only has to pass the net income test (limit for that period: $1,330 a month for one person, $1,804 for two) and can have up to $4,750 in countable resources (USDA, October 2026–September 2027).

HouseholdOct 2026–Sep 2027 net monthly income limitOct 2026–Sep 2027 resource limitOct 2026–Sep 2027 maximum monthly benefit
One person$1,330$4,750$306
Two people$1,804$4,750$562
  1. Know the rules for people 60 and older. Net income is income after deductions. Under USDA's rules for October 2026 to September 2027, medical costs over $35 a month that insurance doesn't pay can be deducted, including doctor bills, prescription drugs, dentures, certain transportation costs, and health insurance premiums, and the shelter deduction has no cap. The home and lot and most retirement plans don't count as resources. From October 2026 to September 2027, the most a household can get is $306 a month for one person or $562 for two (48 states and D.C.).
  2. Apply in the state where your parent lives through the local SNAP office, the state website, or the state hotline; find them in USDA's SNAP State Directory. If your parent can't go, they can name someone in writing as an authorized representative to apply and do the interview.
  3. Bring proof of what you report, such as Social Security, pension, or VA benefit letters (USDA counts these as income), rent or mortgage, property tax, and utility bills, and receipts for medical costs; USDA says proof of medical expenses is required for the deduction.
  4. Do the interview. It's usually by phone or in person. The state usually decides within 30 days, and benefits count back to the application date; very low-income households may get benefits within 7 days.
  5. If you disagree with the decision, ask for a fair hearing within 90 days. USDA says a 2025 law (the One Big Beautiful Bill Act) changed SNAP work requirements and noncitizen eligibility and that it is still updating its page, so confirm current rules with your state.
