How to Get Paid to Care for a Parent (Medicaid, VA & More)

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 4, 2026

US programs only.

In plain words: The most common way to get paid is a Medicaid home care program. Medicaid is a joint federal and state program for people with limited income and resources. Medicare, U.S. government health insurance for older people and some younger people with disabilities, generally doesn't pay family caregivers. VA programs and other benefits may also help.

Can I get paid for taking care of my elderly mother?

Possibly: the most common route is a Medicaid home care program that lets your mother hire you. Other routes are VA programs, long-term care insurance, or a written agreement paid from her own money (KFF; Medicaid.gov, Self-Directed Services).

  1. Call her state Medicaid office or Area Agency on Aging (find it through the Eldercare Locator). Medicaid is health coverage for eligible people with low incomes, including older adults and people with disabilities. Each state runs it, and the state and the federal government pay for it jointly (Medicaid.gov). An Area Agency on Aging is a local office that helps older adults find services. Ask: "Do you have a self-directed home care program, and can an adult child be the paid caregiver?" Self-directed means your parent hires and directs their own caregiver. Details are in the Medicaid question below.
  2. If she is a veteran, ask VA about the Program of Comprehensive Assistance for Family Caregivers (PCAFC). VA says the Veteran must meet all of these rules. A VA disability rating of 70% or higher. A disability rating is the percentage VA assigns to a Veteran's disability. Discharge from the U.S. military, or a date of medical discharge. A need for at least 6 months of continuous, in-person personal care. Enrollment in VA health care. The caregiver must be at least 18. They must also be a family member (including a son or daughter) or live full time with the Veteran. A Primary Family Caregiver may get a monthly stipend (payment). They may also get at least 30 days of respite care a year. Respite care is a break from caregiving. They may also get health coverage through CHAMPVA if not otherwise covered. CHAMPVA is VA's health insurance for certain family members. Apply together with VA Form 10-10CG. VA says it assigns a caregiver no later than 90 days after it gets the application. Questions: VA Caregiver Support Line, 1-855-260-3274.
  3. Ask about VA's other options. In Veteran-Directed Care, the Veteran gets help making a spending plan and hiring workers. VA says workers might include a family member. Aid and Attendance or Housebound benefits are monthly payments added to a VA pension. They are paid to your parent, not to you. VA pays the difference between a yearly limit and the Veteran's countable income. The yearly limit is called the Maximum Annual Pension Rate. Countable income is the income VA counts. A net worth limit (assets plus yearly income) also applies (VA pension rates). The current limits are in the VA question below.
  4. Check private options. Some long-term care insurance policies may pay a family member, depending on the policy (NIA). Call the insurer. Or use a written caregiver agreement. In it, your parent pays you from their own money for specific care. Payments can affect later Medicaid eligibility and taxes. So have an elder law attorney draft or review it. An elder law attorney is a lawyer who helps older adults and their families interpret state laws. They also help plan how wishes will be carried out and understand financial options (NIA). Medicaid rules on these agreements vary by state. For example, Nevada's and North Carolina's Medicaid manuals require the written agreement to be signed before the care is given. They treat payment for care given in the past as a transfer of assets. That means money given away without fair value in return (Nevada Medicaid manual, section F-415.23, MTL 04/23; North Carolina Medicaid manual MA-2240, revised September 1, 2025).
  5. If you work, check leave. The federal Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) is a leave law. It gives eligible employees up to 12 workweeks of unpaid, job-protected leave in a 12-month period. That includes leave to care for a parent with a serious health condition (US Department of Labor). Ask your HR office and your state labor department whether paid family leave is available.

Does Medicare pay family caregivers?

Generally no: Medicare doesn't pay for long-term (custodial) care, like help with bathing, dressing, eating, and using the bathroom. It also has no program that pays a family member to be the caregiver (Medicare.gov).

  1. Use Medicare for what it does cover. That is part-time skilled home health care for a homebound patient. Homebound means leaving home takes great effort. The care is given by a Medicare-certified agency, one approved by Medicare (Medicare.gov, home health services).
  2. Turn to Medicaid or VA for paid family caregiving (see the other questions on this page).
  3. Get free help sorting out coverage from your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP). SHIP gives free, unbiased Medicare counseling. Reach it at shiphelp.org or 1-877-839-2675.

Does Medicaid pay for family members to be a caregiver?

Yes, in some cases, in every state that answered KFF's 2024 survey (48 states including D.C.). It's usually through home care programs your parent can self-direct (KFF).

Self-directed means your parent (or their representative) chooses, hires, trains, and supervises their own caregiver (Medicaid.gov). Home- and community-based services (HCBS) are Medicaid services. They help people get care at home instead of in a nursing home. Many are offered through 1915(c) waivers (a state Medicaid program that pays for care at home or in the community instead of a nursing home).

  1. Check your parent's Medicaid eligibility with the state Medicaid office. Your parent must qualify for Medicaid and the program. Ask about income and asset limits. For most waivers, also ask about the required level of care.
  2. Ask which program fits your parent's age and needs. Ask whether it allows self-direction and whether an adult child can be paid. KFF is a health policy research group. It found all responding states except Alaska allow self-direction in at least some programs.
  3. Ask about a waiting list and get on it early. Waiver programs can limit how many people they serve.
  4. Ask about structured family caregiving. In this model, Medicaid pays a daily rate. The family caregiver gets paid plus support. KFF reported it for seniors and people with disabilities in 7 states. They are Connecticut, Georgia, Indiana, Louisiana, North Carolina, North Dakota, and South Dakota.
  5. Ask about training, background checks, and payroll. Many programs use a fiscal intermediary (a company that handles payroll and taxes).

Can VA help me care for a parent who is a veteran?

Possibly: VA has two programs for family caregivers. Separately, Aid and Attendance adds money to your parent's VA pension if they need help with daily activities (VA.gov, checked November 2026).

  1. Make sure your parent is enrolled in VA health care. Both caregiver programs require it. If your parent isn't enrolled yet, they apply for VA health care first.
  2. Apply for PCAFC if your parent qualifies. PCAFC is the Program of Comprehensive Assistance for Family Caregivers. It can include a monthly stipend (payment) for the main caregiver. VA says the Veteran needs a VA disability rating of 70% or higher. The rating is the percentage VA assigns to a Veteran's disability. The Veteran must have been discharged from the U.S. military or have a date of medical discharge. They also need at least 6 months of continuous, in-person personal care. That means help with things like bathing, feeding, and dressing. You and your parent apply together on VA Form 10-10CG (online, by mail, or in person). You both sign it. A Primary Family Caregiver may get a monthly stipend. They may also get at least 30 days of respite care (a break from caregiving) a year. And they may get health coverage through CHAMPVA if not covered by another plan. CHAMPVA is VA's health insurance for certain family members (VA PCAFC).
  3. If PCAFC doesn't fit, ask about PGCSS. PGCSS is the Program of General Caregiver Support Services. It offers training, coaching, and peer support. Contact the caregiver support team at your parent's nearest VA facility (VA caregiver support teams). Or ask your parent's VA health care team for a referral. You don't need to be a relative or live with your parent. But your parent must agree and list you as their caregiver in their VA health record. Services include peer support mentoring, skills training, coaching, telephone support, online programs, and referrals (VA PGCSS).
  4. If your parent needs help with daily activities, apply for Aid and Attendance. It's paid to your parent, not to you, on top of a VA pension. The pension is for wartime Veterans without a dishonorable discharge. It is for those 65 or older. It is also for those who meet another condition, such as a permanent and total disability. It also requires income and net worth within limits (VA pension eligibility). For December 1, 2025, to November 30, 2026, the yearly limit with Aid and Attendance is $29,093. That is for a Veteran with no dependents. The net worth limit is $163,699 (VA pension rates). A medical examiner fills out part of VA Form 21-2680. If your parent is in a nursing home, add VA Form 21-0779 (VA Aid and Attendance).
  5. Get free help. Call the VA Caregiver Support Line at 1-855-260-3274. It's open Monday to Friday, 8:00 a.m. to 8:00 p.m. ET. For pension claims, an accredited Veterans Service Organization (VSO) representative helps for free. Accredited means approved by VA. VA notes that accredited attorneys and claims agents can charge fees (VA accredited representatives).

How can my wife become my paid caregiver?

It's harder for a spouse, since KFF says payment rules are stricter for "legally responsible relatives," which include spouses. For waiver programs, states may pay them only for "extraordinary care" beyond what a spouse would normally provide (KFF).

  1. Ask the state Medicaid office whether any program pays legally responsible relatives, and under what conditions.
  2. If you are a veteran, a spouse can apply as a family caregiver under VA's PCAFC. See the first question.
  3. Ask about respite care either way. KFF found 47 responding states cover it for family caregivers.

How much do family caregivers get paid?

It depends on the program and state. KFF says that in most cases family caregivers paid through Medicaid get hourly wages like other employees (KFF, January 2025).

  1. Ask the state Medicaid office or case manager what hourly rate the program pays. A case manager is the person who arranges your parent's services. Also ask how many hours your parent's care plan allows.
  2. Ask whether the state has structured family caregiving and what its daily rate is. These programs pay a per diem (daily) rate. The examples KFF cites are around $40 or $50 per day.
  3. For VA's PCAFC, the Primary Family Caregiver may get a monthly stipend. VA explains how it's set on its PCAFC page.
  4. For a caregiver agreement paid from your parent's own money, the written agreement sets the amount. Have an elder law attorney review it (see the first question).

Do I have to pay taxes on caregiver payments?

Possibly; it depends on the program and your situation. Under Notice 2014-7, certain Medicaid waiver payments for caring for someone in your home may be excludable from income (IRS).

  1. Find out where the money comes from. Is it a state Medicaid home- and community-based services (HCBS) waiver program? Or is it VA, or your parent's own funds?
  2. If it's a Medicaid waiver payment, check where your parent lives. If they live in your home under their plan of care, read the IRS questions and answers on Notice 2014-7. A plan of care is the written plan for their care. Excludable means not counted as income.
  3. Know that excluded doesn't always mean no tax. The IRS says payments to employees can be excludable from income. But they generally still count as wages for Social Security and Medicare tax.
  4. For any other payments, including a caregiver agreement, ask a tax professional.

Can my parent get help paying for prescription drugs (Extra Help)?

Yes, if your parent has Medicare and limited income and resources (savings and things they own). The 2026 limits for one person are $23,940 a year in income and $18,090 in resources (Medicare.gov, 2026; CMS, 2026).

Extra Help is a Medicare program that helps pay Medicare drug coverage (Part D) premiums, deductibles, and copays. Premiums are regular charges for coverage. Deductibles and copays are your parent's share of the cost. Limits are higher for a married couple (see the table). The one-person resource limit includes $1,500 for burial expenses.

Extra Help: 2026 limits (Medicare.gov; CMS)
Who2026 yearly income limit2026 resource limit
One person$23,940$18,090 with burial expenses / $16,590 without
Married couple$32,460$36,100 with burial expenses / $33,100 without

Social Security uses the higher limit if your parent expects to use some savings for funeral or burial costs (CMS, 2026; SSA).

  1. Check whether your parent already has it. Medicare.gov names who gets Extra Help automatically. It is people who get full Medicaid or a Medicare Savings Program (see the next question). It is also people who get Supplemental Security Income (SSI). They receive a notice in the mail.
  2. Gather documents for your parent and their spouse. These include bank statements and tax returns. Include IRA or 401(k) balances, which are retirement savings accounts (IRS). Also gather statements for pensions, veterans' benefits, annuities, and Railroad Retirement Board benefits (Social Security). Annuities are contracts with an insurance company that pay regular income in return for earlier payments (Investor.gov).
  3. Apply with Social Security online. Or call 1-800-772-1213 (TTY 1-800-325-0778) and ask for an appointment to apply for Part D Extra Help. TTY is a phone service for people who are deaf or hard of hearing. Your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP: free Medicare counseling) can help you apply at shiphelp.org.
  4. Let the same application start a Medicare Savings Program. Social Security sends the information to your parent's state to start that application. It does this unless you say no on the Extra Help form (Medicare.gov).
  5. Know what your parent will pay. In 2026, people with Extra Help pay a $0 deductible. They pay up to $5.10 for each generic drug or $12.65 for each brand-name drug. A generic drug is a lower-cost copy of a brand-name drug. Some people who also have full Medicaid pay less (CMS, 2026). Starting in 2027, people with Extra Help pay a $0 plan premium and a $0 deductible. At plan pharmacies, they pay up to $5.80 for each generic drug or $14.40 for each brand-name drug (Medicare.gov, 2027). If your parent doesn't qualify now, they can reapply any time their income or resources change.

Can my parent get help paying Medicare premiums (Medicare Savings Programs)?

Possibly: Medicare Savings Programs are state programs that help pay Medicare Part B (medical insurance) premiums. Apply even if you don't think your parent qualifies, Medicare.gov says, since some states allow more income or resources (Medicare.gov, 2026).

In the QMB program, they also help pay Part A (hospital insurance) premiums, deductibles, and coinsurance. Coinsurance is a percentage share of the cost.

Medicare Savings Programs: 2026 federal limits, one person (married couple) (Medicare.gov)
ProgramHelps pay2026 monthly income limit2026 resource limit
QMB (Qualified Medicare Beneficiary)Part A and Part B premiums, deductibles, coinsurance, copays$1,350 ($1,824)$9,950 ($14,910)

2026 income limits are slightly higher in Alaska and Hawaii. Anyone in one of these programs also gets Extra Help with drug costs.

  1. Find your parent's state Medicaid office on Medicaid.gov's state contact list. It lists a Medicare Savings Program contact for each state. Apply there. The state decides which program your parent qualifies for.
  2. Or start with Extra Help (see the question above). Social Security will pass the information to the state unless you say no.
  3. Ask the state which proof it needs of income and resources. Have records ready, such as Social Security benefit letters, pension statements, and bank statements.
  4. If your parent is in QMB, have them show both cards each time they get care. One is their Medicare card. The other is their Medicaid or QMB card. Medicare providers aren't allowed to bill them for Medicare-covered services, including deductibles, coinsurance, and copays.
  5. If your parent is in QI, reapply every year. States approve QI applications first come, first served, with priority for people who had QI the year before.

Can my parent get a monthly check if they have little income (SSI)?

Possibly, if your parent is 65 or older, blind, or has a disability and has little income and resources. In 2026, the federal SSI payment is up to $994 a month for one person (up to $1,029 in 2027) (SSA, 2026 and 2027; SSA).

Supplemental Security Income (SSI) is a monthly payment from Social Security. Some states add a state supplement on top of it.

SSI: federal amounts for 2026 and 2027 (SSA)
Who2026 federal payment, up to (monthly)2027 federal payment, up to (monthly)2026 and 2027 resource limit
One person$994$1,029$2,000
Couple$1,491$1,544$3,000
  1. Check the rules on SSA's Who can get SSI page. SSA counts income from work and other sources, such as pensions. It says citizens 65 or older don't need a disability to get SSI. Noncitizens must meet extra rules.
  2. Make an appointment soon. Call 1-800-772-1213 (TTY 1-800-325-0778). You can call for your parent. SSA can't pay for months before the application date. If your parent keeps the appointment and files, SSA may use the date of the call as the filing date (SSA).
  3. Gather documents (SSA list). Include a Social Security card or number. Include proof of age, such as a birth record. Include proof of citizenship or immigration status, such as a U.S. passport or Permanent Resident Card (Form I-551). Include proof of income, such as award letters and bank statements. Include proof of resources, such as bank statements, life insurance policies, burial contracts, and vehicle titles. Add proof of living arrangements, such as a lease or rent receipt. Also include the household's rent and utility costs. SSA needs originals or certified copies, not photocopies, and returns them.
  4. Read the written decision. SSA sends a notice for every decision, and most can be appealed. If your parent gets SSI, Medicare.gov says they also get Extra Help automatically.
  5. If your parent lives with family, how housing costs are shared can change the SSI amount. SSA can lower SSI when others pay your parent's shelter costs. SSA calls this in-kind support and maintenance (ISM). ISM is shelter, and in some cases meals, that others pay for or provide (SSA Spotlight on Living Arrangements, 2026 edition). For someone in their own household, ISM is valued at no more than $351.33 a month in 2026 (SSA POMS SI 00835.901, 2026 table). See whether your parent should pay rent.

Can my parent get help buying food (SNAP)?

Possibly: SNAP (the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program) loads money for groceries onto a card each month. From October 1, 2026, to September 30, 2027, a household with someone 60 or older only has to pass the net income test (USDA, October 2026–September 2027).

The card is an EBT card, and it works like a debit card. Net income is income after deductions. The net income limit for that period is $1,330 a month for one person. The household can have up to $4,750 in countable resources. These are savings and other things that count toward the limit. Examples are cash or money in a bank account.

SNAP: limits for October 1, 2026, to September 30, 2027, for households with someone 60 or older (USDA; 48 states and D.C.)
HouseholdOct 2026–Sep 2027 net monthly income limitOct 2026–Sep 2027 resource limitOct 2026–Sep 2027 maximum monthly benefit
One person$1,330$4,750$306
Two people$1,804$4,750$562
  1. Know the rules for people 60 and older. USDA's rules here are for October 2026 to September 2027. Medical costs insurance doesn't pay can be deducted for the part over $35 a month. This includes doctor bills, prescription drugs, dentures, certain transportation costs, and health insurance premiums. The shelter deduction, for housing costs, has no cap. The home and lot and most retirement plans don't count as resources. From October 2026 to September 2027, the most a household can get is $306 a month for one person. It is $562 for two (48 states and D.C.).
  2. Apply in the state where your parent lives. Use the local SNAP office, the state website, or the state hotline. Find them in USDA's SNAP State Directory. If your parent can't go, they can name someone in writing as an authorized representative. That is someone who applies and does the interview for them.
  3. Bring proof of what you report. Examples are Social Security, pension, or VA benefit letters. USDA counts these as income. Also bring rent or mortgage, property tax, and utility bills, and receipts for medical costs. USDA says proof of medical expenses is required for the deduction.
  4. Do the interview. It's usually by phone or in person. The state usually decides within 30 days. Benefits count back to the application date. Very low-income households may get benefits within 7 days.
  5. If you disagree with the decision, ask for a fair hearing within 90 days. A fair hearing is a review of the decision. USDA says a 2025 law (the One Big Beautiful Bill Act) changed SNAP work requirements and noncitizen eligibility. It says it is still updating its page. So confirm current rules with your state.
