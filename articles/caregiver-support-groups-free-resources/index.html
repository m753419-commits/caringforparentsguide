Free Help for Family Caregivers: Support Groups and Resources

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 4, 2026

US programs only.

Please note: This post is general information, not legal, financial, or medical advice.

Programs and phone numbers can change. Check details with each agency before you rely on them (full disclaimer).

In plain words: This guide lists help for people who care for a parent. Some of it is free. You are not alone, and help exists for stress too. Your first step is one call to the Eldercare Locator.

Where can I find help as a family caregiver?

Call the Eldercare Locator at 1-800-677-1116 for local help. It is a public service of the Administration for Community Living, the federal agency for aging and disability programs (Eldercare Locator).

  1. Call, text, or chat: You can call or text trained staff. You can also chat online with the Eldercare Locator (Eldercare Locator).
  2. Search by place: The Eldercare Locator's Find a Resource page finds help near you. Just type in a place (Eldercare Locator).
  3. Your Area Agency on Aging: This is the local public office for older adults' services. Under federal law, these agencies give support services to family caregivers. Groups they hire may also do this (42 U.S.C. 3030s-1; govinfo).
  4. What NIA says: NIA is the National Institute on Aging, a federal research agency. It says the Eldercare Locator can help you find in-home help and rides. It can also give facts about paying for care (NIA).

How do I find a caregiver support group near me?

Join an online or in-person caregiver support group, NIA suggests. People there know what you're going through and may have advice (NIA).

  1. Ask your parent's health care team: NIA says doctors and nurses may know of groups. They may know about respite care and other help too. Respite care is short-term care that gives a caregiver a break (NIA).
  2. Call the Eldercare Locator at 1-800-677-1116 to reach local services (Eldercare Locator).
  3. If your parent has dementia: NIA points families to two groups for local support groups. One is the Alzheimer's Association (a private dementia organization). The other is the Alzheimer's Foundation of America (a private dementia organization). The Alzheimer's Association's free 24/7 helpline is 800-272-3900 (NIA; Alzheimer's Association).
  4. Dementia facts: NIA's ADEAR Center (a government information center you can call) answers questions at 800-438-4380. It helps in English and Spanish. Hours are 8:30 a.m.–5:00 p.m. ET, Monday–Friday (NIA).
  5. Caring for a veteran: The VA has caregiver programs. The VA is the federal agency for veterans' benefits. They include group support. See the veterans question below (VA Caregiver Support Program).

What does the National Family Caregiver Support Program offer?

It must offer five kinds of help: facts, help getting services, counseling and training, respite care, and some extra help. This is under the Older Americans Act (42 U.S.C. 3030s-1(b); govinfo).

  • What the words mean: The Older Americans Act is a federal law. It funds services for older adults. The National Family Caregiver Support Program gives states federal money to help family caregivers. The third kind covers counseling, support groups, and training. The law calls the extra services limited supplemental services. That means a bit of extra help on top of the care you give.
  1. Who it serves: The law describes support services for family caregivers. Area Agencies on Aging or groups they hire provide them (42 U.S.C. 3030s-1(a); govinfo).
  2. Training and counseling: The law covers one-on-one counseling and setting up support groups. It also covers caregiver training in health, food, and money skills. It also covers how to make choices and solve care problems (42 U.S.C. 3030s-1(b)(3); govinfo).
  3. Respite and extra services are only for caregivers of frail older people. Older means age 60 or older. In plain terms, here is our summary of 42 U.S.C. 3002(22). The older person can't do at least two activities of daily living (basic everyday self-care tasks) without substantial help from another person (including reminders or supervision). Or the person needs substantial supervision because a cognitive or other mental impairment leads to behavior that is a serious health or safety hazard to themselves or others (42 U.S.C. 3030s-1(c); uscode.house.gov; 42 U.S.C. 3002(22); uscode.house.gov).
  4. Dementia priority: States must put caregivers of older people with dementia first. This means Alzheimer's disease (the most common form of dementia) and related disorders (42 U.S.C. 3030s(b); govinfo).
  5. To apply, contact your Area Agency on Aging. Reach it through the Eldercare Locator at 1-800-677-1116 (Eldercare Locator).

What help is there for caregivers of veterans?

Call the VA Caregiver Support Line at 1-855-260-3274. It is open Monday–Friday, 8am–8pm ET (VA).

  1. General caregiver support (PGCSS): This is the VA's Program of General Caregiver Support Services. It offers skills training, mobile support, one-on-one coaching, and group support. It also offers self-care, peer mentors, phone support, online programs, and referrals. It is open to caregivers of veterans who served at any time. The veteran must be in VA health care (VA).
  2. Comprehensive assistance (PCAFC): This is the VA's Program of Comprehensive Assistance for Family Caregivers. The caregiver must be at least 18. They must be a family member or live full time with the veteran. The veteran must have a VA disability rating of 70% or higher. A disability rating is the VA's percentage score for a disability. The veteran must need in-person personal care for at least 6 months in a row. They must be enrolled in VA health care. There are other rules too (VA).
  3. Monthly stipend: This is a payment to an eligible main family caregiver. The VA starts with the yearly pay for the federal GS-4, step 1 grade. It uses the pay for the veteran's area. GS is a federal government pay scale. The VA divides that by 12. Then it multiplies by 0.625 (Level One) or 1.00 (Level Two). VA pages don't list a dollar amount. That is because it depends on location (VA).
  4. Respite: See our respite care guide.

Where can I learn caregiving skills?

Use NIA's caregiving guides on caring for your parent and for yourself. They include worksheets and tips on sharing the work (NIA).

  1. Caregiver training: The National Family Caregiver Support Program must offer it (42 U.S.C. 3030s-1(b)(3); govinfo).
  2. Veterans' caregivers can get skills training and online programs through PGCSS (VA).
  3. Dementia care: The ADEAR Center gives research-based facts to families of people with dementia (NIA).
  4. Share the load: See splitting caregiving among siblings and local services that fill the gaps.

Where can I get help with Medicare, money, and benefits?

Start with SHIP (State Health Insurance Assistance Program) for free one-on-one help with Medicare. Medicare is the federal health insurance for people 65 and older (SHIP).

  1. SHIP counselors can help compare costs and review coverage. They can check provider networks. They can also help during Open Enrollment. That is the yearly time to change Medicare plans. Counseling is private (SHIP). See also our Medicare help guide.
  2. Find benefits: USA.gov (the U.S. government's official website) has a benefit finder. It lets you explore government benefits and learn how to apply. The old Benefits.gov address now sends you there (USA.gov).
  3. Managing a parent's money: The CFPB has easy guides. It is the federal agency that protects consumers' money. They are for people who manage money for a loved one. This includes agents under a power of attorney. That is a legal paper letting someone act for another person. It also includes guardians named by a court (CFPB).
  4. Getting paid or taking leave: See how to get paid to care for a parent and FMLA basics. FMLA is the federal law on job-protected family leave.

What if I need help right now?

Call or text 988: the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline is free, private, and open 24/7/365. It is for everyone and can help you or a loved one during difficult moments (988 Lifeline).

  1. Caregiver stress is real: You may feel anxious, frustrated (worried, upset), or depressed. NIA says help is there (NIA).
  2. Know the warning signs: See when to get help right away.
  3. Take a break: See how to find respite care near you.
