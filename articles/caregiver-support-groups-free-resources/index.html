Free Help for Family Caregivers: Support Groups and Resources

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 4, 2026

US programs only.

Please note: This post is general information, not legal, financial, or medical advice.

Programs and phone numbers can change, so confirm details with each agency before you rely on them (full disclaimer).

Where can I find free help as a family caregiver?

Start with the Eldercare Locator: a public service of the Administration for Community Living (the federal agency for aging and disability programs) connects you to services for older adults and their families at 1-800-677-1116 (Eldercare Locator).

  1. Call, text, or chat: you can call or send a text message to trained staff, or chat online, through the Eldercare Locator (Eldercare Locator).
  2. Search by location: the Eldercare Locator's Find a Resource page lets you find help in your community by entering a location (Eldercare Locator).
  3. Your Area Agency on Aging (the local public office for older adults' services): under federal law, these agencies, or groups they contract with, provide support services for family caregivers (42 U.S.C. 3030s-1; govinfo).
  4. What NIA says: NIA (the National Institute on Aging, a federal research agency) notes the Eldercare Locator can help you find in-home help and transportation and give information about paying for care (NIA).

How do I find a caregiver support group near me?

Ask locally and look online: NIA suggests joining an online or in-person support group for caregivers, people who will know what you're going through and may have suggestions or advice (NIA).

  1. Ask your parent's health care team: NIA says health care professionals may know about support groups, respite care (short-term care that gives a caregiver a break), and other resources in your community (NIA).
  2. Call the Eldercare Locator at 1-800-677-1116 to reach local services (Eldercare Locator).
  3. If your parent has dementia: NIA points families to the Alzheimer's Association (a private dementia organization) or the Alzheimer's Foundation of America (a private dementia organization) for local support groups; the Alzheimer's Association's free 24/7 helpline is 800-272-3900 (NIA; Alzheimer's Association).
  4. Dementia information: NIA's ADEAR Center (NIA's dementia information service) answers questions at 800-438-4380, English and Spanish, 8:30 a.m.–5:00 p.m. ET, Monday–Friday (NIA).
  5. Caring for a veteran: VA (the federal agency for veterans' benefits) caregiver programs include group support (see the veterans question below) (VA Caregiver Support Program).

What does the National Family Caregiver Support Program offer?

Five kinds of help: under the Older Americans Act (the federal law that funds services for older adults), the National Family Caregiver Support Program (federal grants to states for family caregiver services) must include information, help getting services, counseling and support groups and training, respite care, and limited supplemental services (42 U.S.C. 3030s-1(b); govinfo).

  1. Who it serves: the law describes support services for family caregivers and for older relative caregivers (in plain terms, our summary: older relatives who take on caregiving for a family member; the legal definition is in 42 U.S.C. 3002, govinfo), provided through Area Agencies on Aging or groups they contract with (42 U.S.C. 3030s-1(a); govinfo).
  2. Training and counseling: the law covers individual counseling, organizing support groups, and caregiver training in health, nutrition, and financial literacy, and in making decisions and solving problems about caregiving (42 U.S.C. 3030s-1(b)(3); govinfo).
  3. Respite and supplemental services are limited to caregivers of older individuals (people age 60 or older) who are frail (in plain terms, our summary: an older person who needs a lot of help with daily care because of physical or mental limits; the legal definition is in 42 U.S.C. 3002) (42 U.S.C. 3030s-1(c); 42 U.S.C. 3002; govinfo).
  4. Dementia priority: states must give priority to caregivers of older individuals with Alzheimer's disease (the most common form of dementia) and related disorders (42 U.S.C. 3030s(b); govinfo).
  5. To apply, contact your Area Agency on Aging through the Eldercare Locator at 1-800-677-1116 (Eldercare Locator).

What help is there for caregivers of veterans?

Call the VA Caregiver Support Line: the VA line is 1-855-260-3274, Monday–Friday, 8am–8pm ET (VA).

  1. General caregiver support (PGCSS: the VA's Program of General Caregiver Support Services): skills training, mobile support, one-on-one coaching, group support, self-care, peer support mentoring, telephone support, online programs, and referrals; it is available to caregivers of veterans enrolled in VA health care who served in any era (VA).
  2. Comprehensive assistance (PCAFC: the VA's Program of Comprehensive Assistance for Family Caregivers): the caregiver must be at least 18 and a family member or live full time with the veteran, and the veteran must have a VA disability rating (the VA's percentage score for a disability) of 70% or higher, need at least 6 months of continuous in-person personal care, and be enrolled in VA health care, among other rules (VA).
  3. Monthly stipend (a payment to an eligible primary family caregiver): the VA bases it on the federal GS-4, step 1 (a federal government pay grade) annual pay rate for the veteran's locality divided by 12, times 0.625 (Level One) or 1.00 (Level Two); VA pages don't list a dollar amount because it depends on location (VA).
  4. Respite: see our respite care guide.

Where can I learn caregiving skills for free?

Use NIA's caregiving guides: NIA's caregiving pages explain how to be an effective caregiver while also taking care of yourself, with worksheets and tips on sharing caregiving responsibilities (NIA).

  1. Caregiver training is one of the services the National Family Caregiver Support Program must include (42 U.S.C. 3030s-1(b)(3); govinfo).
  2. Veterans' caregivers can get skills training and online programs through PGCSS (VA).
  3. Dementia care: the ADEAR Center gives evidence-based information to families of people living with dementia (NIA).
  4. Share the load: see splitting caregiving among siblings and local services that fill the gaps.

Is there free help with Medicare, money, and benefits?

Yes: your local SHIP (State Health Insurance Assistance Program) offers one-on-one Medicare (the federal health insurance program for people 65 and older) counseling at no cost (SHIP).

  1. SHIP counselors can help compare costs, review coverage, check provider networks, and make decisions during Open Enrollment (the yearly window to change Medicare plans), and counseling is confidential (SHIP); see also our Medicare help guide.
  2. Find benefits: USA.gov's benefit finder lets you explore government benefits and learn how to apply; the old Benefits.gov address now redirects there (USA.gov).
  3. Managing a parent's money: the CFPB (the federal consumer financial protection agency) offers easy-to-understand guides for people managing money for a loved one, including agents under a power of attorney and court-appointed guardians (CFPB).
  4. Getting paid or taking leave: see how to get paid to care for a parent and FMLA basics.

What if I need help right now?

Call or text 988: the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (a free, confidential crisis line) is available 24/7/365, is for everyone, and can help you or a loved one during difficult moments (988 Lifeline).

  1. Caregiver stress is real: NIA says if you are feeling anxious, frustrated, or depressed, help is available (NIA).
  2. Know the warning signs: see when to get help right away.
  3. Take a break: see how to find respite care near you.
