How to Read a Parent's Medicare Summary Notice

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 9, 2026

US programs only.

Last checked: November 2026

This is general information, not advice for one person's plan. Check Medicare.gov or call 1-800-MEDICARE before you act. 1-800-MEDICARE is Medicare's official phone line, 1-800-633-4227.

You can also ask Social Security (the federal agency that pays retirement and disability benefits). Or ask SHIP (the State Health Insurance Assistance Program) for free, fair Medicare help (full disclaimer).

Found a Medicare Summary Notice in your parent's mail and not sure whether to pay it? Don't pay from it. It isn't a bill. It's a claims notice. Use it to check the provider's bills before anyone pays.

In plain words: A Medicare Summary Notice, or MSN, is a claims notice, not a bill. Medicare is U.S. government health insurance for older people and some younger people with disabilities. The notice is for Original Medicare, also called regular Medicare. Compare it with the provider's bills before anyone pays.

Part A, Medicare's hospital insurance, is one part on the notice; it helps pay for hospital stays, skilled nursing facility care, hospice and some home health care. Part B, Medicare's medical insurance, is the other part on many notices; it helps pay for doctor visits, outpatient care, medical equipment and many preventive services.

A confusing notice is common. Start with the name and the claim dates.

Is a Medicare Summary Notice a bill?

No. Before anyone pays, line up three things: the provider's bill, what was already paid, and any other insurance. Medicare.gov's Medicare Summary Notice page describes it this way.

Keep the receipts and bills, and compare them with the services, supplies, or equipment on the notice. If your parent has other insurance, see whether it covers anything Medicare didn't.

Common mistake: Paying a bill twice. If a bill was already paid, compare that bill with the notice, and don't pay the notice total again. Max You May Be Billed is a cap, the most the provider may bill. It isn't a balance due today. Neither is Total You May Be Billed, which the summary may also show.

How often does the paper notice come?

Every 6 months, if your parent had services or supplies in that period. If there weren't any, Medicare doesn't send a paper notice. That's how the current Medicare.gov page puts it, so use the 6-month rule only as stated there. Don't call it a 2026 edition rule; the page doesn't give a start date for this mailing.

Electronic notices work differently. If your parent chooses them, an email brings a link in any month with a processed claim. This guide didn't test signing in, and it doesn't claim you can switch without an account.

All of this applies to Original Medicare. It isn't the appeal path for a Medicare Advantage plan, a Medicare-approved plan from a private company. For that, see the guide on a rehab denial or care that is ending.

What should I check on the notice?

Go through these items in order. They follow Medicare.gov's sample Part B notice.

  1. Name and the last 4 digits of the Medicare number. This keeps you from checking someone else's notice. Don't type the Medicare number into this website.
  2. Print date and claim dates. Make sure you're comparing the right period.
  3. Visit date, provider, and service. The visit date is the day your parent went to the doctor. Check each line against your receipts and bills.
  4. Approved or denied. Then look at what Medicare paid and the maximum you may be billed.
  5. The Notes under the lines. Read them. Don't look only at the amounts.

What if a service was denied or my parent does not remember it?

Call the provider first and ask whether the claim information was correct. If it wasn't, the office may resubmit the claim. You may still appeal, though an appeal may not change the result. The Medicare.gov notice page covers these steps.

A service your parent doesn't remember isn't proof of fraud by itself. This guide didn't review any one case for fraud. Compare the date, the service, the Notes, and the receipts, and ask the provider what was sent.

Keep an eye on the calendar while the office checks. The appeal date on the notice still applies, so don't let it pass while you wait. If you disagree, the last page of the notice gives step-by-step appeal directions.

How do we appeal an Original Medicare claim on the notice?

File a redetermination, the first Original Medicare appeal, by the date printed on the notice. Send it to the Medicare claims office listed on the last page. That office is the MAC, or Medicare Administrative Contractor: the company that handles the claim. Medicare.gov's page on appeals in Original Medicare sets out the steps.

You can follow the instructions on the notice, send a written request, or use the redetermination form. Mail the papers to the address in the notice.

About that first date: the sample Part B notice says you have 120 days to appeal, and the date in the box is when the appeal must be received. Don't count from the visit date yourself. Medicare.gov also says you must file by the date in the notice.

Missed it? You may still file if you show good cause, such as a disability, illness, or accident that caused the delay. A late appeal isn't guaranteed to be accepted.

The 180-day path is not the Medicare Advantage 65-day path. For 2026, the minimum amount at OMHA, the Office of Medicare Hearings and Appeals that can hold a hearing, is $200. For a federal court case, it's $1,960. Neither amount is a fee to file the first appeal.

Can I appeal for my parent?

Not just because you're their child. If you file as the appointed representative, include proof of that appointment. Use the Appointment of Representative form, or another written paper that has the required parts, as Medicare.gov's Original Medicare appeals page explains.

This guide didn't sign in to a parent's account or check any family's permission. And this path is only for Original Medicare claims. A Medicare Advantage plan uses a different appeal, covered in the guide on a Medicare Advantage denial or ending covered care.

What should I write down while I check?

One line per service, in your own notes. This is a blank list from this guide, not an official Medicare form. The fields follow the sample notice.

  1. The notice period, the provider, the service date, the service description, and whether it was approved or denied.
  2. What Medicare paid, the maximum that may be billed, what was already paid, and whether other insurance is still to check.
  3. The Notes, the date you called the office, and the reply.
  4. The appeal due date printed on the notice, and your next step.

Don't copy the sample's dollar amounts as what your parent owes. And keep the Medicare number off this website.
