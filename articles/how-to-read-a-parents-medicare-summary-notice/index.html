How to Read a Parent's Medicare Summary Notice

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 7, 2026

US programs only.

Please note: This post is general information, not legal, financial, or medical advice.

This is general information, not advice for one person's plan. Check Medicare.gov or call 1-800-MEDICARE before you act. 1-800-MEDICARE is Medicare's official phone line, 1-800-633-4227.

You can also ask Social Security (the federal agency that pays retirement and disability benefits). Or ask SHIP (the State Health Insurance Assistance Program) for free, fair Medicare help (full disclaimer).

In plain words: This guide shows how to check a parent's Medicare Summary Notice (MSN, a claims notice, not a bill). Medicare is U.S. government health insurance for older people and some younger people with disabilities. It is for Original Medicare (regular Medicare). Compare the notice with the provider's bills before anyone pays.

Part A (Medicare's hospital insurance; it helps pay for hospital stays, skilled nursing facility care, hospice and some home health care) is one part on the notice (as listed on Medicare.gov (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov).

Part B (Medicare's medical insurance; it helps pay for doctor visits, outpatient care, medical equipment and many preventive services) is the other part on many notices (as listed on Medicare.gov (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov).

A confusing notice is common. Start with the name and the claim dates.

Is a Medicare Summary Notice a bill?

No, a Medicare Summary Notice is not a bill. Compare the provider's bill, what was already paid, and any other insurance before anyone pays (as listed on Medicare.gov (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov).

  1. Keep the receipts and bills. Compare them with the notice so you can see the services, supplies, or equipment listed (as listed on Medicare.gov (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov).
  2. If a bill was already paid, compare that bill with the notice. Do not pay the notice total again (as listed on Medicare.gov (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov).
  3. If there is other insurance, check it. See whether it covers anything Medicare did not (as listed on Medicare.gov (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov).
  4. Read Max You May Be Billed as a cap, not a balance due today. It is the most the provider may bill. The summary may also say Total You May Be Billed. That total is not a balance due today either. Also check payments already made and other insurance (as listed on the sample Part B MSN (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov sample).

How often does the paper notice come?

A paper notice comes every 6 months when there were services or supplies in that period. If there were none, Medicare does not send a paper notice (as listed on Medicare.gov (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov).

  1. Use the 6-month rule only as the current page states it. Do not call it a 2026 edition rule. The page does not give a start date for this mailing (as listed on Medicare.gov (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov).
  2. An electronic notice is different. If your parent chooses electronic notices, an email brings a link in any month with a processed claim (as listed on Medicare.gov (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov).
  3. This guide did not test signing in. It does not claim you can switch notices without an account.
  4. This notice path is for Original Medicare. It is not the appeal path for a Medicare Advantage plan (a Medicare-approved plan from a private company) (as listed on Medicare.gov (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov; Medicare.gov).

For a Medicare Advantage plan, see our guide on a rehab denial or care that is ending.

What should I check on the notice?

Check the name, the last 4 digits of the Medicare number, the print date, and the claim dates. Next, compare each service with the bills and the Notes (as listed on the sample Part B MSN (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov sample).

  1. Match the name and the last 4 digits. That helps you avoid another person's notice. Do not type the Medicare number into this website (as listed on the sample Part B MSN (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov sample).
  2. Match the print date and the claim dates. Compare the right period (as listed on the sample Part B MSN (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov sample).
  3. Compare the visit date, the provider, and the service. The visit date is the date your parent went to the doctor. Use your receipts and bills (as listed on the sample Part B MSN (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov sample).
  4. See if the claim was approved or denied. Also see what Medicare paid, and the maximum you may be billed (as listed on Medicare.gov (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov).
  5. Read the Notes under the lines. Do not look only at the amounts (as listed on the sample Part B MSN (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov sample).

What if a service was denied or my parent does not remember it?

Call the provider and ask whether the claim information was correct. If it was not, the office may resubmit the claim. You may still appeal (as listed on Medicare.gov (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov). In plain terms, an appeal may not change the result.

  1. A forgotten service is not proof of fraud by itself. This guide did not review any one case for fraud.
  2. Compare the date, the service, the Notes, and the receipts. Ask the provider what was sent (as listed on the sample Part B MSN (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov sample).
  3. If you disagree, use the last page of the notice. It gives step-by-step appeal directions (as listed on Medicare.gov (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov).
  4. Do not wait out the appeal date while the office checks the claim. The date on the notice still applies (as listed on Medicare.gov (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov).

How do we appeal an Original Medicare claim on the notice?

File a redetermination, the first Original Medicare appeal, by the date printed on the notice. Send it to the Medicare claims office on the last page (as listed on Medicare.gov (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov).

  1. The sample Part B notice says you have 120 days to appeal. The date in the box is when the appeal must be received. Do not start that count from the visit date yourself (as listed on the sample Part B MSN (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov sample). Medicare.gov also says you must file by the date in the notice (as listed on Medicare.gov (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov).
  2. You can follow the instructions on the notice. You can send a written request. Or you can use the redetermination form. Mail the papers to the address in the notice (as listed on Medicare.gov (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov).
  3. That office is the MAC (Medicare Administrative Contractor), the company that handles the claim. The MAC generally decides within 60 days after it gets the appeal (as listed on Medicare.gov (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov).
  4. If the date is missed, you may still file if you show good cause. Good cause can include a disability, illness, or accident that delayed you. A late appeal is not guaranteed to be accepted (as listed on Medicare.gov (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov).
  5. If you disagree with the MAC, you have 180 days after you get its decision letter or a notice. Ask a QIC (Qualified Independent Contractor), an outside reviewer that did not take part in the level 1 decision. This 180-day path is not the Medicare Advantage 65-day path (as listed on Medicare.gov (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov).

For 2026, the minimum at OMHA (the Office of Medicare Hearings and Appeals, the office that can hold a hearing) is $200 (as listed on Medicare.gov (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov).

For 2026, the minimum for a federal court case is $1,960 (as listed on Medicare.gov (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov).

Those amounts are not a fee to file the first appeal (as listed on Medicare.gov (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov).

Can I appeal for my parent?

Not just because you are their child. If you file as the appointed representative, include proof of that appointment (as listed on Medicare.gov (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov).

  1. Use the Appointment of Representative form. Or use another written paper that has the required parts (as listed on Medicare.gov (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov).
  2. This guide did not sign in to a parent's account. It did not check any family's permission.
  3. Keep this path for Original Medicare claims. A Medicare Advantage plan uses a different appeal (as listed on Medicare.gov (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov).

See our guide on a Medicare Advantage denial or ending covered care.

What should I write down while I check?

Write one line per service in your own notes. This list is our blank tool, not an official Medicare form (as listed on Medicare.gov (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov).

  1. Write the notice period, the provider, and the service date. Add the service description, and whether it was approved or denied (as listed on the sample Part B MSN (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov sample).
  2. Write what Medicare paid and the maximum that may be billed. Add what was already paid, and whether other insurance is still to check (as listed on Medicare.gov (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov).
  3. Write the Notes, the date you called the office, and the reply. Add the appeal due date printed on the notice, and the next step (as listed on the sample Part B MSN (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov sample).
  4. Do not copy sample dollar amounts as what your parent owes. Do not put the Medicare number on this website (as listed on the sample Part B MSN (checked November 2026), Medicare.gov sample).
