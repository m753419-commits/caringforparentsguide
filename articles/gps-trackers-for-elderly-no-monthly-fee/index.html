GPS Trackers for Elderly Parents With No Monthly Fee: What to Know Before You Buy

This guide is written for families in the United States; where it mentions Medicare or Medicaid, it means the US programs.

Is there a GPS tracker for elderly parents with truly no monthly fee?

Some devices have no subscription, but a device can only report its location when it has a network connection (Apple Support), and a tracker that reports from anywhere usually uses a cellular one, which is what a monthly fee typically pays for. "No monthly fee" usually means one of the four options in the table below.

The four kinds of "no monthly fee" options

Bluetooth item tracker (tags made for keys and bags) Nearby phones on the maker's network report the tag's approximate location (Apple) No subscription; the tag's battery needs replacing Finding a lost wallet, keys, or a bag your parent carries Needs phones nearby; designed for belongings, not people; anti-stalking alerts may notify your parent's phone

This table describes how these categories generally work. Check each product's specs and terms.

  1. Ask the seller how the location gets to your phone (Bluetooth through nearby phones, cellular, or your parent's own phone); the answer tells you which row of the table the device is in.
  2. For a device with "free" service included, get in writing how long the service lasts, what renewal costs, and whether the device stops sending location if you don't renew.
  3. For a device that takes your own SIM, confirm with the carrier that the device works on its network and that the plan may be used in that kind of device.
  4. If your parent already carries a smartphone, turn on its location sharing first and set a nightly charging spot before buying anything else.

Can I use an AirTag to track my mom or dad?

Not reliably: Apple says AirTag "was designed to help people locate their personal belongings, not to track people or another person's property," and that using AirTag to track people without consent is a crime in many regions (Apple Newsroom, February 10, 2022). A tag can still help you find a lost bag your parent carries, if they know it is there.

  1. Ask your parent first and put the tag only in a wallet, handbag, or key ring they agree to.
  2. Expect alerts on your parent's phone: if a tag separated from its owner moves with someone over time, their iPhone or Android phone may show an alert or the tag may play a sound (Apple Support).
  3. Check coverage where your parent goes: the tag's location comes from nearby Apple devices on the Find My network (Apple Support), so a quiet rural road may have none to report it.
  4. If you need to find a person quickly, use a cellular tracker or phone location sharing instead (see the table above).

What features should I compare before buying a tracker?

Compare how location is found, how often it updates, alerts, battery, how it is worn, SOS and two-way talk, water resistance, total cost over two years, and privacy, using the manufacturer's published specs rather than the listing headline.

Do GPS trackers work indoors?

Often not well: the Alzheimer's Association says no location tracking system is 100% accurate and some devices don't work well inside buildings (Alzheimer's Association). It also says tracking is not the same as having a 24-hour companion and should be used alongside other safety measures.

  1. Test the device inside your parent's home and the places they go most before you rely on it.
  2. Keep a recent close-up photo of your parent and a list of places they might go, such as former homes, past jobs, or a place of worship (Alzheimer's Association, "Wandering").
  3. Ask neighbors, friends, and family to call you if they see your parent lost or wandering (Alzheimer's Association).
  4. Walk through the home with our home safety checklist, and if family lives far away, agree on a plan with our long-distance caregiving checklist.
  5. If your parent with dementia is missing and not found within 15 minutes, call 911 and tell them your parent has dementia (Alzheimer's Association); for advice any time, call the 24/7 Helpline at 1-800-272-3900.

Does Medicare pay for GPS trackers?

Original Medicare's equipment list doesn't include location trackers, and it denies telephone alert systems because they are emergency communication systems with no diagnostic or therapeutic purpose (CMS NCD 280.1, version effective June 9, 2025). A Medicare Advantage plan may offer an in-home emergency response device as an extra benefit, but that benefit can't include cellular telephones (CMS Medicare Managed Care Manual, Ch. 4, checked November 2026).

  1. If your parent has a Medicare Advantage plan, call the number on the plan card and ask whether it offers a personal emergency response device.
  2. If your parent has Medicaid, ask the state Medicaid office or the Area Agency on Aging (through the Eldercare Locator) whether the state's Medicaid program covers a personal emergency response system.
  3. Compare options and costs with our guide to medical alert coverage.

Is it legal to track my parent?

It depends on your state's laws and on your parent's consent and capacity (whether they can understand and make the decision); Apple notes that using AirTag to track people without consent is a crime in many regions (Apple Newsroom, 2022).

  1. If your parent can take part, ask before you buy anything, and let them choose the device; see what to do if your parent refuses help, or our guide on driving if that is the worry.
  2. If your parent has dementia and can no longer weigh the decision, involve whoever holds their health care power of attorney (the person named in a legal document to make health decisions for them) and talk with their doctor.
  3. If no one holds that authority or family members disagree, talk with an elder law attorney in your parent's state.

This guide is general information. If your parent has dementia and has wandered or gotten lost, talk with their doctor. In an emergency, call 911.
