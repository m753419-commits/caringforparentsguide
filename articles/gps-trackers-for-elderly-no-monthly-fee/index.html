GPS Trackers for Elderly Parents With No Monthly Fee: What to Know Before You Buy

By Ellen Carter. Published November 2, 2026. Publisher: Family Caregiver Practical Guide.

This article contains affiliate links. As an Amazon Associate I earn from qualifying purchases. How affiliate links work on this site.

Key Takeaways

  • A tracker that can show your parent's location from anywhere needs a data connection, and that connection usually costs something. "No monthly fee" generally means one of four things: a Bluetooth item tracker, service bundled for a set period, a device that uses a SIM plan you pay for separately, or a location-sharing app on a phone your parent already has.
  • Bluetooth item trackers have no subscription, but they rely on nearby phones and are made for belongings. Apple says AirTag was "designed to help people locate their personal belongings, not to track people."
  • No tracker is 100% accurate, and some don't work well indoors (Alzheimer's Association). A tracker doesn't replace supervision.
  • Talk with your parent first, if they can take part. Consent and dignity matter.
  • Compare on how location is found, update speed, alerts, battery, how it's worn, and the full cost after any "free" period, using the manufacturer's specs.

This guide is general information. If your parent has dementia and has wandered or gotten lost, talk with their doctor and see the Alzheimer's Association resources below. In an emergency, call 911.

Maybe it was the afternoon your mom drove to a store she's been to a hundred times and called you, confused, from a parking lot across town. Or the evening your dad went for his usual walk and didn't come back for three hours. Afterward you search "GPS tracker for elderly," and the results are full of monthly plans. You just want something simple that you buy once.

That's a reasonable thing to want. But it helps to understand why most trackers charge monthly, and what you give up when they don't. Here's a plain-English guide.

Why most GPS trackers charge a monthly fee

A GPS chip can figure out where it is. It can't tell you where it is. To send its location to your phone, the device needs a connection, usually a cellular one, like a phone. Somebody pays for that connection. With most trackers, that's the monthly fee.

So when a product says "no monthly fee," it's worth asking: How does the location get to my phone? The answer tells you a lot about what it can and can't do.

A closer look at Bluetooth item trackers

These small tags are popular because you buy them once. They work by connecting to nearby phones from the same network, which anonymously report the tag's location. In a busy area, that can work well. On a quiet country road, there may be no phone nearby to report anything.

Two important points. First, Apple has said AirTag "was designed to help people locate their personal belongings, not to track people or another person's property," and that tracking people without consent is a crime in many places. Second, these tags are built with anti-stalking features: if a tag that isn't paired with a phone travels with someone, their phone may alert them. If your parent has a smartphone, they may get those alerts.

Used openly, with your parent's agreement, a tag in a wallet or handbag can help you find a lost bag. Just don't count on it to find a person quickly.

Two widely sold examples: Apple AirTag (2nd generation), which works with the Find My app on an iPhone or iPad, and Tile Mate by Life360, which works with the Tile app on iPhone or Android. Check the current specs and requirements on each product page before you buy.

Trackers with "free" service included

Some trackers include cellular service for a period. That can be a good way to see whether a tracker works for your family. Before you buy, find out:

  • How long is the included service, in writing?
  • What does renewal cost?
  • What happens if you don't renew: does the device stop sending location?

Trackers that use your own SIM

Some devices let you insert a SIM card from a carrier you choose. There's no fee to the tracker company, but you still pay for the data plan. Check that the device supports your carrier's network and that the carrier allows the plan to be used in that kind of device.

Location sharing on a phone your parent already has

If your parent carries a smartphone, its built-in location sharing (and some free apps) can show you where the phone is, with no extra device. The Alzheimer's Association notes that smartphone apps may send notifications when a person leaves a familiar place.

The catch is obvious: it only helps if the phone is with your parent, turned on, and charged. Many families combine this with a simple routine, like charging the phone every night in the same spot.

The features to compare

Whatever type you consider, compare the same things. Pull each answer from the manufacturer's published specs, not just the product listing headline.

  1. How location is found. GPS, cellular, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, or a mix. The Alzheimer's Association notes that some devices don't work well inside buildings, and some can only show a general area.
  2. How often it updates. Every few seconds, every few minutes, or only when someone requests it? Faster updates usually drain the battery faster.
  3. Alerts. Can you set a "safe zone" (geofence) and get an alert when your parent leaves it? How does the alert arrive: app, text, or call?
  4. Battery life and charging. How long does it last in real use, and who will charge it? A tracker that needs daily charging may not work for a parent who won't remember.
  5. How it's worn or carried. Watch, pendant, clip, shoe insert, keychain, or card. The Alzheimer's Association mentions watches and shoe inserts among wearable options. Think about whether your parent might take it off.
  6. SOS button and two-way talk. Some devices add these. If you mainly want help after a fall, a medical alert system may be a better fit.
  7. Water resistance. For walks in the rain or wearing it in the shower.
  8. Total cost over two years. Device + any activation + any subscription after a free period + SIM plan, if needed.
  9. Privacy. Who can see the location data? Can you delete it?

Talk with your parent before you track them

This part matters more than any feature. Being tracked can feel like a loss of independence, and many older adults push back hard. If your parent can take part in the decision, ask, don't announce. You might say: "I worry when I can't reach you. Would you be willing to try this, so I can stop calling you five times a day?"

Our guide on how to talk to your aging parent about needing help has more ways to open the conversation. If the worry is mainly about driving, our guide to talking with a parent about driving may help.

If your parent has dementia and can no longer weigh the decision, involve whoever holds their health care power of attorney, and talk with their doctor.

A tracker is one piece of a safety plan

The Alzheimer's Association is clear that location tracking is not the same as having a 24-hour companion, and that it should be used alongside other safety measures. A few that families combine with a tracker:

  • An updated recent photo of your parent and a list of places they might go.
  • Neighbors who know to call you.
  • Home changes that reduce risk. Our home safety checklist walks through each room.
  • A shared plan among family members, especially if you live far away. See our long-distance caregiving checklist.

The Alzheimer's Association runs a 24/7 Helpline at 800-272-3900 if you need to talk it through.

Frequently asked questions

Is there a GPS tracker for elderly parents with truly no monthly fee?

Some devices have no subscription, but a tracker that reports location from anywhere needs a data connection. No-fee options are usually Bluetooth item trackers (which depend on nearby phones), devices with service included for a limited time, devices that use a SIM plan you pay for separately, or location sharing on your parent's own phone.

Can I use an AirTag to track my mom or dad?

Apple says AirTag is designed to find belongings, not to track people, and its anti-stalking alerts may notify your parent's phone. Some families keep a tag in a parent's wallet or bag, with their knowledge, to find the bag if it's lost. It isn't a reliable way to find a person quickly.

Do GPS trackers work indoors?

Often not well. The Alzheimer's Association notes that no location tracking system is 100% accurate and some devices don't work well inside buildings.

Does Medicare pay for GPS trackers?

We haven't found any Original Medicare coverage for location trackers. Medicare's equipment list denies "telephone alert systems" (CMS NCD 280.1). Some Medicare Advantage plans or state Medicaid programs may help with emergency response devices; see our guide to medical alert coverage.

Is it legal to track my parent?

Laws vary by state and depend on consent and capacity. Apple notes that tracking people without consent is a crime in many places. If your parent can't consent, talk with an elder law attorney.
