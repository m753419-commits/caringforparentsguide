Guardianship vs. Power of Attorney: What Families Need to Know

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 3, 2026

US programs only; examples use Texas and New York law, and other states’ rules differ.

Please note: This post is general information, not legal, financial, or medical advice.

Rules and costs vary by state, so talk with an elder law attorney (a lawyer who focuses on older adults’ legal issues), a court help center, or a lawyer referral service in your state before you act (New York Courts; State Bar of Texas; full disclaimer).

What is the difference between a power of attorney and guardianship?

The key difference: A power of attorney (a document in which your parent names someone to act for them) is something your parent signs, while guardianship is a legal arrangement where a court gives a person the legal right to make decisions for another person who is unable to make decisions for themselves (Texas Estates Code ch. 751; New York Courts).

  1. Who’s who: the principal (the adult who signs the power of attorney) names an agent (the person chosen to act for them), which Texas law says includes an attorney in fact (another name for an agent) (TX Estates Code §751.002).
  2. Durable (still valid after your parent loses the ability to decide): in Texas, a durable power of attorney must say it “is not affected by subsequent disability or incapacity (no longer able to make decisions) of the principal” or that it “becomes effective on the disability or incapacity of the principal” (TX Estates Code §751.0021).
  3. Health care is separate: in Texas, an advance directive (a document about future medical care) can be a directive, an out-of-hospital DNR (do-not-resuscitate) order, or a medical power of attorney (TX Health & Safety Code §166.002); in New York, a competent adult may appoint a health care agent by a health care proxy (a form naming who makes medical decisions) (NY Public Health Law §2981). A living will (a document stating the medical care your parent does or doesn’t want) is another common form; see our documents checklist.
  4. Two kinds of guardian: New York courts say a guardian of the person can make life decisions such as health care, education and welfare decisions, and there is also a guardian of the property (who manages money and property); Texas uses guardian of the person and guardian of the estate (the Texas term for a guardian of the property) (New York Courts; TX Estates Code §1002.012).
  5. Agents owe duties: New York says an agent has a fiduciary relationship (a legal duty to act in the other person’s interest) with the principal, including acting in the principal’s best interest, keeping the principal’s property separate, and keeping records; Texas says an agent has a duty to inform and to account for actions taken (NY Gen. Oblig. Law §5-1505; TX Estates Code §751.101).

When should my parent sign a power of attorney?

While your parent can still decide: New York requires a power of attorney to be signed by a principal with capacity (the ability to understand what they are signing), and Texas requires an adult principal to sign it and have it acknowledged (signed before an official who confirms the signature) (NY Gen. Oblig. Law §5-1501B; TX Estates Code §751.0021).

  1. Texas: it must be acknowledged before an officer authorized to take acknowledgments to deeds of conveyance (confirm signatures on property deeds) and to administer oaths (have people swear to tell the truth) (TX Estates Code §751.0021).
  2. New York: the signature must be acknowledged and witnessed by two persons who are not named as agents or as recipients of gifts, and the person who takes the acknowledgment may serve as one witness (NY Gen. Oblig. Law §5-1501B); a health care proxy needs two adult witnesses (NY Public Health Law §2981).
  3. When it ends in Texas: a durable power of attorney terminates when the principal dies or revokes it (cancels it), among other events, or when a permanent guardian of the estate has qualified (TX Estates Code §751.131).
  4. When it ends in New York: it terminates when the principal dies, becomes incapacitated if it isn’t durable, revokes it, or a court order revokes it (NY Gen. Oblig. Law §5-1511).
  5. If your parent moves to another state, ask a lawyer in the new state whether the documents should be redone; New York’s court system can help you find the right lawyer (New York Courts).

Will Social Security, Medicare, and the IRS accept a power of attorney?

Each has its own form: Social Security (the federal agency that pays retirement and disability benefits) says having power of attorney is not the same as being a payee, so you must still apply to serve as your parent’s representative payee (a person or organization SSA appoints to receive benefits for someone) (SSA).

  1. Social Security: Social Security appoints a payee for anyone who can’t manage or direct the management of their Social Security or SSI (Supplemental Security Income) benefits, and the Treasury Department (the federal finance department) does not recognize power of attorney for negotiating federal payments (SSA; our documents checklist).
  2. Medicare: Medicare (the federal health insurance program for people 65 and older) uses Form CMS-10106 to give 1-800-MEDICARE (Medicare’s official phone line, 1-800-633-4227) permission to share your parent’s personal health information with someone else, and your parent can change or cancel it at any time (CMS-10106 (03/2026); our Medicare permission form guide).
  3. IRS: Form 2848 authorizes an individual who is eligible to practice before the IRS (the U.S. tax agency) to represent your parent, and Form 8821 lets someone inspect or receive your parent’s confidential tax information (IRS Form 2848; IRS Form 8821).

When is guardianship needed?

When less restrictive options won’t work: A Texas court must find by clear and convincing evidence (a high level of proof) that alternatives to guardianship and available supports and services have been considered and aren’t feasible, and a New York court must consider the sufficiency and reliability of available resources (TX Estates Code §1101.101; NY Mental Hygiene Law §81.02).

  1. Incapacitated person in Texas (the legal term for someone who can’t manage on their own): an adult who, because of a physical or mental condition, is substantially unable to provide food, clothing, or shelter, care for their own physical health, or manage their own financial affairs (TX Estates Code §1002.017).
  2. New York: a finding of incapacity must be based on clear and convincing evidence that the person is likely to suffer harm because they can’t provide for personal needs or property management and can’t adequately understand that inability, and the court gives primary consideration to the person’s functional level (NY Mental Hygiene Law §81.02).
  3. Texas needs a doctor’s letter: the court generally can’t create a guardianship without a written letter or certificate from a physician licensed in Texas (TX Estates Code §1101.103).
  4. Limited powers: Texas courts can appoint a guardian with limited powers if your parent can do some but not all tasks (TX Estates Code §1101.152), and New York uses the least restrictive form of intervention (giving the guardian only the powers needed, so your parent keeps as much independence as possible) (NY Mental Hygiene Law §81.03).

How does guardianship work, and what does it cost?

It goes through court: In New York, the petitioner (the person who asks the court to start the case) fills out the forms, files the petition (a written request asking the court to start a case), pays the filing fees, and serves the papers (formally delivers copies to the people involved), and in Texas any person may file a sworn written application (New York Courts; TX Estates Code §1101.001).

  1. New York hearing: it takes place no more than 28 days from the date the judge signs the order to show cause (a court order setting the hearing), and the court appoints a court evaluator (a person who looks into the case for the judge) (New York Courts).
  2. Temporary guardian (a short-term guardian for urgent situations): Texas courts appoint one when there is substantial evidence a person may be incapacitated and probable cause (good reason to believe) that immediate appointment is needed, and in contested cases the term can last until the nine-month anniversary unless extended (TX Estates Code §§1251.001, 1251.052); New York courts may appoint one upon a showing of danger in the reasonably foreseeable future (NY Mental Hygiene Law §81.23).
  3. Costs: fees vary by county, so ask the court clerk; a New York judge may order a bond, which is like an insurance policy protecting the incapacitated person against any errors made by the guardian (New York Courts).
  4. After appointment in New York: complete a guardianship training course within 90 days, file an initial report within 90 days of the commission (the court paper that lets the guardian start acting), and file an annual report by May 31 each year (New York Courts).
  5. After appointment in Texas: a guardian of the person files a report once each year, and a guardian of the estate files an account within 60 days after the first anniversary of qualifying unless the court extends it (TX Estates Code §§1163.001, 1163.101).

Are there alternatives to guardianship?

Often, yes: New York law lists available resources such as visiting nurses, home health aides, adult day care, powers of attorney, health care proxies, trusts, and representative and protective payees, and Texas has a Supported Decision-Making Agreement Act (NY Mental Hygiene Law §81.03; Texas Estates Code ch. 1357).

  1. Supported decision-making in Texas (your parent makes their own choices with help from people they trust): the law defines it as a process of supporting and accommodating an adult with a disability to enable the adult to make life decisions (TX Estates Code §1357.002).
  2. In New York or another state, ask a lawyer or the court whether a similar agreement is available; court Help Centers provide free information to people who do not have an attorney (New York Courts).
  3. Find local services: the Eldercare Locator connects you to services for older adults and their families at 1-800-677-1116 (Eldercare Locator).
  4. Plan with family: see our long-distance caregiving checklist.

What if an agent or guardian misuses their power?

Go to the court: A Texas ward (a person who has a guardian) or anyone interested in the ward’s welfare can ask the court to end or change the guardianship, and a New York court order can revoke a power of attorney (TX Estates Code §1202.051; NY Gen. Oblig. Law §5-1511).

  1. Right to a lawyer in New York: a person facing guardianship can choose their own lawyer, and the court must appoint one if the person requests counsel or wishes to contest the petition (NY Mental Hygiene Law §81.10).
  2. Ask for records: Texas agents must timely inform the principal of each action taken, and New York agents must keep a record of all receipts, disbursements, and transactions (TX Estates Code §751.102; NY Gen. Oblig. Law §5-1505).
  3. Find a lawyer: State Bar of Texas Lawyer Referral Service, (800) 252-9690 (State Bar of Texas); New York’s court system can help you find the right lawyer (New York Courts).
  4. If your parent is in immediate danger, call 911.
  5. Prevent family disputes: see how to avoid disputes over a parent’s money.
