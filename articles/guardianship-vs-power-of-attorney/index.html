Guardianship vs. Power of Attorney: What Families Need to Know

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 7, 2026

US programs only; examples use Texas and New York law, and other states’ rules differ.

Rules and costs vary by state. Before you act, talk with an elder law attorney. That’s a lawyer who focuses on older adults’ legal issues. A court help center or a lawyer referral service in your state can also help (New York Courts; State Bar of Texas; full disclaimer).

In plain words: This guide compares ways to make decisions for a parent. In one, your parent signs a paper that names someone to act for them. In the other, a court gives someone that right when your parent can’t decide for themselves. Rules differ by state, so ask a lawyer before you act.

What is the difference between a power of attorney and guardianship?

A power of attorney is a document your parent signs to name someone to act for them. Guardianship is when a court gives a person the legal right to decide for someone who can’t decide for themselves (Texas Estates Code ch. 751; New York Courts).

  1. Who’s who. The principal is the adult who signs the power of attorney. They name an agent (the person chosen to act for them). Texas law says an agent includes an attorney in fact. That is another name for an agent (TX Estates Code §751.002).
  2. Durable means still valid after your parent loses the ability to decide. In Texas, a durable power of attorney must use certain words. It must say it “is not affected by subsequent disability or incapacity of the principal”. Or it must say it “becomes effective on the disability or incapacity of the principal”. Incapacity means no longer able to make decisions (TX Estates Code §751.0021).
  3. Health care is separate. In Texas, an advance directive can be one of these. That is a document about future medical care. It can be a directive, an out-of-hospital DNR (do-not-resuscitate) order, or a medical power of attorney (TX Health & Safety Code §166.002). In New York, a competent adult may appoint a health care agent. Competent means able to decide. This is done by a health care proxy. That is a form naming who makes medical decisions (NY Public Health Law §2981). A living will is another common form. It states the medical care your parent does or doesn’t want. See our documents checklist.
  4. Two kinds of guardian. In New York, there is a guardian of the person. New York courts say this guardian can make life decisions such as health care, education and welfare decisions. There is also a guardian of the property (who manages money and property). Texas uses guardian of the person and guardian of the estate. That is the Texas term for a guardian of the property (New York Courts; TX Estates Code §1002.012).
  5. Agents owe duties. New York says an agent has a fiduciary relationship with the principal. That is a legal duty to act in the other person’s interest. It includes acting in the principal's best interest. It includes keeping the principal's property separate. It also includes keeping records. Texas says an agent has a duty to inform. They also have a duty to account for (report on) actions taken (NY Gen. Oblig. Law §5-1505; TX Estates Code §751.101).

When should my parent sign a power of attorney?

While your parent can still decide. New York requires a power of attorney signed by a principal with capacity, and Texas requires an adult principal to sign it and have it acknowledged (NY Gen. Oblig. Law §5-1501B; TX Estates Code §751.0021). Capacity means the ability to understand what they are signing. Acknowledged means signed before an official who confirms the signature.

  1. Texas. It must be acknowledged before an officer authorized to take acknowledgments to deeds of conveyance. That is an officer who can confirm signatures on property deeds. The officer must also be authorized to administer oaths. That means having people swear to tell the truth (TX Estates Code §751.0021).
  2. New York. The signature must be acknowledged and witnessed by two persons. They must not be named as agents or as recipients of gifts. The person who takes the acknowledgment may serve as one witness (NY Gen. Oblig. Law §5-1501B). A health care proxy needs two adult witnesses (NY Public Health Law §2981).
  3. Ending in Texas. A durable power of attorney ends when the principal dies or revokes it (cancels it). Other events can end it too. It also ends when a permanent guardian of the estate has qualified (TX Estates Code §751.131).
  4. Ending in New York. It ends when the principal dies or revokes it. It also ends if the principal becomes incapacitated and it isn’t durable. A court order can also revoke it (NY Gen. Oblig. Law §5-1511).
  5. If your parent moves to another state, ask a lawyer there. Ask if the documents should be redone. New York’s court system can help you find the right lawyer (New York Courts).

Will Social Security, Medicare, and the IRS accept a power of attorney?

Each has its own form. Social Security (the federal agency that pays retirement and disability benefits) says having power of attorney isn’t the same as being a payee (SSA). A payee is a person or organization SSA appoints to receive benefits for someone.

  1. Social Security. You must still apply to serve as your parent’s representative payee. SSA appoints a payee for anyone who can’t manage their Social Security or SSI benefits. That includes anyone who can’t direct the management of them. SSI stands for Supplemental Security Income. The Treasury Department is the federal finance department. It does not recognize power of attorney for negotiating (cashing) federal payments (SSA; our documents checklist).
  2. Medicare. Medicare (the federal health insurance program for people 65 and older and some younger people with disabilities) uses Form CMS-10106. 1-800-MEDICARE is Medicare’s official phone line (1-800-633-4227). The form lets it share your parent’s personal health information with someone else. Your parent can change or cancel it at any time (CMS-10106 (03/2026); our Medicare permission form guide).
  3. IRS. The IRS is the U.S. tax agency. Form 2848 is for an individual who is eligible to practice before the IRS. It authorizes that person to represent your parent. Form 8821 lets someone inspect or receive your parent’s confidential tax information (IRS Form 2848; IRS Form 8821).

When is guardianship needed?

A Texas court must find by clear and convincing evidence that alternatives to guardianship and available supports and services have been considered and aren’t feasible (TX Estates Code §1101.101). A New York court must consider the sufficiency and reliability of available resources (NY Mental Hygiene Law §81.02). Clear and convincing evidence means a high level of proof. Feasible means workable.

  1. Texas. Incapacitated person is a legal term. It means someone who can’t manage on their own. It means an adult with a physical or mental condition. Because of it, they are substantially unable to do certain things. These are providing food, clothing, or shelter. They also include caring for their own physical health, or managing their own financial affairs (TX Estates Code §1002.017).
  2. New York. A New York court must consider the sufficiency and reliability of available resources. That means whether other help is enough and dependable. A finding of incapacity must be based on clear and convincing evidence. The evidence must show the person is likely to suffer harm because they can’t provide for personal needs or property management. The evidence must also show that they cannot adequately understand that inability. The court gives primary consideration to the person’s functional level. That means how well they can do daily tasks (NY Mental Hygiene Law §81.02).
  3. Doctor’s letter in Texas. The court generally can’t create a guardianship without a written letter or certificate. It must come from a physician licensed in Texas (TX Estates Code §1101.103).
  4. Limited powers. Texas courts can appoint a guardian with limited powers. This applies if your parent can do some but not all tasks (TX Estates Code §1101.152). New York uses the least restrictive form of intervention. That means giving the guardian only the powers needed. Your parent then keeps as much independence as possible (NY Mental Hygiene Law §81.03).

How does guardianship work, and what does it cost?

It goes through court. In Texas, any person may file a sworn written application (TX Estates Code §1101.001).

  1. New York steps. The petitioner fills out the forms. That is the person who asks the court to start the case. They file the petition, a written request asking the court to start a case. They pay the filing fees. They also serve the papers (formally deliver copies to the people involved) (New York Courts). The judge signs an order to show cause. That is a court order setting the hearing. The hearing takes place no more than 28 days from that date. The court appoints a court evaluator. That is a person who looks into the case for the judge (New York Courts).
  2. Temporary guardian. This is a short-term guardian for urgent situations. Texas courts appoint one in certain cases. There must be substantial evidence a person may be incapacitated. There must also be probable cause (good reason to believe) that immediate appointment is needed. In contested cases (cases someone disputes), the term can last until the nine-month anniversary unless extended (TX Estates Code §§1251.001, 1251.052). New York courts may appoint one upon a showing of danger in the reasonably foreseeable future (NY Mental Hygiene Law §81.23).
  3. Costs. Fees vary by county. Ask the court clerk. A New York judge may order a bond. A bond is like an insurance policy protecting the incapacitated person against any errors made by the guardian (New York Courts).
  4. After appointment in New York. Complete a guardianship training course within 90 days. File an initial report within 90 days of the commission. That is the court paper that lets the guardian start acting. File an annual report by May 31 each year (New York Courts).
  5. After appointment in Texas. A guardian of the person files a report once each year. A guardian of the estate files an account. It is due within 60 days after the first anniversary of qualifying. The court can extend this (TX Estates Code §§1163.001, 1163.101).

Are there alternatives to guardianship?

Often, yes, and New York law lists available resources. Texas has a Supported Decision-Making Agreement Act (NY Mental Hygiene Law §81.03; Texas Estates Code ch. 1357).

  1. New York’s list. It includes visiting nurses and home health aides. Aides are workers who help with daily care at home. Adult day care is on it too (daytime care and activities outside the home). So are powers of attorney, health care proxies, and trusts. Trusts are legal arrangements to hold and manage money or property. Representative and protective payees are also listed (NY Mental Hygiene Law §81.03).
  2. Supported decision-making in Texas means your parent makes their own choices. People they trust help them. The law defines it as a process of supporting and accommodating an adult with a disability. This is to enable the adult to make life decisions (TX Estates Code §1357.002).
  3. In New York or another state, ask a lawyer or the court. Ask whether a similar agreement is available. Court Help Centers provide free information to people who do not have an attorney (New York Courts).
  4. Find local services. The Eldercare Locator connects you to services for older adults and their families. Call 1-800-677-1116 (Eldercare Locator).
  5. Plan with family. See our long-distance caregiving checklist.

What if an agent or guardian misuses their power?

A Texas ward (a person with a guardian) or anyone interested in the ward's welfare can ask the court to end or change the guardianship (TX Estates Code §1202.051). A New York court order can revoke (cancel) a power of attorney (NY Gen. Oblig. Law §5-1511).

  1. In New York. A person facing guardianship can choose their own lawyer. The court must appoint one if the person requests counsel or wishes to contest the petition (NY Mental Hygiene Law §81.10).
  2. Ask for records. Texas agents must timely inform the principal of each action taken. New York agents must keep a record of all receipts, disbursements (payments made), and transactions (TX Estates Code §751.102; NY Gen. Oblig. Law §5-1505).
  3. Find a lawyer. Call the State Bar of Texas Lawyer Referral Service at (800) 252-9690 (State Bar of Texas). New York’s court system can help you find the right lawyer (New York Courts).
  4. If your parent is in immediate danger, call 911.
  5. Prevent family disputes. See how to avoid disputes over a parent’s money.
