Caring for a Parent While Working: FMLA Basics and Practical Options

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 3, 2026

US programs only.

Can I use FMLA to care for my mother or father?

Yes, if you and your employer are covered: the Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) gives eligible employees up to 12 workweeks of unpaid, job-protected leave in a 12-month period, with group health benefits kept on the same terms (U.S. Department of Labor, page last updated January 26, 2026; checked November 2026). Caring for a parent with a serious health condition (generally one involving a period of incapacity, meaning being unable to work or do regular daily activities) is a qualifying reason (DOL Fact Sheet #28F, March 2023).

  1. Check that your employer is covered: a private employer with 50 or more employees in 20 or more workweeks in the current or previous calendar year; or a public agency (federal, state, or local government) of any size; or a public or private elementary or secondary school of any size (DOL Fact Sheet #28E, March 2025; checked November 2026).
  2. Check that you are eligible: you have worked for the employer for at least 12 months, have at least 1,250 hours of service in the 12 months before leave starts, and work at a location where the employer has at least 50 employees within 75 miles (DOL Fact Sheet #28E, March 2025).
  3. Check who counts as a parent: a biological, adoptive, step, or foster parent, or someone who stood in loco parentis (in the role of a parent) to you when you were a child. Parents-in-law are not included.
  4. Know what "caring for" covers. DOL says it includes help with basic medical, hygienic, nutritional, safety, or transportation needs, physical care, and psychological comfort, for example driving a parent to treatment (DOL Fact Sheet #28F). When you return, you must be restored to the same or a virtually identical position.
  5. If you don't qualify, ask HR about company policy and check your state labor department; DOL says state law may give additional or greater protections. Questions: DOL Wage and Hour Division, 1-866-487-9243.

Is FMLA leave paid?

No. FMLA leave is unpaid under federal law, but it can be used at the same time as employer-provided paid leave (DOL).

  1. Ask HR which paid leave you can use during FMLA leave, such as vacation, sick, or personal days.
  2. Check for a state paid leave program. As of November 2026, DOL's Women's Bureau says 13 states and the District of Columbia have laws creating paid family and medical leave programs, and it keeps a map with links to state agencies (DOL, Paid Leave).
  3. Ask about an employee assistance program (EAP), if your employer offers one, for counseling or referrals to elder care services.

Can I use FMLA to care for my mother-in-law?

Not under the federal FMLA: DOL's definition of "parent" does not include parents-in-law, unless she stood in the role of a parent to you when you were a child (DOL).

  1. Check your state's family leave law, through your state labor department; it may cover more relatives.
  2. Ask HR about company leave policies that go beyond the FMLA.
  3. If your spouse is eligible, your spouse may be able to take FMLA leave to care for their own parent, since DOL's definition covers an employee's own parent (DOL).

Can I take FMLA leave a few hours at a time?

Yes, when medically necessary. Eligible employees may take FMLA leave intermittently (in separate blocks of time for one qualifying reason) or on a reduced schedule (fewer hours a day or week) (DOL).

  1. List the regular needs, such as treatment appointments, and how often they happen.
  2. Ask HR for intermittent or reduced-schedule leave for those times.
  3. Expect a medical certification request. Your employer may ask for certification from your parent's health care provider supporting the need for leave.

How do I ask my employer for leave, and do I have to share my parent's diagnosis?

Not necessarily: your notice only needs enough information for your employer to see the leave may be FMLA leave, such as that your parent was hospitalized overnight (DOL Fact Sheet #28E, March 2025; 29 CFR 825.303(b)). On a certification (a form your parent's health care provider fills out), the provider may, but doesn't have to, list the diagnosis, and your employer must keep it confidential (DOL Form WH-380-F; 29 CFR 825.500(g)).

  1. Start with HR if you can, and ask them to walk you through family leave options and the FMLA paperwork. Give at least 30 days' notice when you know about the need ahead of time, or as soon as possible and practical (DOL Fact Sheet #28E).
  2. Come with a specific plan, for example: "My mother has appointments every other Thursday. I'd like to request intermittent leave for those afternoons."
  3. Offer solutions for your work: who covers what, which deadlines move, and how people can reach you.
  4. Get it in writing. Keep copies of forms, emails, and approvals.
  5. Ask HR what information they need and who will see it. An employer may not require information beyond what the FMLA certification rules specify (29 CFR 825.306(b)).
Things you might ask for
Option What it might look like
Intermittent FMLA leave Half-days for a parent's appointments
Reduced schedule Working fewer hours per week for a period
Flexible hours Starting early to leave for afternoon appointments
Remote work Working from your parent's home during a hard stretch
Paid time off Using vacation or personal days first
Employee assistance program (EAP) Free counseling or referrals to elder care services, if your employer offers one

What if my FMLA leave runs out but my parent still needs care?

The federal FMLA gives up to 12 workweeks in a 12-month period, so plan the next step before the weeks are used up (DOL, page last updated January 26, 2026; checked November 2026).

  1. Talk to HR about every option, including unpaid leave, a reduced schedule, flexible hours, and state leave programs.
  2. Add paid help or respite so you aren't the only caregiver; call the local Area Agency on Aging through the Eldercare Locator (eldercare.acl.gov, 1-800-677-1116) to ask about respite. Our respite care guide lists other places to look.
  3. Compare the cost of paid help with the income you'd give up by cutting back; what it costs to care for an aging parent has national prices and a budget worksheet.
  4. Before cutting hours or leaving work, look at the long-term costs, including lost income, retirement savings, and benefits; a financial professional can help.
