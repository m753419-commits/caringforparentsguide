Medigap Plan G vs. Plan N for a Parent: Questions to Compare

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 9, 2026

US programs only.

Last checked: November 2026

This is general information, not advice for one person's plan. Check Medicare.gov or call 1-800-MEDICARE before you act. 1-800-MEDICARE is Medicare's official phone line, 1-800-633-4227.

You can also ask Social Security (the federal agency that pays retirement and disability benefits). Or ask SHIP (the State Health Insurance Assistance Program) for free, fair Medicare help (full disclaimer).

You're helping your parent choose between Medigap Plan G and Plan N, and you want to know what really separates them. On Medicare's chart, the two standard plans line up on most listed benefits. The differences that matter are the excess charge, which standard Plan G pays and Plan N doesn't, and the visit charges Plan N has in some cases. Neither one pays the Part B deductible.

In plain words: Medigap plans are private policies that help pay some costs in Original Medicare, also called regular Medicare. Medicare is U.S. government health insurance for older people and some younger people with disabilities. Standard Plan G and Plan N don't pay the Part B deductible, the amount paid each year before coverage starts. High-deductible Plan G is a different policy.

These plans help pay costs under two parts of Medicare. Part A is hospital insurance; it helps pay for hospital stays, skilled nursing facility care, hospice and some home health care. Part B is medical insurance; it helps pay for doctor visits, outpatient care, medical equipment and many preventive services.

When you read the chart, a covered mark means that one listed benefit. It doesn't mean every medical bill or living cost is paid.

What are standard Plan G and Plan N?

They're Medigap plan names. There's no part of Medicare called G or N. Each policy covers one person, and your parent generally needs both Part A and Part B. Medicare.gov's page on how Medigap works covers these basics.

Medigap can't be used to pay costs in a Medicare Advantage plan, which is a Medicare-approved plan from a private company. That's a different path.

A few rules trip people up:

  • Massachusetts, Minnesota, and Wisconsin standardize Medigap in a different way. Don't use the national chart as those states' chart.
  • People new to Medicare on or after January 1, 2020, can't buy Plan C or Plan F. That date limit doesn't mean Plan G or Plan N is only for people eligible after 2020.
  • Under 65, federal law generally doesn't require a company to sell Medigap, though companies do offer it in some states. At 65, check the right that starts then.

A few terms in that table: coinsurance is your percentage share of a covered cost. A copayment is a set dollar amount for a service. An excess charge is the difference when a provider may legally charge more than the Medicare-approved amount. This guide doesn't state a nationwide percent cap on it.

Because of that charge, ask whether the doctor accepts assignment, meaning the provider agrees to the Medicare-approved amount. A doctor who participates with Medicare accepts assignment for Medicare patients.

Plan N's visit charges are specific. Your parent pays up to $20 for some office visits, and up to $50 for an emergency room visit if they aren't admitted as an inpatient. Those amounts don't apply to every visit.

Other limits worth knowing:

  • Skilled nursing facility coinsurance isn't payment for all long-term living care. The guide on the cost of care covers those costs.
  • The Part B deductible amount is on the current-year costs page. Official pages don't all show the same 2026 figure, so this guide doesn't print one.
  • This guide didn't list every time limit or lifetime limit for foreign travel emergency. Read the policy.
  • The chart doesn't show a yearly out-of-pocket limit, a cap on what your parent pays themselves, for these two plans. Don't assume one exists, as it does with some other Medicare plans.

How is high-deductible Plan G different?

It's a separate policy, not standard Plan G with a twist. In some states, your parent pays Medicare-covered costs up to $2,950 in 2026 before that policy pays. Standard Plan G doesn't use that threshold. The figure comes from Medicare.gov's Medigap comparison chart.

Those costs are the coinsurance, copayments, and deductibles that Medicare already covers. So the $2,950 figure isn't:

  • a monthly premium, the amount paid, usually each month;
  • a cap on all care;
  • the separate deductible for foreign-travel emergency that the March 2026 Medigap guide describes. Read the guide for that benefit.

And standard Plan G still doesn't pay the Part B deductible, so don't describe it as a plan with nothing to pay.

Ask whether high-deductible Plan G is sold where your parent lives. This guide has no company prices and names no company. Don't guess a savings amount. Ask the company to put the price in writing.

What should you ask before comparing price?

Price only means something when the letter and the type match: standard or high-deductible. Get each premium in writing. This guide has no prices and doesn't recommend a policy. The March 2026 Choosing a Medigap Policy guide explains what moves the price.

Common mistake: Setting a high-deductible Plan G quote next to a standard Plan N quote, or borrowing a friend's price. Match the letter and the type, and check the state and the start date.

Before you call, write the plan type at the top of a page and keep a paper copy. Then go one question at a time:

  1. Your parent's facts. Note the state and age, the Part A and Part B start dates, when Medicare eligibility first began, and whether your parent has Original Medicare or a Medicare Advantage plan.
  2. Is it Medicare SELECT? That's a Medigap policy that uses a network of hospitals and may limit doctors. Weigh that limit along with the premium.
  3. Will the company use medical underwriting? That's a review for a yes or a price. Where your parent lives, discounts, and that review can all change the price.
  4. What about drugs? Medigap policies sold after 2005 don't include prescription drugs. Your parent may need a separate Medicare drug plan, Medicare Part D: private insurance, approved by Medicare, that helps pay for prescription drugs.

If your parent already has a Medigap policy, leave it in place for now. Don't cancel it first. Read the guide on a missed Medigap enrollment before dropping anything.

Does fall enrollment let your parent buy either plan?

No. Fall Medicare open enrollment, the yearly time to change Medicare plans, doesn't guarantee your parent can buy Medigap. It doesn't guarantee an exemption from medical underwriting, either. Medicare.gov's Get ready to buy page covers the timing.

The one-time Medigap period is 6 months, and it isn't the fall window. The missed-enrollment guide lists what to check next.

A right to buy doesn't mean every letter is available, Plan N included. Official pages don't all name the same policies, so use the current page for your parent's case.

One last thing that often gets mixed in: an extra Medicare charge based on income is separate from this premium. The guide on asking Social Security to review that charge explains it.
