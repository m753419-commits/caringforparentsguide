Does Medicare Cover Medical Alert Systems?

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 3, 2026

US programs only.

Questions in this guide

  1. Does Medicare cover life alert systems? If not, why?
  2. Do Medicare Advantage plans cover medical alert systems?
  3. Where can people on fixed incomes get help paying for a medical alert system?
  4. Is a medical alert system tax-deductible?
  5. Can a smartwatch replace a medical alert system?

Does Medicare cover life alert systems? If not, why?

No. Original Medicare doesn't cover them because CMS's national equipment list says: "Deny - these are emergency communications systems and do not serve a diagnostic or therapeutic purpose" (CMS NCD 280.1).

Part B covers durable medical equipment (DME: equipment such as walkers and hospital beds that is primarily for a medical purpose). A medical alert button isn't counted as DME, so a doctor's prescription doesn't change the answer.

  1. Don't count on Medigap. Medicare Supplement Insurance (Medigap) helps pay your parent's share of costs in Original Medicare (Medicare.gov); since Original Medicare doesn't cover alert systems, there's generally no share for it to pay.
  2. If your parent has Medicare Advantage, check the plan (next question).
  3. If your parent has Medicaid or limited income, check state programs (third question).
  4. If no program pays, compare systems on: in-home vs. mobile (cellular/GPS) use; who answers (a 24/7 monitoring center or family only); fall detection and its fee; cellular coverage where your parent lives; water resistance for the shower; battery life; total yearly cost (monthly fee × 12, plus equipment, activation, add-ons, and lost-device fees); and contract and cancellation terms, in writing. Brands you'll often see advertised include Medical Guardian, Bay Alarm Medical, Lively, and MobileHelp. Listing a brand isn't an endorsement. Once a device is set up, press the button together once so your parent knows what happens.

Do Medicare Advantage plans cover medical alert systems?

Some do. CMS lets Medicare Advantage plans offer in-home Personal Emergency Response devices as a supplemental benefit if they are primarily health related; a PERS may not include cell phones (CMS Medicare Managed Care Manual, Ch. 4).

  1. Search the plan's Evidence of Coverage or Summary of Benefits (the plan's yearly coverage documents) for "personal emergency response system," "PERS," or "medical alert."
  2. Or call member services using the number on your parent's plan card. Ask which device, what it costs, and who qualifies.
  3. Check again each year; benefits can change.
  4. Compare plans during Open Enrollment (October 15–December 7) with Medicare Plan Finder.

Where can people on fixed incomes get help paying for a medical alert system?

Start with Medicaid: some states' home- and community-based services (HCBS) waivers cover a PERS, sometimes including installation and the monthly fee (Medicaid.gov fact sheets for Alabama and Michigan waivers). An HCBS waiver is a Medicaid program that pays for services that help people stay at home (Medicaid.gov, 1915(c)).

  1. Call your parent's state Medicaid office or case manager and ask whether their waiver covers a personal emergency response system. For example, Medicaid.gov lists "personal emergency response systems-installation/monthly fee" in an Alabama waiver and a personal emergency response system in Michigan's MI Choice waiver.
  2. Expect eligibility rules. Waivers usually require Medicaid eligibility and a certain level of care need.
  3. Ask your local Area Agency on Aging about local help, through the Eldercare Locator (eldercare.acl.gov or 1-800-677-1116).
  4. If your parent is a veteran enrolled in VA health care, ask their VA care team whether a device or service is available. VA's Veteran-Directed Care gives eligible Veterans a budget for home and community based services; services vary by location.
  5. Long-term care insurance. Read the policy or call the insurer.

Is a medical alert system tax-deductible?

Possibly, but IRS Publication 502 (2025) doesn't name medical alert systems. A cost can count only if it fits the IRS definition of a medical expense, and only if you itemize deductions (list deductions on Schedule A instead of taking the standard deduction) (IRS Topic 501; IRS Publication 502 (2025)).

  1. Check the definition. The IRS says medical expenses are "the costs of diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention of disease and for the purpose of affecting any part or function of the body."
  2. Check the threshold. Medical expenses are deducted on Schedule A (Form 1040), and only the part above 7.5% of adjusted gross income (AGI: income after certain adjustments, shown on the tax return) counts (2025 returns).
  3. If you pay for your parent, the IRS says you can include medical expenses you paid for your dependent; Publication 502 explains who qualifies.
  4. Keep receipts and ask a tax professional before claiming it.

Can a smartwatch replace a medical alert system?

It depends on the watch and on your parent. Check the maker's documentation for what the watch can do, and don't expect Medicare to pay for it: Original Medicare denies alert systems (CMS NCD 280.1), and a Medicare Advantage PERS benefit can't include cellular telephones (CMS Medicare Managed Care Manual, Ch. 4).

  1. Check the watch's features in the maker's documentation: fall detection, emergency calling, and whether it needs its own cellular plan.
  2. Be honest about daily use: will your parent charge it every day and wear it in the shower and at night?
  3. Decide who should answer a call for help, and check in the maker's documentation (for the watch) and in the provider's written terms (for a medical alert service) who actually receives the call and when.
  4. Don't expect plan coverage for it. Original Medicare denies alert systems, and a Medicare Advantage PERS benefit can't include a cell phone.
