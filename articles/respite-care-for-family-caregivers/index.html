Respite Care for Family Caregivers: How to Take a Real Break

Quick answer

  • What it is: short-term care for your parent so you can rest. It can last from a few hours to several weeks. It can be at home, at an adult day center, or in a facility. An adult day center is a center where your parent spends the day with activities and supervision. The National Institute on Aging (NIA, a federal research agency) describes this care (National Institute on Aging, NIA).
  • Who to call first: the Eldercare Locator. It is a free federal helpline at 1-800-677-1116 or eldercare.acl.gov. Use it to reach your parent's local Area Agency on Aging. That is the local public office for older-adult services. For a veteran, call the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) Caregiver Support Line, 1-855-260-3274. VA is the federal agency for veterans' benefits (VA Caregiver Support Program).
  • Who pays. Medicare (U.S. government health insurance for older people and some younger people with disabilities) usually pays only during hospice (up to 5 days at a time) or, for some people with dementia, through the CMS GUIDE model (CMS, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, is the U.S. agency that runs Medicare) that began July 1, 2024 (up to $2,500 a year per patient). Medicaid is a joint federal and state program that helps with health costs for people with limited income and resources. Outside those Medicare payments, Medicaid, your local caregiver program, the VA, or long-term care insurance may help.

In plain words: Respite care is a short break while someone else stays with your parent. You can look for it near your parent. Medicare, Medicaid, or VA may help in some cases. First, call the local office for older adults.

How do I find and apply for respite care near my parent?

Call the Eldercare Locator at 1-800-677-1116 or use eldercare.acl.gov. Ask for your parent's Area Agency on Aging (ARCH, Access to Respite Care and Help, a national respite resource center). That local office most often runs respite through the family caregiver support program (ARCH). ARCH National Respite Network (a national group that helps families find respite care, which means short breaks for caregivers) lists each state's Lifespan Respite Program or State Respite Coalition (ARCH). Those are state-level respite groups that can tell you about providers and ways to pay.

  1. Ask the Area Agency on Aging how to apply for respite through its family caregiver program. That program serves family caregivers of people 60 or older, among others (ARCH).
  2. For a veteran, call the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) Caregiver Support Line at 1-855-260-3274 (VA Caregiver Support Program; ARCH).
  3. Search the ARCH National Respite Locator for home care agencies and other providers. Also search for assisted living. Assisted living is housing with help with daily activities. ARCH says it is not a complete list. Adult day centers are often covered by Medicaid or charge on a sliding fee scale. A sliding fee scale means prices based on income (ARCH).
  4. Find emergency respite before a crisis, as ARCH advises. Emergency respite is care on short notice. Use the same provider for planned breaks so your parent gets to know them (ARCH).

Questions to ask a respite provider:

  • ☐ What services do you provide, and what can't you do?
  • ☐ How are your staff or volunteers screened and trained?
  • ☐ Do you have experience with memory loss or my parent's needs?
  • ☐ What's the minimum visit or stay, and how much notice do you need? Can you help in an emergency?
  • ☐ What does it cost, and what payment do you accept?

One-page care sheet to leave with the provider: emergency contacts, including you and a backup. Your parent's routine (meals, naps, favorite activities). Things that comfort them and things that upset them. Any instructions from their doctor or pharmacist, kept exactly as given. And where to find keys, the information folder, and the doctor's number.

Does Medicare cover respite care?

Mainly through hospice, comfort care for someone who is terminally ill. Medicare covers short-term inpatient respite that the hospice team arranges, for up to 5 days at a time (Medicare Hospice Benefits, March 2026). For Medicare, people 65 or older must meet citizenship or residency requirements (Medicare.gov). Medicare also covers some younger people with a disability. Medicare also covers some younger people with End-Stage Renal Disease (permanent kidney failure). Medicare also covers some younger people with ALS (also called Lou Gehrig's disease). Original Medicare is regular Medicare. A Medicare Advantage plan is a Medicare-approved plan from a private company. Short-term inpatient respite for up to 5 days at a time can be used more than once (Medicare Hospice Benefits, March 2026; Medicare.gov hospice page; NIA). NIA says Medicare pays most of that cost in a hospital or a skilled nursing facility. A skilled nursing facility is a nursing home with nurses and therapists on staff (Medicare Hospice Benefits, March 2026; Medicare.gov hospice page; NIA). Outside hospice, Medicare doesn't pay for long-term care, including adult day health care (Medicare.gov).

  1. Check hospice eligibility. Your parent needs Part A (Medicare's hospital insurance; it helps pay for hospital stays, skilled nursing facility care, hospice and some home health care). The hospice doctor must certify a life expectancy of 6 months or less. The regular doctor must too, if there is one. Your parent must choose comfort care instead of treatment to cure the illness (Medicare.gov).
  2. Ask the hospice team to arrange respite. Your parent may pay 5% of the Medicare-approved amount (the payment Medicare sets for a service) for inpatient respite care (Medicare.gov).
  3. If your parent has dementia, Medicare's GUIDE Model may help. GUIDE means Guiding an Improved Dementia Experience. It started July 1, 2024. It pays up to $2,500 a year for respite for some people with dementia whose care team takes part (CMS, checked November 2026).
  4. If your parent isn't in hospice, see who else pays below.

Is respite care covered by insurance, and who else pays?

Most private health insurance doesn't cover respite. Some long-term care insurance may (NIA). Long-term care insurance is private insurance that helps pay for daily care. You pay what insurance or government programs don't (NIA). Medicaid, the Area Agency on Aging's family caregiver program, state respite vouchers, and VA may help pay (ARCH).

  1. Ask the state Medicaid office about home and community-based services waivers. An HCBS waiver is a Medicaid program that pays for care at home or in the community instead of a nursing home. ARCH says most include planned and emergency respite. Rules differ by state (ARCH). Find your state's Medicaid office through Medicaid.gov (NIA). See how Medicaid pays for care. See whether Medicaid can pay a family caregiver.
  2. Ask the Area Agency on Aging whether its family caregiver program has respite funds, including for emergencies. Ask your state respite program about vouchers. A voucher is money toward the cost. ARCH says state programs increasingly offer vouchers for emergencies (ARCH).
  3. For a veteran, ask VA. Veterans enrolled in VA health care can get VA Respite Care where it's available. Their health needs must meet VA's clinical criteria. A copay may apply. A copay is a set fee you pay (VA). Caregivers in VA's Program of Comprehensive Assistance for Family Caregivers get at least 30 days of respite a year. That program supports eligible family caregivers of veterans and pays a monthly stipend (VA).
  4. Read any long-term care insurance policy your parent has, and ask the insurer whether it covers respite.

What is respite care, and what types are there?

Respite care is short-term care for your parent. It gives you, the main caregiver, time to rest, travel, or see family and friends. It can last from a few hours to several weeks. It can be at home, in a health care facility, or at an adult day center (NIA).

  1. Decide what break you need: regular hours, a few days away, or emergency backup.
  2. Ask family, friends, or volunteers first; NIA says their help may have no cost (NIA).
  3. Compare prices for professional respite. NIA says it is charged by the hour or by the number of days or weeks (NIA). As a rough guide, 2025 national medians were $35 an hour for a non-medical in-home caregiver. Adult day health care was $95 a day (CareScout/Genworth, private survey, 2025; see our cost guide).
Types of respite care
Type What it looks like Good for
In-home respite A paid aide, trained volunteer, or friend comes to your parent's home Parents most comfortable at home
Adult day programs Part of the day at a center with activities and supervision Social parents; caregivers who work
Short-term residential stays A few days or weeks in assisted living or a nursing home with space Vacations, surgery, or longer breaks
Informal respite Family, friends, neighbors, or a faith group take a regular shift Families with willing helpers
Emergency respite Short-notice care, from one hour to several weeks (ARCH) Illness, a family emergency, or unavoidable travel

How often should a caregiver take respite?

No federal rule sets how often. The ARCH National Respite Network suggests respite at least once a week, when possible (ARCH, 9 Steps to Respite Care; NIA). NIA suggests arranging regular respite (NIA). In a 2018 evaluation, caregivers who got 4 or more hours of respite a week reported less burden over time. The evaluation was by the Administration for Community Living. That is the federal agency for older adults and people with disabilities (ARCH summary of the evaluation).

  1. Put a weekly break on the calendar. A volunteer, a relative, an in-home aide, or an adult day program can cover it (ARCH, 9 Steps to Respite Care). If you work, see caring for a parent while working.
  2. Book longer breaks ahead of time, since respite can last up to several weeks (NIA).
  3. Ask family and friends for specific shifts, starting with small requests. NIA says a text or email works if asking face-to-face is hard (NIA).
  4. If you live far away, offer to stay with your parent for a few days. That lets the main caregiver take a vacation (NIA).

What if my parent refuses respite care?

Start small and build it around what your parent wants. NIA suggests mentioning your worry without sounding critical (NIA). NIA suggests trying to fulfill your parent's wishes as far as possible. NIA suggests offering specific, practical help (NIA).

  1. Say what you need, calmly. For example, "I need a few hours to rest on Thursdays." Ask what would make that time comfortable for your parent.
  2. Start with a short visit by a familiar person while you're in the next room or on a quick errand.
  3. Call it company, not "a sitter": "Someone's coming to keep you company while I run errands."
  4. If you're worried about your parent's health or memory, suggest a visit to a health care provider. Or talk with a geriatric care manager, as NIA suggests. A geriatric care manager is a professional who helps coordinate care (NIA).
