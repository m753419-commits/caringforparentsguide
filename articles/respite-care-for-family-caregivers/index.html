Respite Care for Family Caregivers: How to Take a Real Break

By Ellen Carter. Published October 31, 2026. Publisher: Family Caregiver Practical Guide.

The first time someone suggested respite care to me, I nodded politely and changed the subject. A break sounded lovely and completely impossible. Who would make my mother's lunch the way she liked it? Who would know that she got anxious around four in the afternoon? And honestly, what kind of daughter needs a break from her own mother?

It took a bad winter, when I was running on four hours of sleep and snapping at everyone, for me to try it. A friendly woman from a local agency came for one afternoon a week. My mother grumbled for the first two visits, then started saving the crossword for her. I used those afternoons to sleep, see a friend, and once, just sit in my car in a parking lot and listen to the radio.

That small break didn't make me a worse caregiver. It made me a better one. This guide explains what respite care is, the options you may have, and how to plan your first break.

Please note: This post is general information, not medical, legal, or financial advice. Program availability, eligibility, and costs vary by state and community. Check with local agencies for details that apply to your family.

Key takeaways

  • Respite care is short-term care for your parent so you, the caregiver, can rest.
  • It can be a few hours at home, a day program, or a short overnight stay.
  • Your local Area Agency on Aging and the ARCH National Respite Locator are good places to start.
  • Plan ahead: try respite before you're in a crisis so your parent gets used to it.
  • A break is part of caregiving, not a failure at it.

What Is Respite Care?

Respite care is temporary care for the person you look after, so that you can take a break. It can last a few hours or several days, and it can happen at home or somewhere else. The goal is simple: give the caregiver time to rest, handle their own life, or recover from illness, while the parent is cared for safely.

It is not a sign that you've given up. It's one of the most practical ways to keep caregiving sustainable. If you're already seeing signs of exhaustion, read our guide to caregiver burnout warning signs, then come back here.

Types of Respite Care

Type What it looks like Good for
In-home respite A paid aide, trained volunteer, or friend comes to your parent's home Parents who are most comfortable at home
Adult day programs Your parent spends part of the day at a center with activities and supervision Social parents; caregivers who work
Short-term residential stays A few days or weeks in an assisted living community or nursing home with available space Vacations, surgery, or longer breaks
Informal respite Family, friends, neighbors, or a faith group take a regular shift Families with a willing network
Emergency respite Short-notice care during a caregiver's crisis Illness, a family emergency, or travel you can't avoid

The ARCH National Respite Network notes that emergency respite is easier to arrange if you've planned for it in advance and, ideally, used the provider before (ARCH, Find a Respite Provider).

How to Find Respite Care Near Your Parent

Start with these places:

  1. Your local Area Agency on Aging (AAA). Respite is one of the supports offered through the National Family Caregiver Support Program, which is most often run by AAAs. According to ARCH, it serves family caregivers of people age 60 or older, among others. Find your AAA through the Eldercare Locator (eldercare.acl.gov, 1-800-677-1116).
  2. Your state's Lifespan Respite Program or State Respite Coalition. Where they exist, ARCH suggests contacting them first for information about providers or ways to pay.
  3. The ARCH National Respite Locator Service. A searchable listing of respite providers by state. ARCH notes it isn't a complete list, so check other sources too.
  4. Adult day centers. Search for adult day services in your parent's area and visit before enrolling.
  5. Home care agencies. Many offer short shifts. Our guide to in-home care vs. assisted living explains how in-home help works.
  6. Veterans' programs. If your parent is a veteran, ask about the VA Caregiver Support Program; ARCH lists the VA caregiver support line as 1-855-260-3274.
  7. Faith communities and local nonprofits. Some run volunteer visiting or day programs at low or no cost.

How Respite Is Paid For

Costs and coverage vary a lot. Depending on where your parent lives and their situation, options may include:

  • Private payment
  • Long-term care insurance, if your parent has a policy (check what it covers)
  • State or local programs, including some respite voucher programs
  • Medicaid programs in some states, depending on eligibility
  • Veterans' benefits for eligible veterans
  • Free or low-cost help from volunteers and faith groups

Ask every provider what they charge and what funding sources they accept. Our guide to what it costs to care for an aging parent can help you fit respite into the bigger budget picture.

Questions to Ask a Respite Provider

  • ☐ What services do you provide, and what can't you do?
  • ☐ How are your staff or volunteers screened and trained?
  • ☐ Do you have experience with memory loss or the needs my parent has?
  • ☐ What's the minimum visit length or stay?
  • ☐ How much notice do you need? Can you help in an emergency?
  • ☐ What does it cost, and what payment sources do you accept?
  • ☐ Who do I call if there's a problem during the visit?
  • ☐ Can my parent visit or meet the caregiver first?

Planning Your First Break

Start small. A two-hour visit while you're in the next room or running a quick errand lets everyone get comfortable.

Introduce it the right way. Many parents resist "a sitter." Try "Someone's coming to keep you company while I run errands," or "This is a club with lunch and music." Our guide to talking to your aging parent about help has more scripts.

Write a one-page care sheet. Include:

  • ☐ Emergency contacts, including you and a backup
  • ☐ Your parent's routine: meals, naps, favorite activities
  • ☐ Things that comfort them and things that upset them
  • ☐ Any instructions from their doctor or pharmacist (keep these exactly as given)
  • ☐ Where to find keys, the information folder, and the doctor's number

Keep it regular. A predictable weekly break is easier on your parent than an occasional one, and easier for you to protect.

Actually rest. It's tempting to use every respite hour for errands. Save at least some of it for sleep, friends, exercise, or doing nothing at all.

When Your Parent Says No

Resistance is common. A few approaches that help:

  • Make it about you. "I need to see my own doctor on Tuesdays" is harder to argue with than "You need someone here."
  • Pick a person they already like. A familiar neighbor or relative is a gentle first step.
  • Agree to a trial. "Let's try it four times and then decide."
  • Ask siblings to help. If a brother or sister can take regular shifts, that's respite too. See our guide to splitting caregiving responsibilities among siblings.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is respite care for the elderly?

Respite care is short-term care for an older adult so that their regular caregiver can take a break. It may be a few hours of in-home help, a day at an adult day program, or a short stay in a residential community.

How do I find respite care near me?

Start with your parent's local Area Agency on Aging, which you can find through the Eldercare Locator. Your state's Lifespan Respite Program or Respite Coalition and the ARCH National Respite Locator are other good starting points.

Is respite care covered by insurance?

It depends. Some long-term care insurance policies, state programs, Medicaid programs, and veterans' benefits may help pay for respite, depending on eligibility. Ask each provider and program directly what they cover.

How often should a caregiver take respite?

There's no fixed rule. Many caregivers find that a regular, predictable break, even a few hours each week, is easier to sustain and easier for their parent to accept than an occasional long one.

What if my parent refuses respite care?

Start small, choose someone your parent already knows if possible, and frame it as something you need. A short trial period often helps a parent get comfortable.
