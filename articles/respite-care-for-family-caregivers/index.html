Respite Care for Family Caregivers: How to Take a Real Break

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 3, 2026

US programs only.

What is respite care?

Respite care is short-term care for your parent that gives you, the primary caregiver (the person who handles most of the everyday care), time to rest, travel, or see family and friends. It can last from a few hours to several weeks, at home, in a health care facility, or at an adult day care center (National Institute on Aging).

  1. Decide what kind of break you need: a few regular hours, a few days away, or backup for an emergency. ARCH describes emergency respite as short-term care that may last from one hour to several weeks, in or out of the home (ARCH).
  2. Ask family, friends, or volunteers first. NIA says respite from a friend, relative, or volunteer may have no cost (NIA).
  3. Get prices for professional respite. NIA says professional services charge by the hour or by the number of days or weeks (NIA).
  4. Choose a type from the table below, and list the days and times you need covered before you call providers.
Types of respite care
Type What it looks like Good for
In-home respite A paid aide, trained volunteer, or friend comes to your parent's home Parents who are most comfortable at home
Adult day programs Your parent spends part of the day at a center with activities and supervision Social parents; caregivers who work
Short-term residential stays A few days or weeks in an assisted living community or nursing home with available space Vacations, surgery, or longer breaks
Informal respite Family, friends, neighbors, or a faith group take a regular shift Families with a willing network
Emergency respite Short-notice care during a caregiver's crisis Illness, a family emergency, or travel you can't avoid

How do I find respite care near my parent?

Start with your state's Lifespan Respite Program or State Respite Coalition (state-level respite groups that ARCH says can tell you about providers and ways to pay), then your parent's local Area Agency on Aging (ARCH National Respite Network).

  1. Contact your state's Lifespan Respite Program or State Respite Coalition first, where one exists; the ARCH locator page lists them by state (ARCH).
  2. Call the Area Agency on Aging (AAA), the local agency that runs many services for older adults. Respite is one of the supports in the National Family Caregiver Support Program, most often run by AAAs, and it serves family caregivers of people 60 or older, among others. Find the AAA through the Eldercare Locator (eldercare.acl.gov, 1-800-677-1116).
  3. Search the ARCH National Respite Locator, which lists mostly home care agencies, assisted living, and agencies that provide respite. ARCH says it is not a complete list.
  4. Check other local options: adult day centers (ARCH notes they are often covered by Medicaid or offered on a sliding fee scale), home care agencies that offer short shifts, faith groups and nonprofits, and, for a veteran, the VA Caregiver Support Line at 1-855-260-3274.
  5. Line up emergency respite before you need it. ARCH advises finding emergency providers in advance and using them for planned respite first, so your parent and the provider already know each other.

Questions to ask a respite provider:

  • ☐ What services do you provide, and what can't you do?
  • ☐ How are your staff or volunteers screened and trained?
  • ☐ Do you have experience with memory loss or the needs my parent has?
  • ☐ What's the minimum visit length or stay?
  • ☐ How much notice do you need? Can you help in an emergency?
  • ☐ What does it cost, and what payment sources do you accept?
  • ☐ Who do I call if there's a problem during the visit?
  • ☐ Can my parent visit or meet the caregiver first?

One-page care sheet to leave with the provider:

  • ☐ Emergency contacts, including you and a backup
  • ☐ Your parent's routine: meals, naps, favorite activities
  • ☐ Things that comfort them and things that upset them
  • ☐ Any instructions from their doctor or pharmacist (keep these exactly as given)
  • ☐ Where to find keys, the information folder, and the doctor's number

Does Medicare cover respite care?

Through hospice (comfort care for someone who is terminally ill), yes: Medicare covers short-term inpatient respite care that the hospice team arranges, and NIA says it covers most of the cost for up to five consecutive days in a hospital or skilled nursing facility (Medicare.gov; NIA). Outside hospice, Medicare doesn't pay for long-term care, including adult day health care (Medicare.gov).

  1. Check hospice eligibility (Medicare.gov). Your parent needs Part A (Medicare hospital insurance); the hospice doctor (and regular doctor, if any) must certify a life expectancy of 6 months or less; and your parent must choose comfort care instead of treatment to cure the illness.
  2. Ask the hospice team to arrange respite. Medicare covers the facility stay for respite care the hospice team arranges. Your parent may pay 5% of the Medicare-approved amount (the amount Medicare sets as payment for a service) for inpatient respite care, and that copayment can't be more than the inpatient deductible.
  3. Expect other hospice costs to be small: nothing for hospice care from a Medicare-approved provider, and up to $5 per prescription for pain and symptom drugs. Medicare doesn't pay room and board at home or in a nursing home.
  4. If your parent isn't in hospice, call the state Medicaid office and the Area Agency on Aging and ask what respite help your parent could get; NIA says Medicaid also may provide payment assistance (NIA).

Is respite care covered by insurance, and who else pays?

NIA says most private health insurance plans don't cover respite care, though some long-term care insurance plans may, and you pay whatever insurance or government programs don't cover (NIA). Medicaid home and community-based waivers, the local Family Caregiver Support Program, state Lifespan Respite voucher programs, and VA may help pay (ARCH).

  1. Read any long-term care insurance policy your parent has, and ask the insurer whether it covers respite and what it needs from you to pay.
  2. Ask the state Medicaid office. ARCH says most Medicaid home and community-based services (HCBS) waivers (Medicaid programs that pay for care at home or in the community) include planned and emergency respite; eligibility and funding differ by state.
  3. Ask the Area Agency on Aging whether its Family Caregiver Support Program has respite funds, including for emergencies, and ask your state Lifespan Respite Program about respite vouchers (ARCH says state Lifespan Respite Programs increasingly offer vouchers to help pay for emergency respite).
  4. For a veteran, ask VA. All enrolled veterans are eligible for VA Respite Care if they meet the clinical criteria and it's available; a copay may apply (VA). Family caregivers in VA's Program of Comprehensive Assistance for Family Caregivers get at least 30 days of respite care per year (VA).

How often should a caregiver take respite?

No federal rule sets how often; NIA suggests arranging regular respite care, and the ARCH National Respite Network suggests getting respite at least once a week, when possible (NIA; ARCH, 9 Steps to Respite Care). In the Administration for Community Living's (ACL) evaluation of the National Family Caregiver Support Program, caregivers who got 4 or more hours of respite a week reported less burden over time (ARCH summary of the ACL evaluation, 2018).

  1. Put a weekly break on the calendar, as ARCH suggests, covered by a volunteer, a relative, an in-home aide, or an adult day program (ARCH, 9 Steps to Respite Care). NIA gives the example of another family member offering to stay with the older person one afternoon a week (NIA).
  2. Plan longer breaks too. Respite can last from a few hours to several weeks, so book a few days for rest or travel ahead of time (NIA).
  3. Ask family and friends for specific shifts. NIA suggests asking for small things first, sending a text or email if asking face-to-face is hard, and thinking about each person's skills (NIA).
  4. If you live far away, offer to stay with your parent for a few days so the primary caregiver can take a vacation (NIA).
  5. Keep using planned respite. ARCH says planning for an emergency is easier for families who regularly use planned respite (ARCH).

What if my parent refuses respite care?

Start small and build it around what your parent wants: NIA suggests mentioning your worry without sounding critical, trying to fulfill your parent's wishes as far as possible, and offering specific, practical help (NIA).

  1. Say what you need, calmly, for example: "I need a few hours to rest on Thursdays." Then ask what would make that time comfortable for your parent.
  2. Start with a short visit while you're in the next room or on a quick errand.
  3. Introduce it as company, not "a sitter," for example: "Someone's coming to keep you company while I run errands."
  4. Pick a familiar person first, such as a neighbor or relative, and agree to a set number of visits before deciding.
  5. If you're worried about your parent's health or memory, suggest a visit to a health care provider, or speak with a geriatric care manager (a professional who helps coordinate care), as NIA suggests (NIA).
