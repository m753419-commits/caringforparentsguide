Respite Care for Family Caregivers: How to Take a Real Break

Quick answer

  • What it is: Short-term care for your parent, from a few hours to several weeks, at home, at an adult day center (a center where your parent spends the day with activities and supervision), or in a facility, so you can rest (National Institute on Aging, NIA).
  • Who to call first: The Eldercare Locator (a free federal helpline), 1-800-677-1116 or eldercare.acl.gov, to reach your parent's local Area Agency on Aging (the local public office for older-adult services). For a veteran, call the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) Caregiver Support Line, 1-855-260-3274 (VA Caregiver Support Program).
  • Who pays: Medicare usually pays only during hospice (up to 5 days at a time) or, for some people with dementia, through its GUIDE program (up to $2,500 a year). Otherwise: Medicaid, your local caregiver program, the VA, or long-term care insurance.

How do I find and apply for respite care near my parent?

Call the Eldercare Locator at 1-800-677-1116 (or use eldercare.acl.gov) and ask for your parent's Area Agency on Aging, the local office that most often runs respite through the family caregiver support program (ARCH, Access to Respite Care and Help, a national respite resource center). Then contact your state's Lifespan Respite Program or State Respite Coalition (state-level respite groups that can tell you about providers and ways to pay), which ARCH lists by state (ARCH).

  1. Ask the Area Agency on Aging how to apply for respite through its family caregiver program, which serves family caregivers of people 60 or older, among others (ARCH).
  2. For a veteran, call the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) Caregiver Support Line at 1-855-260-3274 (VA Caregiver Support Program; ARCH).
  3. Search the ARCH National Respite Locator for home care agencies, assisted living (housing with help with daily activities), and other providers; ARCH says it is not a complete list, and adult day centers are often covered by Medicaid (health coverage run by each state for people with low income and few savings) or charge on a sliding fee scale (prices based on income) (ARCH).
  4. Find emergency respite (care on short notice) before a crisis, as ARCH advises, and use the same provider for planned breaks so your parent gets to know them (ARCH).

Questions to ask a respite provider:

  • ☐ What services do you provide, and what can't you do?
  • ☐ How are your staff or volunteers screened and trained?
  • ☐ Do you have experience with memory loss or my parent's needs?
  • ☐ What's the minimum visit or stay, and how much notice do you need? Can you help in an emergency?
  • ☐ What does it cost, and what payment do you accept?

One-page care sheet to leave with the provider: emergency contacts, including you and a backup; your parent's routine (meals, naps, favorite activities); things that comfort them and things that upset them; any instructions from their doctor or pharmacist, kept exactly as given; and where to find keys, the information folder, and the doctor's number.

Does Medicare cover respite care?

Mainly through hospice (comfort care for someone who is terminally ill): Medicare (federal health insurance for people 65 or older who meet citizenship or residency requirements, and for some younger people with a disability, End-Stage Renal Disease (permanent kidney failure), or ALS; Medicare.gov) covers short-term inpatient respite that the hospice team arranges, for up to 5 days at a time (it can be used more than once), and NIA says it pays most of the cost in a hospital or skilled nursing facility (a nursing home with nurses and therapists on staff) (Medicare Hospice Benefits, March 2026; Medicare.gov hospice page; NIA). Outside hospice, Medicare doesn't pay for long-term care, including adult day health care (Medicare.gov).

  1. Check hospice eligibility: your parent needs Part A (Medicare hospital insurance), the hospice doctor (and regular doctor, if any) to certify a life expectancy of 6 months or less, and a choice of comfort care instead of treatment to cure the illness (Medicare.gov).
  2. Ask the hospice team to arrange respite. Your parent may pay 5% of the Medicare-approved amount (the payment Medicare sets for a service) for inpatient respite care (Medicare.gov).
  3. If your parent has dementia: Medicare's GUIDE (Guiding an Improved Dementia Experience) Model (started July 1, 2024) also pays up to $2,500 a year for respite for some people with dementia whose care team takes part (CMS, checked November 2026).
  4. If your parent isn't in hospice, see who else pays below.

Is respite care covered by insurance, and who else pays?

Most private health insurance doesn't cover respite, though some long-term care insurance (private insurance that helps pay for daily care) may, and you pay what insurance or government programs don't (NIA). Medicaid, the Area Agency on Aging's family caregiver program, state respite vouchers, and VA may help pay (ARCH).

  1. Ask the state Medicaid office about home and community-based services waivers, Medicaid programs that pay for care at home or in the community; ARCH says most include planned and emergency respite, and rules differ by state (ARCH). Find your state's Medicaid office through Medicaid.gov (NIA), and see how Medicaid pays for care and whether Medicaid can pay a family caregiver.
  2. Ask the Area Agency on Aging whether its family caregiver program has respite funds, including for emergencies, and ask your state respite program about vouchers (money toward the cost), which ARCH says state programs increasingly offer for emergencies (ARCH).
  3. For a veteran, ask VA. Veterans enrolled in VA health care whose health needs meet VA's rules (clinical criteria) can get VA Respite Care where it's available, and a copay (a set fee you pay) may apply (VA). Caregivers in VA's Program of Comprehensive Assistance for Family Caregivers (a VA program that supports, and pays a monthly stipend to, eligible family caregivers of veterans) get at least 30 days of respite a year (VA).
  4. Read any long-term care insurance policy your parent has, and ask the insurer whether it covers respite.

What is respite care, and what types are there?

Respite care is short-term care for your parent that gives you, the main caregiver, time to rest, travel, or see family and friends. It can last from a few hours to several weeks, at home, in a health care facility, or at an adult day center (NIA).

  1. Decide what break you need: regular hours, a few days away, or emergency backup.
  2. Ask family, friends, or volunteers first; NIA says their help may have no cost (NIA).
  3. Compare prices for professional respite, which NIA says is charged by the hour or by the number of days or weeks (NIA). As a rough guide, 2025 national medians were $35 an hour for a non-medical in-home caregiver and $95 a day for adult day health care (CareScout/Genworth, private survey, 2025; see our cost guide).
Types of respite care
Type What it looks like Good for
In-home respite A paid aide, trained volunteer, or friend comes to your parent's home Parents most comfortable at home
Adult day programs Part of the day at a center with activities and supervision Social parents; caregivers who work
Short-term residential stays A few days or weeks in assisted living or a nursing home with space Vacations, surgery, or longer breaks
Informal respite Family, friends, neighbors, or a faith group take a regular shift Families with willing helpers
Emergency respite Short-notice care, from one hour to several weeks (ARCH) Illness, a family emergency, or unavoidable travel

How often should a caregiver take respite?

No federal rule sets how often, but the ARCH National Respite Network suggests respite at least once a week, when possible, and NIA suggests arranging regular respite (ARCH, 9 Steps to Respite Care; NIA). In a 2018 evaluation by the Administration for Community Living (the federal agency for older adults and people with disabilities), caregivers who got 4 or more hours of respite a week reported less burden over time (ARCH summary of the evaluation).

  1. Put a weekly break on the calendar, covered by a volunteer, a relative, an in-home aide, or an adult day program (ARCH, 9 Steps to Respite Care). If you work, see caring for a parent while working.
  2. Book longer breaks ahead of time, since respite can last up to several weeks (NIA).
  3. Ask family and friends for specific shifts, starting with small requests; NIA says a text or email works if asking face-to-face is hard (NIA).
  4. If you live far away, offer to stay with your parent for a few days so the main caregiver can take a vacation (NIA).

What if my parent refuses respite care?

Start small and build it around what your parent wants: NIA suggests mentioning your worry without sounding critical, trying to fulfill your parent's wishes as far as possible, and offering specific, practical help (NIA).

  1. Say what you need, calmly, for example: "I need a few hours to rest on Thursdays," and ask what would make that time comfortable for your parent.
  2. Start with a short visit by a familiar person while you're in the next room or on a quick errand.
  3. Call it company, not "a sitter": "Someone's coming to keep you company while I run errands."
  4. If you're worried about your parent's health or memory, suggest a visit to a health care provider, or talk with a geriatric care manager (a professional who helps coordinate care), as NIA suggests (NIA).
