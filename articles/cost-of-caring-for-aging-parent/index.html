How Much Does It Cost to Care for an Aging Parent?

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 4, 2026

US programs only.

In plain words: Prices for care vary widely by state, so start with a monthly budget. Medicare is U.S. government health insurance for older people and some younger people with disabilities. It doesn't pay for long-term help with daily tasks like bathing and dressing. Medicaid, a joint federal and state program for people with limited income and resources, may help.

How much do in-home care, assisted living, and nursing homes cost?

2025 national medians (middle prices): an in-home caregiver cost $35 an hour, and assisted living cost $6,200 a month. A semi-private (shared) nursing home room cost $315 a day (CareScout).

We worked out the in-home monthly cost from CareScout's median. The math is $35 × 44 hours × 52 weeks ÷ 12 ≈ $6,673. CareScout's annual figure is $80,080. The other figures are CareScout's.

Families also pay out of pocket, meaning from their own money. AARP's 2021 study found family caregivers spent an average of $7,242 a year. That was about 26% of their income (AARP).

  1. Look up your parent's area on the CareScout cost tool. Prices vary widely by state. State and local prices can be far from the national median.
  2. Price the hours you need. Use this math. Hours per week × local hourly rate × 52 ÷ 12 = monthly cost. At $35 an hour, 20 hours a week is about $3,033 a month. (Our math: $35 × 20 × 52 ÷ 12.)
  3. Get two or three written quotes. Ask these questions. What is the hourly rate, and is there a minimum number of hours? Are weekend, holiday, or overnight rates different? If you use an agency, are caregivers its employees? Who handles taxes and insurance? If you hire on your own, have you asked a tax professional about household employer duties? Those are the tax rules for paying someone who works in a home. What is the cancellation policy?
  4. Add the costs families often forget. These include agency minimum hours and moving costs. Running two households until a house sells is another. Also count equipment and supplies, like grab bars and shower chairs. Count incontinence supplies (for bladder or bowel leaks) and mobility aids too. Mobility aids are tools that help someone move around. Add your own travel and lost work hours too.
  5. Fill in a monthly budget (below). It shows the gap between costs and your parent's income. Also price a mix of care types. For example, take 20 paid hours a week at the national medians (about $3,033). Add 8 adult day program days a month (8 × $95 = $760). That comes to about $3,793 a month. Compare that with about $6,673 for 44 hours a week of in-home care. (Our math uses CareScout medians.)

How much does assisted living cost per month?

The 2025 national median was $6,200 a month, or $74,400 a year, says CareScout. Costs in your parent's area may be very different (CareScout).

The National Institute on Aging (NIA) describes assisted living. It is for people "who need help with daily care, but not as much help as a nursing home provides" (NIA).

  1. Look up the median for your parent's area with the CareScout cost tool.
  2. Get the full price in writing from each community. Ask for the base monthly rate and the levels of care. Also ask what each extra service costs. Examples are medication management (help taking medicines), laundry, or transportation. NIA notes that residents pay more if they need extra services or special care (NIA).
  3. Ask how and when prices go up. Ask about yearly increases and moves to a higher level of care.
  4. Ask the state Medicaid office whether it helps pay. Medicaid is a joint federal and state program. It helps with health costs for people with limited income and resources. NIA says Medicaid may provide coverage for some aspects of assisted living (NIA).
  5. Compare with care at home. Here is the math. In-home help at CareScout's national median is $35 an hour. More than about 41 hours a week of it costs more than $6,200 a month. (Our math: $35 × 41 × 52 ÷ 12 ≈ $6,218.)

Does Medicare pay for long-term care?

No, Medicare doesn't pay for long-term care, and neither do Medigap (Medicare Supplement Health Insurance) or most health insurance (Medicare.gov). Long-term care, also called custodial care, is help with everyday personal tasks such as bathing, dressing, and using the bathroom.

  1. Separate custodial care from skilled care. Custodial care can be at home or in a facility. Skilled care is care from nurses or therapists. Medicare may pay for some short-term skilled care when specific conditions are met. It doesn't pay for custodial care when that is the only care your parent needs.
  2. Short nursing home stays. Part A (hospital insurance) covers a skilled nursing facility (SNF: daily skilled nursing or therapy). This is generally after a qualifying inpatient hospital stay of at least 3 days. Inpatient means admitted to the hospital. Costs are set per benefit period. It begins the day your parent is admitted as an inpatient. It ends after 60 days in a row without inpatient hospital or SNF care (Medicare.gov, Your Medicare Benefits). In 2026, your parent pays $0 a day for days 1–20 after the $1,736 Part A deductible. The deductible is what your parent pays before Medicare pays. Then it is $217 a day for days 21–100, and all costs after day 100 (Medicare.gov; CMS 2026 fact sheet).
  3. Care at home. Medicare covers part-time skilled home health care for a parent who is homebound. That means leaving home takes great effort. It doesn't cover 24-hour care at home (Medicare.gov).
  4. Ask about your parent's case at 1-800-MEDICARE (1-800-633-4227).
  5. Plan other payment for long-term care. Medicare.gov says people may get long-term care through Medicaid if they meet state eligibility rules. Another way it lists is private long-term care insurance. Call the state Medicaid office. Read any policy your parent owns (Medicare.gov).

Does Medicare pay for a home health aide?

Medicare covers home health aide care only part-time, and only while your parent also gets skilled nursing care or therapy. It doesn't cover personal care (help with bathing, dressing, or using the bathroom) when that is the only care needed (Medicare.gov).

  1. Check that your parent is homebound. Medicare.gov's definition has two parts. First, leaving home isn't recommended because of their condition, or they need help to leave. Examples of help are a cane, walker, or wheelchair. Special transportation or another person also counts. Second, leaving home takes a lot of effort, so they normally can't.
  2. Get an in-person visit and an order. A doctor or other provider must see your parent in person. One example is a nurse practitioner, a nurse with advanced training. The provider must also confirm your parent needs home health care and order it.
  3. Use a Medicare-certified home health agency. That means one approved by Medicare. A home health aide helps with things like walking, bathing, or grooming. Ask the agency to confirm the aide visits are in the plan of care. That is the written plan for your parent's care.
  4. Know the hour limits. Intermittent means off and on. "Part-time or intermittent" usually means skilled nursing and home health aide services up to 8 hours a day combined. The maximum is 28 hours a week.
  5. Budget for the rest. 24-hour care at home isn't covered. Neither are homemaker services (help with chores like cleaning) unrelated to the care plan.

Does a hospital "observation" stay count toward Medicare's 3-day rule?

No, time under observation or in the emergency room before admission doesn't count, even if your parent stays overnight. Medicare requires a 3-day inpatient stay for SNF coverage (Medicare.gov).

  1. Ask the hospital directly. Observation is outpatient care while doctors decide whether to admit someone. Outpatient means care without being admitted. Ask whether your parent has been formally admitted as an inpatient. Also ask on what date. Medicare counts the 3 days from the day of inpatient admission. It doesn't include the day of discharge (Medicare.gov).
  2. Before discharge, ask whether Medicare will cover a SNF stay. An Accountable Care Organization (ACO) is a group of doctors, hospitals, and other providers. They accept Original Medicare (regular Medicare). They work together to coordinate care (Medicare.gov). The 3-day rule may not apply if the doctor is in an ACO approved for a "Skilled Nursing Facility 3-Day Rule Waiver." Some Medicare Advantage plans (Medicare plans from private companies) also waive it. Ask the plan.
  3. If the hospital changed your parent's status, Medicare.gov says your parent may be able to appeal. That means a change from inpatient to "outpatient getting observation services." To appeal is to ask for a decision to be reviewed.
  4. If there's no qualifying stay, ask the discharge planner about care in other settings. That is the hospital staff member who plans the move out. One is home health care. Also ask about other programs such as Medicaid or VA (Medicare.gov suggests both). VA is the Department of Veterans Affairs.

Can my parent get paid help through Medicaid?

Possibly, since Medicaid covers things Medicare normally doesn't, like nursing home care and personal care services (Medicare.gov). Nearly all states offer home care through home and community-based services (HCBS) waivers (Medicaid.gov).

  1. Call your parent's state Medicaid office (find it at Medicaid.gov). Ask who qualifies. The income, asset, and medical rules vary by state.
  2. Ask which HCBS waivers your parent could use. HCBS waivers are Medicaid programs. They pay for long-term care services at home or in the community instead of in an institution. Ask whether there is a waiting list. States can run several waivers. Each is for a target group (a set group of people).
  3. If your parent has both Medicare and Medicaid, they are dual eligible. Medicare pays first and Medicaid pays last (Medicare.gov).
  4. Know about estate recovery. Enrollees are people in the program. For enrollees 55 or older, states must seek repayment from the estate. The estate is what a person leaves when they die. This is for Medicaid-paid nursing facility and home and community-based services. It doesn't apply if a spouse, a child under 21, or a blind or disabled child survives (Medicaid.gov).
  5. Talk with an elder law attorney early. That's a lawyer who focuses on older adults' legal issues. Ask about asset and transfer rules before moving money or property. Transfer rules cover giving money or property away.

If Medicare doesn't pay, what does?

Usually a mix of your parent's own money, Medicaid if they qualify, and long-term care insurance (Medicare.gov). A veteran or survivor who gets a VA pension may also qualify for Aid and Attendance payments (VA).

  1. Start with your parent's own money. This includes Social Security, pensions, retirement accounts, and home equity. Home equity is the value of the home minus what is owed on it. Get independent financial advice before selling or borrowing against a home. If family members contribute, agree on it and track it in writing.
  2. Apply for Medicaid if your parent may qualify through the state Medicaid office.
  3. Read any long-term care insurance policy. Look for the waiting period and covered services. The waiting period is how long your parent must need care before benefits start. Is your parent a veteran or a surviving spouse? Then check VA Aid and Attendance or Housebound benefits. These are payments added to a VA pension.
  4. Use free help to find benefits. Try BenefitsCheckUp, from the National Council on Aging. Try your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) too. SHIP can also review your parent's Medicare plan each open enrollment. That is the yearly time to change plans. Local programs can be found through the Eldercare Locator (eldercare.acl.gov, 1-800-677-1116; Eldercare Locator). They include meals, respite (a short break for caregivers), and transportation.

What if my parent can't afford Medicare costs?

Apply for a Medicare Savings Program through the state and for Extra Help with drug costs. Medicare.gov says to apply even if you don't think your parent qualifies (Medicare.gov).

  1. Apply for a Medicare Savings Program (MSP) through your parent's state. An MSP is a state program. It helps pay Medicare Part A and Part B (medical insurance) premiums. Premiums are regular charges for coverage. For some people, it also helps pay deductibles, coinsurance, and copayments. These are your parent's share of the cost. The state decides which program fits. One is the Qualified Medicare Beneficiary (QMB) program. For an individual, its 2026 federal limits are $1,350 a month in income and $9,950 in resources. Resources means savings and other assets. For a married couple, they are $1,824 and $14,910. Limits are slightly higher in Alaska and Hawaii. States may allow more.
  2. If your parent qualifies for QMB, Medicare providers can't bill them for Medicare deductibles, coinsurance, or copayments for covered care. Have your parent show both the Medicare card and the Medicaid or QMB card at every visit.
  3. Apply for Extra Help (help paying Part D drug costs) through Social Security, any time. The 2026 limits for an individual are $23,940 in income and $18,090 in resources. For a married couple, they are $32,460 and $36,100 (Medicare.gov).
  4. Get free help applying from your SHIP (shiphelp.org) and check other programs on BenefitsCheckUp.

Are caregiving expenses tax-deductible?

Sometimes, but for 2025 returns only if you itemize (list deductions on Schedule A). You can deduct only the part of medical expenses above 7.5% of your adjusted gross income (AGI) (IRS Publication 502 (2025)).

  1. Check whether your parent counts as your dependent for the medical expense deduction. AGI is your income after certain adjustments. The IRS lets you count some care costs as medical expenses. These are costs for yourself, your spouse, or your dependent. Publication 502 lists the requirements.
  2. Separate nursing-type care from household help. Counted costs include nursing services and qualified long-term care services. That is care a chronically ill person needs under a plan. The plan is prescribed by a licensed health care practitioner. The IRS says only the amount spent for nursing services counts. If an aide also does household work, divide the pay by time spent on each.
  3. For a nursing home, the cost of medical care counts. Meals and lodging (the room) count too. That applies if a principal reason for being there is to get medical care.
  4. If you pay for care so you can work, ask about the child and dependent care credit. A credit lowers the tax you owe. It can apply to care for a person who is physically or mentally not able to care for themselves. The person must also have lived with you for more than half the year (IRS Publication 503 (2025)).
  5. Keep every receipt and ask a tax professional before you claim anything.
