How much do in-home care, assisted living, and nursing homes cost?

At 2025 national medians, an in-home caregiver costs $35 an hour, assisted living $6,200 a month, and a semi-private nursing home room $315 a day (CareScout). Prices vary widely by state.

Families also pay out of pocket: AARP's 2021 study found family caregivers spent an average of $7,242 a year, about 26% of their income (AARP).

  1. Look up your parent's area on the CareScout cost tool; state and local prices can be far from the national median.
  2. Price the hours you need. Hours per week × local hourly rate × 52 ÷ 12 = monthly cost. At $35 an hour, 20 hours a week is about $3,033 a month (our calculation: $35 × 20 × 52 ÷ 12).
  3. Get two or three written quotes and ask: What is the hourly rate, and is there a minimum number of hours? Are weekend, holiday, or overnight rates different? Through an agency, are caregivers employees, and who handles taxes and insurance? Hiring privately, have you asked a tax professional about household employer duties? What is the cancellation policy?
  4. Add the costs families often forget: agency minimum hours, moving costs, running two households until a house sells, equipment and supplies (grab bars, shower chairs, incontinence supplies, mobility aids), and your own travel and lost work hours.
  5. Fill in a monthly budget (below) to see the gap between costs and your parent's income, and price a mix of care types. At the national medians, 20 paid hours a week (about $3,033) plus 8 adult day program days a month (8 × $95 = $760) comes to about $3,793 a month, compared with about $6,673 for 44 hours a week of in-home care (our calculation from CareScout medians).

How much does assisted living cost per month?

The 2025 national median was $6,200 a month ($74,400 a year), according to CareScout; costs in your parent's area may be very different (CareScout). Assisted living is, in the National Institute on Aging's words, for people "who need help with daily care, but not as much help as a nursing home provides" (NIA).

  1. Look up the median for your parent's area with the CareScout cost tool.
  2. Get the full price in writing from each community: the base monthly rate, the levels of care, and what each extra service costs (for example medication management, laundry, or transportation). NIA notes that residents pay more if they need extra services or special care (NIA).
  3. Ask how and when prices go up, both yearly increases and moves to a higher level of care.
  4. Ask the state Medicaid office whether it helps pay. NIA says Medicaid may provide coverage for some aspects of assisted living (NIA).
  5. Compare with care at home. At CareScout's national medians ($35 an hour), more than about 41 hours a week of in-home help costs more than $6,200 a month (our calculation: $35 × 41 × 52 ÷ 12 ≈ $6,218).

Does Medicare pay for long-term care?

No: Medicare doesn't pay for long-term care (also called custodial care: help with everyday personal tasks such as bathing, dressing, and using the bathroom, at home or in a facility), and neither do Medigap or most health insurance (Medicare.gov).

  1. Separate custodial care from skilled care. Medicare may pay for some short-term skilled care when specific conditions are met; it doesn't pay for custodial care when that is the only care your parent needs.
  2. Short nursing home stays: Part A covers a skilled nursing facility (SNF: daily skilled nursing or therapy) generally after a qualifying inpatient hospital stay of at least 3 days. In 2026, per benefit period (it begins the day your parent is admitted as an inpatient and ends after 60 days in a row without inpatient hospital or SNF care; Medicare.gov, Your Medicare Benefits), your parent pays $0 a day for days 1–20 after the $1,736 Part A deductible, $217 a day for days 21–100, and all costs after day 100 (Medicare.gov; CMS 2026 fact sheet).
  3. Care at home: Medicare covers part-time skilled home health care for a parent who is homebound (leaving home takes great effort), but not 24-hour care at home (Medicare.gov).
  4. Ask about your parent's case at 1-800-MEDICARE (1-800-633-4227).
  5. Plan other payment for long-term care. Medicare.gov says people may get long-term care through Medicaid if they meet state eligibility rules, or through private long-term care insurance; call the state Medicaid office and read any policy your parent owns (Medicare.gov).

Does Medicare pay for a home health aide?

Only part-time, and only while your parent also gets skilled nursing care or therapy: Medicare covers home health aide care (help with things like walking, bathing, or grooming) on those terms, but not personal care (help with bathing, dressing, or using the bathroom) when that is the only care needed (Medicare.gov).

  1. Check that your parent is homebound. Medicare.gov's definition has two parts: leaving home isn't recommended because of their condition, or they need help to leave (a cane, walker, wheelchair, special transportation, or another person); and leaving home takes a lot of effort, so they normally can't.
  2. Get an in-person visit and an order. A doctor or other provider such as a nurse practitioner must see your parent in person, confirm they need home health care, and order it.
  3. Use a Medicare-certified home health agency, and ask it to confirm the aide visits are in the plan of care.
  4. Know the hour limits. "Part-time or intermittent" usually means skilled nursing and home health aide services up to 8 hours a day combined, for a maximum of 28 hours a week.
  5. Budget for the rest: 24-hour care at home and homemaker services unrelated to the care plan aren't covered.

Does a hospital "observation" stay count toward Medicare's 3-day rule?

No: Time in the hospital under observation (outpatient care while doctors decide whether to admit someone), or in the emergency room before admission, doesn't count toward the 3-day inpatient stay Medicare requires for SNF coverage, even if your parent stays overnight (Medicare.gov).

  1. Ask the hospital directly whether your parent has been formally admitted as an inpatient, and on what date. Medicare counts the 3 days from the day of inpatient admission, not including the day of discharge (Medicare.gov).
  2. Before discharge, ask whether Medicare will cover a SNF stay. The 3-day rule may not apply if the doctor participates in an Accountable Care Organization (ACO: a group of doctors, hospitals, and other providers that accept Original Medicare and work together to coordinate care; Medicare.gov) approved for a "Skilled Nursing Facility 3-Day Rule Waiver." Some Medicare Advantage plans also waive it; ask the plan.
  3. If the hospital changed your parent from inpatient to "outpatient getting observation services," Medicare.gov says your parent may be able to appeal.
  4. If there's no qualifying stay, ask the discharge planner about care in other settings, such as home health care, or other programs such as Medicaid or VA (Medicare.gov suggests both).

Can my parent get paid help through Medicaid?

Possibly: Medicaid covers things Medicare normally doesn't, like nursing home care and personal care services (Medicare.gov), and nearly all states offer home care through home and community-based services (HCBS) waivers (Medicaid programs that pay for long-term care services at home or in the community instead of in an institution) (Medicaid.gov).

  1. Call your parent's state Medicaid office (find it at Medicaid.gov) and ask about eligibility; the income, asset, and medical rules vary by state.
  2. Ask which HCBS waivers your parent could use and whether there is a waiting list; states can run several waivers, each for a target group.
  3. If your parent has both Medicare and Medicaid, they are dual eligible: Medicare pays first and Medicaid pays last (Medicare.gov).
  4. Know about estate recovery. For enrollees 55 or older, states must seek repayment from the estate for Medicaid-paid nursing facility and home and community-based services, but not if a spouse, a child under 21, or a blind or disabled child survives (Medicaid.gov).
  5. Talk with an elder law attorney early about asset and transfer rules before moving money or property.

If Medicare doesn't pay, what does?

Usually a mix of your parent's own money, Medicaid if they qualify, and long-term care insurance (Medicare.gov). A veteran or survivor who gets a VA pension may also qualify for Aid and Attendance payments (VA), and local programs can be found through the Eldercare Locator (Eldercare Locator).

  1. Start with your parent's own money: Social Security, pensions, retirement accounts, and home equity (get independent financial advice before selling or borrowing against a home). If family members contribute, agree on it and track it in writing.
  2. Apply for Medicaid if your parent may qualify through the state Medicaid office.
  3. Read any long-term care insurance policy for the waiting period (how long your parent must need care before benefits start) and covered services. If your parent is a veteran or a surviving spouse, check VA Aid and Attendance or Housebound benefits.
  4. Use free help to find benefits: BenefitsCheckUp (National Council on Aging), your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP, which can also review your parent's Medicare plan each open enrollment), and the Eldercare Locator (eldercare.acl.gov, 1-800-677-1116) for local meals, respite, and transportation programs.

What if my parent can't afford Medicare costs?

Apply for a Medicare Savings Program through the state and for Extra Help with drug costs. Medicare.gov says to apply even if you don't think your parent qualifies (Medicare.gov).

  1. Apply for a Medicare Savings Program (MSP) through your parent's state. An MSP is a state program that helps pay Medicare Part A and Part B premiums and, for some people, deductibles, coinsurance, and copayments; the state decides which program fits. For the Qualified Medicare Beneficiary (QMB) program, 2026 federal limits are $1,350 a month in income and $9,950 in resources (savings and other assets) for an individual ($1,824 and $14,910 for a married couple), slightly higher in Alaska and Hawaii. States may allow more.
  2. If your parent qualifies for QMB, Medicare providers can't bill them for Medicare deductibles, coinsurance, or copayments (your parent's share of the cost) for covered care. Have your parent show both the Medicare card and the Medicaid or QMB card at every visit.
  3. Apply for Extra Help (help paying Part D drug costs) through Social Security, any time. 2026 limits: income $23,940 and resources $18,090 for an individual; $32,460 and $36,100 for a married couple (Medicare.gov).
  4. Get free help applying from your SHIP (shiphelp.org) and check other programs on BenefitsCheckUp.

Are caregiving expenses tax-deductible?

Sometimes: the IRS lets you count some care costs for yourself, your spouse, or your dependent as medical expenses, including nursing services and qualified long-term care services (care a chronically ill person needs under a plan prescribed by a licensed health care practitioner). For 2025 returns, you can deduct them only if you itemize (list deductions on Schedule A) and only the part above 7.5% of your adjusted gross income (AGI: your income after certain adjustments) (IRS Publication 502 (2025)).

  1. Check whether your parent counts as your dependent for the medical expense deduction; Publication 502 lists the requirements.
  2. Separate nursing-type care from household help. The IRS says only the amount spent for nursing services counts; if an aide also does household work, divide the pay by time spent on each.
  3. For a nursing home, the cost of medical care counts, and meals and lodging count too if a principal reason for being there is to get medical care.
  4. If you pay for care so you can work, ask about the child and dependent care credit, which can apply to care for a person who is physically or mentally not able to care for themselves and lived with you for more than half the year (IRS Publication 503 (2025)).
  5. Keep every receipt and ask a tax professional before you claim anything.
