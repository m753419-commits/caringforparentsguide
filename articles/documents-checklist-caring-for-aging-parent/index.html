What Documents Do You Need When Caring for an Aging Parent? (Checklist)

Published October 31, 2026. Publisher: Family Caregiver Practical Guide.

If you've ever stood in a hospital hallway trying to remember whether your mom ever signed a health care proxy, you know the feeling. Paperwork isn't the most important part of caring for a parent. Your relationship is. But missing paperwork can turn a hard week into an impossible one.

The good news is that you don't have to become a lawyer or an accountant. You just need to know what exists, where it is, and who is allowed to use it. This post walks you through the documents most families end up needing, in plain English, with a checklist you can print or copy.

Please note: This post is general information, not legal, financial, or medical advice. Laws and forms vary by state. Talk with an elder law attorney, your parent's doctor, or a financial professional about your family's situation.

Why Paperwork Matters Before a Crisis

Most families start looking for documents at the worst possible moment: after a fall, a stroke, or a confusing call from the bank. By then, your parent may not be able to tell you where things are, or may no longer be able to sign new documents.

Think of this as a calm, unhurried project you do with your parent, not to them. A good opening line is something like: "I'm not asking about how much money you have. I just want to know where things are and who you'd want to speak for you if you couldn't."

The Four Groups of Documents

It is often easiest to sort everything into four groups. You don't need every item in every group. You need to know which ones your parent has, which are missing, and which are out of date.

1. Health care decision documents

These say who can make medical decisions if your parent can't, and what kind of care they'd want.

  • Health care power of attorney (also called a health care proxy or agent). Names the person who can speak for your parent about medical care.
  • Living will or advance directive. Describes the kinds of treatment your parent would or wouldn't want near the end of life. The National Institute on Aging explains that advance directives are legal documents, and that states have their own forms, often available free.
  • Any state-specific medical order forms your parent's doctor may have completed, such as orders about resuscitation. Ask the doctor what applies in your state.

2. Financial and legal authority documents

These say who can act on your parent's behalf for money and property.

  • Durable financial power of attorney. Lets a named person handle banking, bills, and other financial matters. "Durable" generally means it stays in effect if your parent loses capacity, but check how it's written.
  • Will and/or trust documents. You don't need to read them, but you should know whether they exist and who has the originals.
  • Social Security representative payee information, if relevant. Social Security has its own program for managing benefits for someone who can't, and a power of attorney is not a substitute for it. (See ssa.gov/payee.)
  • VA fiduciary information, if your parent receives VA benefits and can't manage them.

3. Medical information

These help doctors, paramedics, and new caregivers understand your parent quickly.

  • A current medication list, including dose, schedule, prescriber, and over-the-counter products.
  • Insurance cards: Medicare, any Medicare Advantage or Medigap plan, Part D drug plan, Medicaid, and other coverage.
  • A one-page health summary: diagnoses, allergies, recent hospital stays, doctors' names and phone numbers.
  • HIPAA authorization forms at each doctor's office, so staff can share information with the family members your parent chooses.

4. Household and identity records

  • Social Security card, Medicare card, driver's license or state ID, birth certificate, marriage or divorce records, military discharge papers.
  • Bank, retirement, and investment account list (institution names and how to reach them, not passwords written in plain sight).
  • Recurring bills and which ones are on autopay.
  • Property deeds, lease, mortgage, car titles, and insurance policies (home, auto, life, long-term care).
  • Funeral or burial pre-arrangements, if any.

How to Have the Conversation

Imagine a father in his late seventies who is proud, private, and perfectly capable. His daughter wants to know about his documents but worries he'll hear "you're getting old." A few approaches tend to go better than a direct demand:

  • Lead with your own planning. "I just did my own health care proxy, and it made me wonder whether you have one."
  • Use a trigger event gently. A friend's hospital stay, a news story, or a doctor's suggestion can open the door.
  • Ask about wishes before documents. "If you were ever really sick, who would you want talking to the doctors?" The document comes naturally after that.
  • Offer help, not control. "Would it help if I made a list of where things are, so you don't have to remember it all?"
  • Expect more than one conversation. That's normal.

If your parent wants to create or update documents, encourage them to work with an elder law attorney. The National Academy of Elder Law Attorneys has a directory, and your local Area Agency on Aging (reach it through the Eldercare Locator at eldercare.acl.gov or 1-800-677-1116) may know of free or low-cost legal help for older adults.

Where to Keep Everything

A document no one can find is almost as bad as no document at all.

  • Originals go in one safe, predictable place your parent chooses, such as a fireproof box at home or with their attorney. Ask the attorney whether a safe deposit box is a good idea, since access can be tricky.
  • Copies of the health care documents go to the named agent, the primary doctor, and the hospital your parent usually uses.
  • A one-page "where things are" sheet goes to the main caregiver and a backup. It should list where things are, not account passwords.
  • Review once a year, and after any big change, such as a move, a new diagnosis, a death in the family, or a change in who is helping.

A Note About Capacity

Many legal documents require that your parent understands what they're signing. If your parent is already having serious memory problems, talk with an attorney promptly about what's still possible. When people can no longer sign documents and none are in place, families sometimes have to go to court for guardianship or conservatorship. That process is usually slower, costlier, and more restrictive than planning ahead, and the U.S. Department of Justice describes guardianship as something to consider only after less restrictive options.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a lawyer to make a health care proxy or living will?

Not always. Many states offer free advance directive forms, and the National Institute on Aging notes that you don't necessarily need a lawyer. For financial powers of attorney, wills, and trusts, an elder law attorney is usually worth it.

Can I use my parent's power of attorney to manage their Social Security?

No. Social Security does not accept a power of attorney for managing benefits. It has its own representative payee program. Contact Social Security to ask about it.

My siblings and I live in different states. Whose state's forms do we use?

Generally, documents should follow the law of the state where your parent lives. If your parent splits time between states, ask an attorney.

What if my parent refuses to talk about any of this?

Keep the door open, start with small pieces like the medication list, and try again later. Sometimes a doctor or trusted friend can raise the topic more easily than a child can.

Key takeaways

  • Sort documents into four groups: health decisions, financial authority, medical information, and household records.
  • Use the checklist to find gaps, not to finish everything at once.
  • Keep originals in one known place and copies with the people who'll need them.
  • Plan while your parent can still take part. Get legal help for anything you're unsure about.
