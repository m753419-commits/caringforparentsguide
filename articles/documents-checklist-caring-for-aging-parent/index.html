What Documents Do You Need When Caring for an Aging Parent? (Checklist)

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 3, 2026.

US programs only.

In plain words: This guide is a checklist of papers that help when you care for a parent. You do not need every paper on day one. Start with what your parent already has. Then fill the gaps over time.

What documents do I need when caring for an aging parent?

We sort them into four groups (our grouping, based on NIA's checklist): health care decisions, financial authority, medical information, and household and identity records. Find which ones your parent has, which are missing, and which are out of date (NIA). NIA is the National Institute on Aging, a federal research agency.

  1. Health care decisions: a health care power of attorney (also called a health care proxy: the person who speaks for your parent about medical care). And a living will or advance directive (the treatment your parent would or wouldn't want). The National Institute on Aging says a lawyer can help but is not required. Many states have their own forms you can get for free. Examples are AARP (a large nonprofit group for people age 50 and older), the American Bar Association, or the National Hospice and Palliative Care Organization (NIA). Also ask the doctor about medical orders such as a do not resuscitate (DNR) order. That order goes in the medical chart.
  2. Financial authority: a durable financial power of attorney lets a named person handle banking and bills. NIA describes a durable power of attorney for finances as naming someone to make financial decisions when your parent is unable to (NIA). Know whether a will or trust exists and who has the originals. NIA suggests talking with a lawyer about a durable power of attorney, a joint account, or a trust.
  3. Medical information: a current medication list (dose, schedule, prescriber, and over-the-counter products). All insurance cards (Medicare, Medicare Advantage or Medigap, Part D, Medicaid). And a one-page health summary. Our suggestion for that page: diagnoses, allergies, recent hospital stays, and doctors' contacts. Ask each doctor's office for its form to share information with family. Under HIPAA, providers may share with family involved in your parent's care if your parent doesn't object. They aren't required to unless you are your parent's personal representative (HHS).
  4. Household and identity records: Social Security and Medicare cards, ID, and birth, marriage, or divorce records. Military discharge papers. A list of bank and retirement accounts (institutions and how to reach them, not passwords). Recurring bills and autopay. Deeds, leases, titles, and insurance policies. Any funeral or burial arrangements.
  5. Store and review them. NIA suggests putting important papers and copies of legal documents in one place. A fireproof and waterproof safe is one option. If papers are in a bank safe deposit box, keep copies in a file at home. Tell someone trusted where to find them (NIA). Give copies of advance directives to the health care proxy, health care providers, and any lawyer. Review them at least once a year. Also review them after a major change, such as a move out of state or a significant change in health (NIA).

Do I need a lawyer to make a health care proxy or living will?

No, a lawyer is not required. The National Institute on Aging says a lawyer can help, and many states have their own forms you can complete for free (NIA).

  1. Get the free form for your parent's state. If you use forms from a website, NIA says to check that they are legally recognized in your state (NIA).
  2. Help your parent fill it in and name a health care proxy they trust. Ask the state legal aid office or state bar association if you have questions about your state's rules, as NIA suggests.
  3. Give copies to the health care proxy, your parent's health care providers, and any lawyer (NIA).
  4. Talk with a lawyer for the money documents. NIA says you may want a lawyer for a general or durable power of attorney, joint account, or trust, and to ask about fees before making an appointment (NIA).
  5. Find legal help through your local bar association. It can also point to free legal aid. Or use the Eldercare Locator (1-800-677-1116), or the National Academy of Elder Law Attorneys directory at naela.org.

My siblings and I live in different states. Whose state's forms do we use?

Your parent's state, because advance directive forms must be legally recognized where your parent lives. If your parent spends a lot of time in more than one state, use each state's form and keep a copy in each place (NIA).

  1. List where your parent lives and any other state where they spend a lot of time. Where you and your siblings live doesn't change which form to use.
  2. Get each of those states' forms, and check that any website form is legally recognized there (NIA).
  3. To find out a state's laws, contact that state's legal aid office or state bar association, as NIA suggests.
  4. Keep a copy in each place, and give copies to the health care proxy and to your parent's health care providers in each state.
  5. Review the documents at least once a year and after a move to another state (NIA).

How can I talk to Medicare for my parent?

Your parent must first give 1-800-MEDICARE written permission to share their personal health information with you, using form CMS-10106 ("Authorization to Disclose Personal Health Information") (CMS-10106).

  1. Get the form from Medicare.gov's forms page, ideally while your parent can still sign it.
  2. Choose what to share and for how long. The form lets your parent pick "any information" or limited information (eligibility, claims, plan enrollment, premium payments), and share it indefinitely or between set dates. Your state may limit how long.
  3. Submit it. The fastest way is online through your parent's secure Medicare.gov account; or mail the signed form to 1-800-MEDICARE Written Authorization Dept., PO Box 1270, Lawrence, KS 66044.
  4. Know it can be changed. Your parent can cancel or change the authorization at any time by writing to the same address. For a parent who has died, include legal papers such as executor papers with the form.

Can I use my parent's power of attorney to manage their Social Security?

No, Social Security (the federal agency that pays retirement and disability benefits) says power of attorney is not the same as being a representative payee (SSA). Being an authorized representative or sharing a joint bank account is not the same either. A representative payee is the person or organization Social Security appoints to manage someone's benefits.

  1. Apply to be the payee. A payee must apply for and be appointed by Social Security. Start at ssa.gov/payee or call 1-800-772-1213.
  2. Expect a yearly report. Social Security can ask a payee to complete an annual Representative Payee Report accounting for the benefits. Under a 2018 law (Public Law 115-165), SSA no longer requires that report from some people who live with the beneficiary. Those people are spouses, certain parents, and legal guardians. Some state mental institutions are also exempt. Exempt payees can still have site reviews. A site review is a review in which Social Security checks how a payee is managing the benefits (SSA payee FAQ; SSA POMS GN 00605.015, effective May 26, 2023).
  3. Use form SSA-1696 only for a claim. It appoints a representative for a claim or other issue pending with Social Security. The form itself says not to file it if there is no claim or pending issue (SSA-1696).
  4. For VA benefits, ask about a VA fiduciary. When VA finds a beneficiary can't manage their financial affairs, it appoints a fiduciary, normally chosen by the beneficiary and checked by VA first (VA Fiduciary Program).
  5. Ask each bank, insurer, or agency what it accepts before you rely on a power of attorney there.

How do I get my parent to talk about this paperwork?

Start with something small and practical, like the medication list, and come back to the rest over several talks. Your parent doesn't have to discuss their personal affairs, but NIA says someone they trust should know where to find their papers in case of an emergency (NIA).

  1. Start with the medication list. It's useful right away and less personal than money.
  2. Raise your concern without sounding critical, as NIA suggests, and ask what your parent wants before suggesting specific, practical help (NIA). If talking is hard, ask a doctor or trusted friend to raise it.
  3. Ask for the minimum first: one trusted person who knows where the papers are (NIA).
  4. Act early if memory is slipping. NIA says that without an advance directive, if your parent is unable to make decisions, state law decides who makes medical decisions for them (NIA). Find legal help through the local bar association or the Eldercare Locator (1-800-677-1116).
  5. Know the fallback. If your parent can no longer decide and nothing is in place, a family member may have to petition a court for guardianship. A guardian is a court-appointed decision-maker. Some states call a property manager a conservator. It is governed by state law. The court may remove rights such as deciding where to live, consenting to medical treatment, and managing property (U.S. Department of Justice).
