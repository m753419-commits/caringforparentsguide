Signs an Elderly Parent Can No Longer Live Alone (and What to Do Next)

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 4, 2026

US programs only.

Please note: This post is general information, not legal, financial, or medical advice.

If your parent is in immediate danger, call 911: NIA (the National Institute on Aging, a federal research agency) gives this advice for families (NIA; full disclaimer).

How do I know if my parent can't live alone anymore?

Look for changes at home and in health: NIA lists signs that someone needs extra support, including significant weight gain or loss, poor hygiene, confusion, falls, loneliness, trouble getting around, and memory issues (NIA).

  1. Changes at home: NIA suggests asking whether your parent can prepare meals on a stove safely, bathes regularly and dresses for the weather, keeps the home relatively clean, and has and takes needed medications (NIA).
  2. Mood: NIA says changes in a person's mood could indicate a need for help (NIA).
  3. Memory: occasional forgetfulness is a normal part of aging, but NIA says more significant memory problems, changes in thinking or personality, or poor decision-making could indicate a serious condition that needs medical attention; see our guide to early signs of dementia (NIA).
  4. Already watching from afar? See signs a parent needs more help and what to check during a visit.

Which daily tasks should I watch?

Watch personal care and running the home: NIA describes activities of daily living (basic self-care tasks) as bathing, dressing, grooming, using the toilet, eating, and moving around, such as getting out of bed and into a chair (NIA).

  1. Household tasks (sometimes called instrumental activities of daily living, or IADLs: the tasks needed to live on your own): NIA lists housecleaning, yard work, grocery shopping, laundry, preparing meals, paying bills, and filling out health insurance forms among the kinds of help people may need (NIA).
  2. Health care and getting around: NIA also lists help with medications, caring for wounds, rides to the doctor or store, and safety features and help in case of a fall (NIA).
  3. The same tasks guide official assessments: Medicaid (a joint federal and state program that helps with health costs for people with limited income and resources) functional assessments (structured questionnaires about what a person can do) most commonly include activities of daily living, instrumental activities of daily living, memory and cognition (Medicaid.gov).
  4. Make the home safer now: see our home safety checklist.

What health risks make living alone unsafe?

Falls and medicine problems top the list: the CDC (the U.S. public health agency) says falls are the leading cause of injury for adults 65 and older, and over 14 million, or 1 in 4 older adults, report falling every year (CDC, page checked November 2026; CDC).

  1. Fall deaths are rising: the age-adjusted (adjusted so groups of different ages can be compared) fall death rate increased by 21%, from 64.7 per 100,000 older adults in 2018 to 78.4 in 2024 (CDC).
  2. Medicines: NIA says older adults often have multiple conditions and may take many medicines, which puts them at additional risk for negative side effects (NIA).
  3. Ask the doctor to review all medicines, including those prescribed by other doctors, and remind your parent not to take medicine in the dark (NIA).
  4. Help after a fall: see our guide to medical alert systems.

Who can assess what my parent needs?

Start with a doctor: NIA suggests that if you're concerned about your parent's physical or mental health, you suggest a visit to a health care provider and offer to make the appointment, drive them, or go with them (NIA).

  1. Local services: the Eldercare Locator (a federal service connecting you to local help for older adults) can help you find in-home help and transportation and give information about paying for care; call 800-677-1116 (NIA; Eldercare Locator).
  2. Area Agency on Aging (the local public office for older adults' services): federal law defines it as an agency designated under the Older Americans Act (the federal law that funds services for older adults) (42 U.S.C. 3002; govinfo).
  3. Medicaid assessment: states use functional assessments to decide Medicaid level-of-care eligibility (whether a person needs the level of care a nursing home gives) and to plan services, and the tools vary by state (Medicaid.gov; 42 CFR 441.302).
  4. Hiring help to coordinate: see what a geriatric care manager does (in plain terms, a professional who helps families plan and arrange care for an older person).

What are the options besides moving out?

Bring help into the home: federal law lists in-home services for older adults such as homemakers and home health aides, visiting and telephone reassurance, chore maintenance, in-home respite care (short-term care that gives a caregiver a break), adult day care, minor home modifications, and personal care (42 U.S.C. 3002; govinfo).

  1. Adult day care: NIA says these programs provide a safe environment with trained staff and may offer social activities, exercise, meals, personal care, and basic health care; Medicare (the federal health insurance program for people 65 and older) will not pay for adult day care, but Medicaid and other government programs may (NIA).
  2. Medicare home health (skilled care at home for an illness or injury): your parent must need part-time or intermittent (now and then, not every day around the clock) skilled services (care that needs a nurse or therapist) and be homebound (leaving home takes a lot of effort or isn't recommended); Medicare doesn't pay for 24-hour-a-day care at home, home meal delivery, or custodial care (help with bathing, dressing, or using the bathroom) when that is the only care needed (Medicare.gov).
  3. Try home care first: see can we try in-home care first?
  4. Moving in with family: see moving a parent into your home; NIA lists living with a family member among housing options (NIA).

When is it time for assisted living or a nursing home?

When home help is no longer enough: NIA says there may be a time when an older relative can no longer live independently at home, and the choice may depend on health, ability to do daily activities, finances, and personal preferences (NIA).

  1. Assisted living (housing with help for daily care) is, NIA says, for people who need help with daily care but not as much as a nursing home provides, with services like meals, personal care, help with medications, and 24-hour supervision (NIA).
  2. Nursing homes, also called skilled nursing facilities, focus more on medical care, with nursing care, 24-hour supervision, meals, and help with everyday activities; Medicare does not pay for assisted living and generally doesn't cover long-term nursing home stays (NIA).
  3. Compare places: use Medicare's Care Compare tool (an official search tool for rating providers) to find and compare nursing homes and home health services (Medicare.gov).
  4. Decide together: see when it's time for assisted living, care costs, and Medicaid and nursing homes.

What if my parent refuses help?

Raise concerns without criticism: NIA suggests mentioning your worry gently, for example asking whether they're having trouble getting to the store, then trying to fulfill your parent's wishes as much as possible (NIA).

  1. Offer specific, practical help with each suggestion, as NIA recommends (NIA).
  2. Talk through the pros and cons of each housing option before deciding (NIA).
  3. More scripts: see what if my parent refuses help?
  4. If your parent can no longer make decisions, see when guardianship is needed.

What should we do right away if it's unsafe?

Call for help now: NIA says if your family member is in immediate danger, call 911, and if your parent says they feel hopeless or you're worried they may harm themselves, call or text the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (a 24-hour crisis line) at 988 (NIA; NIA).

  1. Adult Protective Services (APS: state or local programs that protect vulnerable adults): under federal rules, APS receives and investigates reports of abuse, neglect, financial exploitation, and self-neglect (when an adult can't take care of their own basic needs) (45 CFR 1324.401; eCFR).
  2. Self-neglect means a serious risk of imminent harm because a physical or mental impairment or diminished capacity (reduced ability to understand and decide) keeps the adult from getting essential food, clothing, shelter, and medical care, or from managing their own finances (45 CFR 1324.401; eCFR).
  3. How to report: each state must accept reports 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, through multiple methods including at least one online method; to find your state or local APS, call the Eldercare Locator at 800-677-1116 (45 CFR 1324.405; eCFR; CFPB).
  4. After a hospital stay: see hospital discharge and home health, and keep key papers ready with our documents checklist.
