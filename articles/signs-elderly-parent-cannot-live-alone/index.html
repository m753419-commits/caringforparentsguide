Signs an Elderly Parent Can No Longer Live Alone (and What to Do Next)

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 7, 2026

US programs only.

Please note: This post is general information, not legal, financial, or medical advice.

If your parent is in immediate danger, call 911. NIA (the National Institute on Aging, a federal research agency) gives this advice for families (NIA; full disclaimer).

In plain words: This guide helps you tell if your parent needs more help at home. Watch how they handle daily tasks, health, and safety. Falls and medicine problems are big risks. Your first step is a talk and a visit to the doctor.

How do I know if my parent can't live alone anymore?

Look for changes at home and in health, like significant weight gain or loss (a big change in weight). NIA lists these signs, plus poor hygiene, confusion, falls, loneliness, trouble getting around, and memory issues (NIA).

  1. Changes at home: NIA suggests asking some questions. Can your parent cook on a stove safely? Do they bathe regularly and dress for the weather? Do they keep the home fairly clean? Do they have and take the medicines they need (NIA)?
  2. Mood: NIA says changes in mood could mean a person needs help (NIA).
  3. Memory: Forgetting things now and then is a normal part of aging. NIA says bigger memory problems could mean a serious health problem. So could changes in thinking or personality, or poor decisions. Any of these could mean a serious condition that needs a doctor's care. See our guide to early signs of dementia (NIA).
  4. Already watching from afar? See signs a parent needs more help and what to check during a visit.

Which daily tasks should I watch?

Watch personal care and running the home. NIA lists activities of daily living (basic self-care tasks) as bathing, dressing, grooming, using the toilet, eating, and moving around (NIA).

  1. Moving around: NIA's list includes moving around — for example, getting out of bed and into a chair.
  2. Household tasks: These are sometimes called IADLs (instrumental activities of daily living). They are the tasks needed to live on your own. NIA lists housecleaning, yard work, grocery shopping, and laundry. It also lists cooking, paying bills, and filling out health insurance forms (NIA).
  3. Health care and getting around: NIA also lists help with medicines and caring for wounds. It lists rides to the doctor or store. It also lists safety features and help in case of a fall (NIA).
  4. The same tasks guide official checks: Medicaid is a joint federal and state program. It helps with health costs for people with limited income and resources. It uses functional assessments. These are set lists of questions about what a person can do. They most often cover daily self-care, household tasks, and memory and thinking (Medicaid.gov).
  5. Make the home safer now: See our home safety checklist.

What health risks make living alone unsafe?

Falls are the leading cause of injury for adults 65 and older, the CDC (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, the U.S. government's main public health agency) says. Over 14 million, or 1 in 4 older adults, report falling every year (CDC, page checked November 2026; CDC).

  1. Fall deaths are rising: The age-adjusted fall death rate rose by 21%. Age-adjusted means groups of different ages can be compared fairly. The rate went from 64.7 per 100,000 older adults in 2018 to 78.4 in 2024 (CDC).
  2. Medicines: NIA says older adults often have many health problems. They may take many medicines. This puts them at more risk of bad side effects (NIA).
  3. Ask the doctor to review all medicines, even those from other doctors. Remind your parent not to take medicine in the dark (NIA).
  4. Help after a fall: See our guide to medical alert systems.

Who can assess what my parent needs?

Start with a doctor visit if you're worried about your parent's body or mind. NIA suggests offering to book it, drive your parent, or go along (NIA).

  1. Local services: The Eldercare Locator (a federal service that connects you to local help for older adults) can help. It can find in-home help and rides. It can also give facts about paying for care. Call 800-677-1116 (NIA; Eldercare Locator).
  2. Area Agency on Aging: This is the local public office for older adults' services. Federal law defines it as an agency named under the Older Americans Act. That is the federal law that funds services for older adults (42 U.S.C. 3002; govinfo).
  3. Medicaid check: States use these checks to decide level-of-care eligibility for Medicaid. That means whether a person needs the care a nursing home gives. States also use them to plan services. The tools vary by state (Medicaid.gov; 42 CFR 441.302).
  4. Hiring help to coordinate: See what a geriatric care manager does. In plain terms, this is a professional who helps families plan and arrange care for an older person.

What are the options besides moving out?

Bring help into the home. Federal law lists many in-home services for older adults (42 U.S.C. 3002; govinfo).

  1. What the law lists: It lists homemakers and home health aides. It lists visits and telephone reassurance (regular check-in calls). It lists chore help and in-home respite care (short-term care that gives a caregiver a break). It also lists adult day care, small home changes, and personal care.
  2. Adult day care: NIA says these programs give a safe place with trained staff. They may offer social activities, exercise, meals, personal care, and basic health care. Medicare (U.S. government health insurance for older people and some younger people with disabilities) will not pay for adult day care. Medicaid and other government programs may (NIA).
  3. Medicare home health means skilled care at home for an illness or injury. Your parent must need skilled care (care that needs a nurse or therapist) part-time or now and then. They must also be homebound. That means leaving home takes a lot of effort or isn't advised. Medicare doesn't pay for care at home 24 hours a day. It doesn't pay for meal delivery. It also doesn't pay for custodial care if that is the only care needed. Custodial care is help with bathing, dressing, or using the bathroom (Medicare.gov).
  4. Try home care first: See “Can we try in-home care first?” in our in-home care vs. assisted living guide.
  5. Moving in with family: See moving a parent into your home. NIA lists living with a family member as one housing option (NIA).

When is it time for assisted living or a nursing home?

When help at home is no longer enough. NIA says the choice may depend on health, daily abilities, money, and what your parent wants (NIA).

  1. Assisted living (housing with help for daily care) is for people who need daily help, NIA says. They need less help than a nursing home gives. Services include meals, personal care, help with medicines, and 24-hour supervision (NIA).
  2. Nursing homes are also called skilled nursing facilities. They focus more on medical care. They give nursing care, 24-hour supervision, meals, and help with daily tasks. Medicare does not pay for assisted living. It generally doesn't cover long nursing home stays (NIA).
  3. Compare places: Use Medicare's Care Compare tool (an official tool that rates providers). It helps you find and compare nursing homes and home health services (Medicare.gov).
  4. Decide together: See when it's time for assisted living, care costs, and Medicaid and nursing homes.

What if my parent refuses help?

Share your worry gently, without blame, then honor your parent's wishes as much as you can. NIA suggests asking whether they have trouble getting to the store (NIA).

  1. Offer clear, practical help with each idea, as NIA advises (NIA).
  2. Talk through the pros and cons of each housing choice before you decide (NIA).
  3. More scripts: see “What if my parent refuses help?” in our in-home care vs. assisted living guide.
  4. If your parent can no longer make decisions, see when guardianship is needed.

What should we do right away if it's unsafe?

Call 911 if your parent is in immediate danger, NIA says. If your parent says they feel hopeless or you're worried they may harm themselves, call or text the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (a 24-hour crisis line) at 988 (NIA; NIA).

  1. Adult Protective Services (APS) are state or local programs that protect adults at risk. Under federal rules, APS takes and looks into reports of abuse, neglect, and money exploitation. It also handles self-neglect (when an adult can't take care of their own basic needs) (45 CFR 1324.401; eCFR).
  2. Self-neglect means a serious risk of harm very soon. It happens when a health problem of body or mind, or diminished capacity, keeps the adult from basic needs. Diminished capacity means less ability to understand and decide. The basic needs are food, clothing, shelter, medical care, or handling their own money (45 CFR 1324.401; eCFR).
  3. How to report: Each state must take reports 24 hours a day, 7 days a week. There must be several ways to report, including at least one online. To find your state or local APS, call the Eldercare Locator at 800-677-1116 (45 CFR 1324.405; eCFR; CFPB).
  4. After a hospital stay: See hospital discharge and home health. Keep key papers ready with our documents checklist.
