Medicaid and Nursing Homes: The 5-Year Look-Back, Penalties, and the Healthy Spouse

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 4, 2026

US programs only.

Please note: This post is general information, not legal, financial, or medical advice.

Rules differ by state. Before moving any money, check with your state Medicaid office or an elder law attorney. Medicaid is a joint federal and state program. It helps with health costs for people with limited income and resources. An elder law attorney is a lawyer who works on older adults’ legal issues (full disclaimer).

In plain words: Medicare is U.S. health insurance for older people and some younger people with disabilities. It usually doesn't pay for a long nursing home stay, but Medicaid can. Medicaid has limits on money and property, and it checks past gifts, and gifts can delay help. Before you give away or move any money, get advice.

Does Medicare pay for a long nursing home stay?

Usually not, unless your parent needs skilled nursing care (care that needs a nurse or therapist). Part A (Medicare's hospital insurance) may cover up to 100 days of skilled nursing facility care (Medicare.gov).

  1. Know the short-term rule. Original Medicare (regular Medicare) covers short-term skilled nursing facility care after a hospital stay. It covers up to 100 days per benefit period. A benefit period is how Medicare measures use of hospital and nursing facility care (Medicare.gov).
  2. Expect to pay for custodial care if that is the only care your parent needs. Custodial care is help with daily tasks like bathing, dressing, or eating (Medicare.gov).
  3. Read our related answers on long-term care costs and Medicare and nursing homes.
  4. Leaving the hospital? See our hospital discharge guide.

How can Medicaid pay for a nursing home?

Nursing home care is a benefit Medicaid must cover for eligible people age 21 and older. States may not have waiting lists for it (Medicaid.gov; Medicaid.gov).

  1. Check the asset limit with your state. It varies by state. Federal rules don't count the home and one car as countable resources. Countable resources are the money and property Medicaid counts (Medicaid.gov; 20 CFR 416.1212; 20 CFR 416.1218).
  2. Ask about income rules. Some states use a medically needy program. It is a state option for people whose income or resources are above the regular limits. People "spend down" to qualify. That means they subtract medical bills from income. Other states use an income cap of 300% of the SSI benefit. SSI is Supplemental Security Income. The cap is $2,982 a month in 2026. These states may allow an income trust. It holds your parent's income. The state is repaid from what is left at death (Medicaid.gov; Medicaid.gov CIB; 42 U.S.C. 1396p(d)(4)(B)).
  3. Expect to pay most income to the facility. Your parent keeps a personal needs allowance of at least $30 a month under federal rules. This is money kept for clothing and other personal needs (42 CFR 435.725).
  4. Apply through your state Medicaid agency (Medicaid.gov). Coverage can usually start up to 3 months before the month you apply. This works if your parent was eligible then (42 CFR 435.915). If Medicaid says no, your parent can ask for a fair hearing. That is a review of the decision by the state. If your parent already gets benefits that will be ended or cut, ask for the hearing before the date of action (the date the change starts). This usually keeps benefits going while you wait. Each state sets the deadline to ask, and it can be up to 90 days (42 CFR 431.230 and 431.221(d)).
  5. Apply early: from January 1, 2027, this retroactive coverage gets shorter. Retroactive coverage is coverage for months before you apply. For applications on or after that date, it drops to 1 or 2 months. It depends on the eligibility group, which is the category a person qualifies under (Medicaid.gov CIB).

What is the Medicaid 5-year look-back?

It is a check of what your parent gave away or sold for less than it was worth. For transfers on or after February 8, 2006, it covers the 60 months before a key date (42 U.S.C. 1396p(c)(1)(B)).

  1. Know the key date. It is the first date your parent is both in a nursing home and has applied for Medicaid.
  2. Gather 5 years of records, such as bank statements and deeds. Transfers for less than fair market value (what something would sell for) can cause a penalty (42 U.S.C. 1396p(c)(1)).
  3. Know the exempt transfers. These are gifts with no penalty. The home can go to a spouse or a child under 21. It can go to a blind or disabled child. It can go to a sibling with an equity interest (an ownership share). The sibling must have lived there for at least 1 year before admission. Or it can go to a child who lived there for at least 2 years. That child must have given care that kept your parent out of a facility (42 U.S.C. 1396p(c)(2)(A)).
  4. Other assets can go to a spouse or to a blind or disabled child without a penalty.
  5. Talk to your state Medicaid office or an elder law attorney before giving anything away (Medicaid.gov).

How does the look-back penalty work?

The penalty is a time with no Medicaid nursing home payment. The state divides the value given away by the state's average monthly private-pay nursing home cost (42 U.S.C. 1396p(c)(1)(E)).

  1. Know the terms. The value given away without fair payment is called the uncompensated value. The state doesn't round the result down.
  2. Know when it starts. It starts on the later of two dates. One is the first day of the month of the transfer. The other is the date your parent would otherwise qualify for Medicaid nursing home care (42 U.S.C. 1396p(c)(1)(D)).
  3. The divisor varies by state. The divisor is the cost the state divides by. Texas uses $262.37 a day, starting September 1, 2025 (Texas HHS). New York uses monthly rates by region for 2026. Examples are $15,282 for New York City and $15,193 for Long Island, starting January 1, 2026 (New York State Department of Health).
  4. Ask about ways to remove the penalty. No penalty applies if all the assets given away are returned. States must also have an undue hardship waiver process. This is a state exception to the penalty for hardship. The nursing home may apply for it if your parent agrees (42 U.S.C. 1396p(c)(2)(C)–(D)).
  5. Ask about annuities and trusts only with a professional. Annuities are contracts that pay a set income over time. Trusts are legal setups where someone holds property for another person. Federal law has specific rules for them. Ask your state Medicaid office or an elder law attorney (42 U.S.C. 1396p(c)(1)(F), (d), (e)).

What happens to the spouse who stays at home?

Rules protect the spouse who still lives at home. That spouse keeps a share of the couple's resources and can get some of the other spouse's income (Medicaid.gov).

  • What the words mean: These are called spousal impoverishment rules. They keep the spouse at home from being left without money. The spouse at home is called the community spouse.
  1. Resources: The community spouse resource allowance is the share of the couple's resources the spouse at home can keep. It is at least $32,532 and at most $162,660 in 2026 (Medicaid.gov CIB).
  2. Income: The minimum monthly maintenance needs allowance is the monthly income the spouse at home may have. It is $2,705 from July 1, 2026 ($3,381.25 in Alaska and $3,111.25 in Hawaii). The 2026 maximum is $4,066.50 (Medicaid.gov CIB).
  3. 2027 amounts were not yet out when this guide was updated. Check Medicaid.gov.

What happens to my parent's house?

The home is usually not counted at first, but there are limits. In 2026, states exclude home equity (value minus debt) up to at least $752,000 and at most $1,130,000 (Medicaid.gov CIB).

  • Exceptions: The limit doesn't apply if a spouse, a child under 21, or a blind or disabled child lives there.
  1. A change is coming: Starting in 2028, the home equity limit will be capped at $1,000,000 (Medicaid.gov CIB).
  2. Know estate recovery. This is when the state collects from a person's estate after death to repay Medicaid costs. States must seek it for nursing home care paid for people age 55 and older (Medicaid.gov).
  3. Know the protections. States may not recover while there is a surviving spouse, a child under 21, or a blind or disabled child. States must have hardship waivers (Medicaid.gov).
  4. Ask about liens. A lien is a legal claim on property. States may place one on the home or land of a Medicaid enrollee who is permanently institutionalized. That means expected to stay in a facility. States can't do this if certain people live in the home. They are a spouse, a child under 21, a blind or disabled child, or a sibling with an equity interest. The state must remove the lien if your parent returns home (Medicaid.gov).
  5. Moving to another state? See our moving checklist.

Can the nursing home make me pay or sign as guarantor?

No, a nursing home may not make someone else guarantee payment to admit or keep your parent. It can ask someone with legal access to your parent's money to pay from that money (42 CFR 483.15).

  1. Read the admission agreement carefully before signing. Look for words about a guarantee (42 CFR 483.15).
  2. Know discharge protections. A facility may move out or discharge a resident only for specific listed reasons. One reason is not paying after fair notice (42 CFR 483.15).
  3. Ask if the facility takes Medicaid. A resident may qualify for Medicaid after moving in. Then they can be charged only the amounts Medicaid allows. A move may be needed if the home isn't Medicaid-certified (approved to take Medicaid payment) (42 CFR 483.15; Medicaid.gov).
  4. Get free help: Call the Eldercare Locator (1-800-677-1116). Or use SHIP (State Health Insurance Assistance Program), which gives free Medicare counseling (Eldercare Locator; Medicare.gov).
  5. Compare facilities on Care Compare, Medicare's tool for comparing providers.
