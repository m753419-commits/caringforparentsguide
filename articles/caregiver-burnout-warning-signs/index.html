Caregiver Burnout: Warning Signs and What to Do About It

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 3, 2026

This guide is written for families in the United States; the support services it points to, such as the Eldercare Locator, are US resources.

What are the warning signs of caregiver burnout?

Burnout shows up as emotional, physical, and behavioral signs, such as feeling overwhelmed or hopeless, being tired most of the time, and skipping your own care (National Institute on Aging; Office on Women's Health). It is common: in AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving's Caregiving in the U.S. 2025 report, 64% of caregivers reported high emotional stress (Executive Summary).

Warning signs named by NIA and the Office on Women's Health
Type Signs
Emotional Feeling overwhelmed, anxious, sad, or hopeless; becoming easily angered or impatient; feeling lonely or cut off; losing interest in activities you used to enjoy
Physical Feeling tired most of the time; sleeping too much or too little; frequent headaches or body aches; gaining or losing a lot of weight
Behavioral Skipping your own personal care or checkups; misusing alcohol or drugs, including prescription medicines; making mistakes when giving medicines
  1. Mark which signs in the table you have had for the past few weeks, and write them down; NIA advises learning your own warning signs before you are completely overwhelmed.
  2. Book an appointment with your own doctor, tell them you are a caregiver, and show them the list (NIA).
  3. If you feel anxious, frustrated, or depressed, ask your doctor for a referral to a counselor and check what your health plan covers (NIA).
  4. If you think you might hurt yourself or your parent, call or text 988 now (988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline).

What should I do when caregiving is wearing me out?

Reduce what you carry: hand specific tasks to other people, schedule regular breaks (respite, meaning someone else provides care for a while), and tell your doctor you are a caregiver (NIA; Office on Women's Health).

  1. Write down every task you do in a week and mark the ones someone else could do, then let helpers choose from that list (NIA; OWH).
  2. Put a regular break on the calendar with a volunteer, an in-home aide, or an adult day program (NIA); our respite care guide shows where to find one.
  3. Make the doctor's appointment you have been putting off and tell the doctor you are a caregiver (NIA).
  4. If you work, ask HR about leave. The Family and Medical Leave Act gives eligible employees of covered employers up to 12 workweeks of unpaid, job-protected leave in a 12-month period, including to care for a parent with a serious health condition (U.S. Department of Labor, page last updated January 26, 2026; checked November 2026); see our FMLA guide.
  5. Hold a family meeting to move tasks off your plate, using our guide to splitting caregiving responsibilities among siblings.

When should I get help right away?

If you feel you might hurt yourself or your parent, call or text 988 now, or call 911 if anyone is in immediate danger. The 988 Lifeline is free, confidential, and available 24/7 (988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline).

  1. Call or text 988, or use the chat at 988lifeline.org.
  2. Call 911 if you or your parent is in immediate danger.
  3. Ask a family member or friend to step in with your parent while you get help (NIA).
  4. If your parent has dementia, call the Alzheimer's Association 24/7 Helpline at 1-800-272-3900 (Alzheimer's Association).
  5. Afterward, see your doctor and ask for a referral to a counselor (NIA).

How do I take a break when there's no one else to step in?

Use respite care (short-term care by someone else, from a few hours to several weeks, at home, in a facility, or at an adult day center), which you can find through the ARCH National Respite Locator (National Institute on Aging) and your parent's Area Agency on Aging (Office on Women's Health).

  1. Call your parent's Area Agency on Aging through the Eldercare Locator (eldercare.acl.gov, 1-800-677-1116) and ask about respite and adult day programs.
  2. Search the ARCH National Respite Locator for state programs, adult day centers, and services for veterans (NIA).
  3. Hire an aide for a few hours a week, or enroll your parent in an adult day program (NIA).
  4. Ask a neighbor or your faith community for one small, specific task, such as sitting with your parent while you run an errand (OWH; NIA).

What if no one offers to help?

Ask instead of waiting: NIA suggests starting with small requests, sending them by text or email if asking face to face is hard, and expecting that some people will say no (National Institute on Aging).

  1. Pick one small, specific task and ask one person to do it (NIA).
  2. Send the request by text or email if that is easier for you (NIA).
  3. Keep your weekly task list handy and let the person choose from it when they say "Let me know if you need anything" (NIA).
  4. Ask beyond family: your doctor, a local senior center, the Area Agency on Aging, or your faith community (NIA).
  5. Bring in outside services such as respite care, nonmedical home care, or meal delivery; the Eldercare Locator can point you to them (OWH).

Where can I find a caregiver support group?

Ask your doctor, your parent's Area Agency on Aging, or a local congregation, and look at both in-person and online groups, including groups for caregivers of people with the same illness as your parent (NIA; Office on Women's Health).

  1. Ask your doctor or your parent's care team; NIA notes health care professionals may know about local support groups.
  2. Call the Area Agency on Aging through the Eldercare Locator (eldercare.acl.gov, 1-800-677-1116).
  3. Ask a local congregation; NIA notes larger congregations may host caregiver support groups.
  4. If your parent has dementia, call the Alzheimer's Association 24/7 Helpline at 1-800-272-3900 (Alzheimer's Association).
  5. If you can't leave home, join an online group (NIA).

Is caregiver burnout the same as depression?

Not necessarily, but they can overlap: long-term caregiver stress can lead to depression and anxiety (Office on Women's Health). Only a doctor or mental health professional can tell you which you have.

  1. Tell your doctor about any symptoms of depression and that you are a caregiver (OWH).
  2. Ask for a referral to a counselor and check your health plan's coverage (NIA).
  3. If you feel hopeless or think about harming yourself, call or text 988 (988 Lifeline).

Is it selfish to take time for myself?

No: NIA says taking time for yourself can actually make you a better caregiver (National Institute on Aging).

  1. Book your own checkups and keep them (OWH).
  2. Schedule a regular break, such as a few hours a week with an aide or adult day program (NIA).
  3. Set a standing time to stay in touch with friends and family (OWH).

General information, not medical or mental health advice. If you're worried about your health or mood, talk with your doctor or a mental health professional; in a crisis, call or text 988, or call 911 in an emergency.
