Caregiver Burnout: Warning Signs and What to Do About It

Published October 31, 2026. Publisher: Family Caregiver Practical Guide.

Caring for a parent can be meaningful. It can also be exhausting in a way that sneaks up on you. One week you're managing fine. A few months later you're snapping at your kids, lying awake at 3 a.m., and can't remember the last time you did something just for yourself.

That slow slide has a name: caregiver burnout. It's not a sign that you're weak or doing it wrong. It's a sign that you've been carrying too much for too long without enough support. Here's how to recognize it and what you can do.

Please note: This post is general information, not medical or mental health advice. If you're worried about your health or mood, talk with your doctor or a mental health professional. If you're in crisis or thinking about harming yourself, call or text 988 to reach the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, or call 911 in an emergency.

You're Not Alone

Burnout is common. In AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving's Caregiving in the U.S. 2025 report:

  • 64% of caregivers reported high emotional stress, and 45% reported physical strain.
  • One in five rated their own health as fair or poor.
  • 24% said they feel alone.
  • Caregivers who felt they had no choice in becoming a caregiver (56% of respondents) reported significantly worse mental health outcomes.

If you see yourself in those numbers, you're in a very large and very human company.

Common Warning Signs

Burnout looks different for different people, but these are common signals that you may be running on empty:

Emotional signs

  • Feeling irritable, resentful, or numb much of the time
  • Feeling hopeless or trapped
  • Losing interest in things you used to enjoy
  • Feeling guilty no matter what you do

Physical signs

  • Constant tiredness, even after rest
  • Trouble sleeping, or sleeping much more than usual
  • Getting sick more often
  • Headaches, aches, or changes in appetite

Behavioral signs

  • Pulling away from friends and family
  • Skipping your own medical appointments
  • Drinking more, or relying on other ways to numb out
  • Losing patience with your parent in ways that scare you

If you ever feel you might hurt your parent or yourself, that's a sign to get help right away. Call 988, or reach out to your doctor, a crisis line, or a trusted person today.

What to Do About It

1. Name it and tell someone

Saying "I'm burned out" out loud to a friend, sibling, or doctor is a real first step. Tell your doctor you're a caregiver. It's important context for your health.

2. Ask for specific help

Vague offers ("Let me know if you need anything") rarely turn into help. Make a list of specific tasks someone else could do, such as picking up prescriptions, sitting with Mom for two hours on Saturday, or handling insurance calls, and match them to people.

3. Plan real breaks (respite)

Respite means planned time off, from a few hours to a few days, while someone else provides care. Options can include family, friends, adult day programs, in-home help, or short-term stays in a residential setting. The ARCH National Respite Network's Respite Locator (archrespite.org) can help you look for services, and your Area Agency on Aging can tell you about local programs. Reach it through the Eldercare Locator at eldercare.acl.gov or 1-800-677-1116.

4. Find a support group

Talking with people who understand can help. Look for caregiver groups through your Area Agency on Aging, hospitals, faith communities, or condition-specific organizations. For dementia caregiving, the Alzheimer's Association has a 24/7 Helpline at 1-800-272-3900.

5. Protect the basics

  • Keep your own medical and dental appointments.
  • Protect some sleep, even if it means asking someone to cover a night.
  • Move a little each day: a short walk counts.
  • Eat real meals when you can.

6. Look at the care plan itself

Sometimes burnout is a sign that the plan needs to change, not that you need to try harder. Is it time for more paid help, a family meeting to rebalance tasks, or a conversation about a different level of care?

7. Know your rights at work

If you work, ask HR about leave and flexible schedules. According to the U.S. Department of Labor, the Family and Medical Leave Act provides eligible employees of covered employers up to 12 workweeks of unpaid, job-protected leave in a 12-month period for qualifying reasons, including caring for a parent with a serious health condition. Not everyone qualifies, and some states offer additional protections.

Small Daily Resets

Big changes take time to arrange. In the meantime, small daily habits can take the edge off:

  • A five-minute pause. Sit in the car for five minutes before going inside, breathe, and let your shoulders drop.
  • One thing that's just yours. A chapter of a book, a favorite podcast, a short call with a friend who makes you laugh.
  • A "done" list. At the end of the day, write down three things you did. Caregiving is full of invisible work, and seeing it on paper can help.
  • Lower the bar where you can. Paper plates, store-bought meals, and a less-than-spotless house are all fine.
  • Step outside. A few minutes of fresh air and daylight can help you reset.

Talking With Your Family About Burnout

Many caregivers hide how tired they are, especially from siblings. But your family can't help with what they don't know. Try being direct and specific: "I'm worn out. I need someone else to take Dad to his appointments this month, and I need one weekend off before the end of the season." If conversations tend to turn into arguments, consider asking a neutral person, such as a social worker or professional care manager, to help you plan a family meeting.

Imagine This

Imagine a woman in her fifties who has been her father's main caregiver for two years. She takes the self-check and realizes she has checked "often" for almost everything. She tells her doctor, who encourages her to look for support. She asks her brother to handle all insurance calls and her neighbor to sit with her dad on Thursday afternoons. She books an adult day program two days a week and joins an online caregiver group. Nothing is perfect, but for the first time in months, she has hours in the week that belong to her.

That's what recovery from burnout often looks like: not one big fix, but several small changes that add up.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is caregiver burnout the same as depression?

They can overlap, but they aren't the same. Only a health professional can tell you what's going on. If you're feeling persistently down or hopeless, talk with your doctor or a mental health professional.

How do I take a break when there's no one else?

Start with your Area Agency on Aging and the ARCH Respite Locator, ask about adult day programs, and consider asking friends, neighbors, or faith communities for small, specific help.

Is it selfish to take time for myself?

No. Taking care of yourself helps you keep caring for your parent over the long run.

When should I get help right away?

If you feel you might hurt yourself or your parent, call or text 988, or call 911 in an emergency.

Key takeaways

  • Burnout is common: 64% of caregivers report high emotional stress (AARP/NAC, 2025).
  • Watch for emotional, physical, and behavioral warning signs.
  • Ask for specific help, plan regular respite, and find support.
  • If the plan is wearing you out, change the plan.
  • In a crisis, call or text 988.
