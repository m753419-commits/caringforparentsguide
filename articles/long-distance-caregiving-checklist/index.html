Long-Distance Caregiving: How to Help a Parent From Far Away

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 7, 2026

This checklist is written for families in the United States. The resources it points to, such as the Eldercare Locator, are US services.

In plain words: You can still help a parent from far away. A few people nearby can check in for you. You can handle papers, bills, and finding services. Start by asking your parent what would help.

Please note: This guide is general information, not medical, legal, or financial advice. For health concerns, talk with your parent's doctor; for legal or financial decisions, consult a qualified professional in your parent's state.

How can I help my aging parent if I live far away?

You can do real work from a distance. The National Institute on Aging (NIA, a federal research agency) says long-distance caregivers can help with several tasks (NIA, What Is Long-Distance Caregiving?). Those tasks include researching care options, organizing paperwork and records, arranging in-home care, and assisting with finances and bills. They can support the primary caregiver nearby by arranging respite and staying in regular contact (NIA, What Is Long-Distance Caregiving?). Pair that with a few people near your parent who can check in for you (NIA).

  1. Pick the tasks that suit you from far away, such as paperwork, bills, research, or phone calls with services (NIA). If you share caregiving with siblings, see our guide to splitting caregiving responsibilities among siblings. It can help you divide the work.
  2. Build a small local team, with your parent's permission. Include a trusted neighbor and a nearby friend or relative. Also include someone from their faith group, club, or senior center. Also include a professional contact such as their doctor's office. NIA suggests asking people who see your parent to call you if they have concerns (NIA).
  3. Ask each person what they're comfortable doing. Examples are knocking on the door if your parent doesn't answer the phone, or giving a ride.
  4. Call the Eldercare Locator (eldercare.acl.gov, 1-800-677-1116) to reach your parent's Area Agency on Aging. That is the local public or nonprofit office for older-adult services (Eldercare Locator).

What are signs an elderly parent needs more help at home?

NIA lists signs such as trouble cooking safely, bathing, or dressing for the weather. Also look for a home that is no longer clean and free of clutter (NIA). Other signs are missed medications, weight gain or loss, and poor hygiene. Also watch for confusion, falls, trouble walking, loneliness, mood changes, and memory problems (NIA). These have many possible causes. Talk with your parent and, if needed, their doctor.

What to look at during a visit, based on NIA's list (NIA) (keep notes privately; you don't need to check every item):

OCC (Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, the U.S. agency that oversees national banks) describes pressure about money (OCC). FTC (Federal Trade Commission, the U.S. agency that protects buyers from scams and unfair business practices) warns about prize claims (FTC).

Area What to notice
Around the house A home that is no longer relatively clean and free of clutter
Kitchen and food Trouble preparing meals on the stove safely
Getting around Falls; trouble walking or getting around (see our room-by-room home safety checklist and talking with a parent about driving)
Daily routines and medicines Not bathing regularly or not wearing clothes suited to the weather; poor hygiene; not having the medications they need or not taking them regularly (don't change anything; note it for their doctor or pharmacist)
Money and scams Scam tactics to watch for: new "friends" pressuring your parent to make financial decisions or give access to their accounts, fake charities asking for donations, and fake lottery or sweepstakes claims (OCC); and a call, email, or letter saying your parent won a sweepstakes, lottery, or prize (FTC) (see when to act right away)
Mood and connection Confusion; loneliness or social isolation
  1. Look, don't inspect, using the table above while you go about the visit. NIA notes that changes at home can show an older adult needs extra support (NIA).
  2. Compare what you see with what you hear on the phone. Write down dates and specifics.
  3. Ask your parent directly how things are going and what would help. NIA says asking directly is often the best way to know (NIA).
  4. Suggest a visit to their health care provider if you notice health or memory changes. Offer to make the appointment, give a ride, or go along (NIA).

What should I do during and after a visit to my parent?

Plan a short list of tasks with your parent before you go. Look gently at daily life, and walk through the house for safety. Afterward, write up what you saw. Share it calmly with siblings. Choose one next step with your parent.

  1. Before the visit, ask your parent what they'd like help with. Book any appointments you want to attend. Check in with the local team.
  2. During the visit, do one or two planned tasks. Walk through the house with our home safety checklist. It follows NIA's room-by-room advice (NIA).
  3. Right after, write up your notes while they're fresh and share them with siblings. Stick to what you saw rather than conclusions.
  4. Choose one next step, such as regular grocery delivery, a doctor's appointment, or a home safety weekend. NIA suggests practical help like grocery delivery (NIA).
  5. Get documents in order with our documents checklist. Note the date for your next visit.

How do I bring up a concern without upsetting my parent?

Choose a quiet one-on-one moment. Mention your concern without sounding critical. Offer one specific kind of help that fits what your parent wants (NIA). Bigger discussions can wait for a calmer call later.

  1. Pick a private moment, such as a walk or washing dishes together. A drive works too. Do not pick a crowded meal.
  2. Ask, don't tell, for example: "What's the hardest part of the day right now?"
  3. Pick one thing and offer practical help that respects your parent's wishes (NIA).
  4. If your parent pushes back, see what to do if your parent refuses help.

When should I act right away?

Call 911 if your parent seems to be in immediate danger or has a medical emergency. If you suspect abuse, neglect, self-neglect, or financial exploitation such as a scam, contact your parent's local Adult Protective Services (NAPSA). NAPSA (National Adult Protective Services Association, a national group for the agencies that look into abuse and neglect of older and disabled adults) is the group behind the link above. Self-neglect is when a person can't meet their own basic needs. Adult Protective Services (APS) is the state or local agency that investigates these reports.

  1. Call 911 for immediate danger or a medical emergency.
  2. Find your parent's state APS number on NAPSA's Help in Your Area page. Or ask the Eldercare Locator (1-800-677-1116) where to turn.
  3. Call 911 right away for sudden confusion, trouble speaking, trouble walking, dizziness, or loss of balance. These can be signs of a stroke (a medical emergency that affects the brain). CDC (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, the U.S. government's main public health agency) lists them (CDC).
  4. If your parent says they feel hopeless or have no reason to live, seek help right away. Also seek help if you're worried they may harm themselves. Call or text the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. NIA lists it for this situation (NIA).

How often should I check in on a parent who lives alone?

There's no single right answer. A predictable rhythm makes it easier to notice when something is off. An example is a short call at the same time each day or every other day. NIA suggests checking in periodically and asking people nearby to call you with concerns (NIA).

  1. Pick a schedule your parent agrees to.
  2. Use video when you can. You can see things a voice won't tell you, like how the kitchen looks.
  3. Ask open questions, such as "What did you have for dinner?" rather than "Are you eating okay?"
  4. Follow up gently on changes, such as new confusion about dates or missed appointments. Also follow up on stories about scary moments. Coordinate with your local team between calls.

What information should I keep in one place?

With your parent's permission, keep one folder, paper or digital, for contacts, medications, insurance cards, and bills. Also note where legal papers such as the will, power of attorney, and advance directive are kept (NIA). A power of attorney is a document naming someone to make decisions for your parent. An advance directive is written wishes for medical care.

  1. List contacts: doctors, pharmacy, and dentist. Neighbors, friends, and local relatives. Faith community or club. Landlord, handyperson, or home care agency.
  2. Add everyday information. Include a current list of medications and doses, kept by your parent or their doctor's office. Include copies of insurance cards, front and back. Include regular bills, how they are paid, and utility accounts.
  3. Note where important documents are kept and who is named in each. Our documents checklist goes through them.
  4. Share where the folder is with your local team.

Can I talk to my parent's doctor if I'm not there?

Often, if your parent agrees, a provider can share information with a family member involved in your parent's care. Your parent must say it can, or does not object. This is under the HIPAA Privacy Rule (the federal rule that protects health information) (HHS). The rule does not require the provider to share unless you are your parent's personal representative. A personal representative is someone with legal authority to make health decisions for them (HHS).

  1. Ask your parent to tell the doctor's office that it can talk with you.
  2. Ask the office what form or authorization your parent needs to sign.
  3. Medicare is U.S. government health insurance for older people and some younger people with disabilities. Original Medicare is regular Medicare. A Medicare Advantage plan is a Medicare-approved plan from a private company. Medicaid is a joint federal and state program that helps with health costs for people with limited income and resources. For Medicare calls, your parent can name you on Medicare's own permission form. See the Medicare permission form.
  4. Plan for a time your parent can't decide. A durable power of attorney for health care names a person to make health decisions for them (NIA).

What is a geriatric care manager, and how do I find one?

A geriatric care manager is a trained professional. They can help find services and make a care plan. They are sometimes called an aging life care professional. NIA says they can be especially helpful when family members live far apart (NIA). Your parent's doctor or the Aging Life Care Association can help you find one (NIA).

  1. Ask your parent's doctor for names (NIA).
  2. Search the Aging Life Care Association directory.
  3. Before you hire anyone, ask about fees and what services they provide (ALCA).

What local services can fill the gaps I can't cover?

Your parent's Area Agency on Aging may have lists of local services. NIA describes help such as meals, transportation, volunteer visits, adult day care, and respite care (NIA). Adult day care is daytime activities, meals, and personal care outside the home. Respite care is short-term care when the regular caregiver isn't available. Reach the agency through the Eldercare Locator at 1-800-677-1116 (Eldercare Locator).

  1. Call the Eldercare Locator and ask for your parent's Area Agency on Aging.
  2. Start with the biggest gap, such as meals or rides to appointments. A home care aide for a few hours a week is another example. See trying in-home care first.
  3. Check the budget with our guide to what care costs.
  4. Plan breaks for whoever is nearby using our guide to finding respite care.

What should our emergency plan include?

Decide now who nearby gets called first and who has a spare key. Also decide who can get there quickly. Also decide where the information folder is. NIA advises that someone your parent trusts should know where to find important papers in an emergency (NIA). Write it down. Share it with your parent and local team. In an emergency, call 911 first.

  1. Name the first person to call nearby and a backup. With your parent's permission, ask neighbors, friends, or local relatives to call you if they have concerns (NIA).
  2. Confirm who has a spare key and who can get there within an hour.
  3. Decide how quickly you can travel. Decide who covers your work or family while you're gone. See taking leave from work.
  4. Share the written plan with your parent and local team.
  5. Know what the hospital can tell you. If your parent is hurt and can't give permission, a provider may share information. They may share it with family. Sharing is allowed if it seems to be in your parent's best interest (HHS). See talking to your parent's doctor.
