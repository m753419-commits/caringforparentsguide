Hospital Discharge and Medicare Home Health: What to Ask Before Your Parent Leaves

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 3, 2026

US programs only.

Please note: This post is general information, not legal, financial, or medical advice.

Before your parent leaves the hospital, confirm the details with the hospital, Medicare.gov, or 1-800-MEDICARE (Medicare's official phone line, 1-800-633-4227) (full disclaimer).

What should we ask before my parent is discharged from the hospital?

Ask about the discharge plan before your parent leaves: Medicare's discharge checklist treats both the patient and their caregiver as important members of the planning team, so ask where your parent will get care after discharge, such as home health care (medical care like nursing or therapy given at home) (Medicare.gov checklist).

  1. Use Medicare's discharge planning checklist (publication 11376) during the stay (Medicare.gov).
  2. Ask whether your parent is an inpatient (formally admitted by a doctor's order) or an outpatient under observation (being watched in the hospital while the doctor decides whether to admit them) (see the next question) (Medicare.gov).
  3. Ask for the list of care providers. When follow-up care is appropriate, the hospital must include in the discharge plan a list of Medicare-participating home health agencies, SNFs (skilled nursing facilities: places that give short-term nursing and rehab care), and similar providers that serve your parent's area (42 CFR 482.43).
  4. Say which provider you prefer. The hospital must tell your parent or their representative about the freedom to choose among participating Medicare providers and must not limit the choices (42 CFR 482.43).
  5. Read Medicare's hospital benefits booklet for more on hospital stays (Medicare.gov, publication 11435).

Is my parent an inpatient or under observation, and why does it matter?

It matters for costs: Observation services are hospital outpatient services your parent gets while the doctor decides whether to admit them as an inpatient or discharge them, and Part B (Medicare's medical insurance for doctor and outpatient care) pays for them, not Part A (Medicare's hospital insurance) (Medicare.gov).

  1. Ask the status each day. Medicare.gov says your parent is an outpatient even after a night in the hospital if the doctor hasn't written an order to admit them (Medicare.gov).
  2. Look for the MOON (Medicare Outpatient Observation Notice: a form telling your parent they are an outpatient under observation). Hospitals must give it to people getting observation services for more than 24 hours, no later than 36 hours after observation begins (CMS (the federal agency that runs Medicare); 42 CFR 405.1205; CMS Medicare Claims Processing Manual, Chapter 30; Medicare.gov).
  3. Ask about drugs. Medicare.gov says Part B generally doesn't cover prescription and over-the-counter drugs you get in an outpatient setting, sometimes called "self-administered drugs" (Medicare.gov).
  4. Know the SNF effect. Time under observation doesn't count toward the 3-day inpatient stay Original Medicare (regular Medicare) requires before it covers SNF care; see our cost guide (Medicare.gov).
  5. If your parent was admitted and then switched to observation: in Original Medicare, people hospitalized on or after February 14, 2025 can ask for a fast appeal (a quick review by an independent reviewer) and should get a Medicare Change of Status Notice; if your parent has a Medicare Advantage Plan (a Medicare-approved plan from a private company), ask the plan or call 1-800-MEDICARE (Medicare.gov).

Will Medicare pay for a skilled nursing facility after the hospital?

Possibly: Original Medicare covers SNF care after a medically necessary inpatient stay of at least 3 days in a row, not counting the day your parent leaves the hospital (Medicare.gov).

  1. Count only inpatient days. Time under observation or in the emergency room before admission doesn't count, even overnight (Medicare.gov).
  2. Know the 2026 costs: days 1–20 cost $0 each day after the $1,736 deductible (the amount paid before Medicare starts to pay); days 21–100 cost $217 each day; and from day 101 your parent pays all costs (Medicare.gov).
  3. Understand the benefit period (the way Medicare measures use of hospital and SNF care): it starts the day your parent starts inpatient hospital or SNF care and ends after 60 days in a row without SNF care, with up to 100 SNF days in each benefit period (Medicare.gov, publication 10153; Medicare.gov).
  4. If your parent has a Medicare Advantage Plan, ask the plan: Medicare.gov says these plans may waive the 3-day minimum (Medicare.gov).

What if we think the hospital is discharging my parent too soon?

Ask for a fast appeal: Follow the directions on the Important Message from Medicare (a notice of your parent's rights in the hospital) no later than the day your parent is scheduled to be discharged, and your parent can stay while waiting for the decision without paying for the stay (except for applicable coinsurance, a percentage share of the cost, or deductibles) (Medicare.gov).

  1. Find the Important Message from Medicare. Hospitals must give it within 2 calendar days of admission and again no more than 2 calendar days before discharge (Medicare.gov; CMS; 42 CFR 405.1205; CMS Medicare Claims Processing Manual, Chapter 30).
  2. Call the BFCC-QIO (Beneficiary and Family Centered Care Quality Improvement Organization: the independent reviewer Medicare uses for these appeals). Commence or Acentra runs it, depending on the state; find yours in the CMS BFCC directory (Medicare.gov; CMS).
  3. Expect a quick decision. In a hospital, the BFCC-QIO decides within one day of getting the information it asked for (Medicare.gov).
  4. If you missed the deadline, you can still ask the BFCC-QIO to review the case, but different rules apply and your parent might have to pay for days past the original discharge date; Medicare Advantage members can ask their plan for an appeal (Medicare.gov).

Does my parent qualify for Medicare home health care?

Possibly: Your parent must need part-time or intermittent skilled services (care that needs a nurse or therapist) and be homebound (unable to leave home without great effort), and a doctor or other allowed provider must see them in person, confirm the need, and order the care from a Medicare-certified (approved by Medicare) home health agency (Medicare.gov).

  1. Check homebound status: leaving home isn't recommended or needs help (like a cane, wheelchair, walker, special transportation, or another person), and your parent is normally unable to leave home and leaving takes a lot of effort; short, infrequent outings such as medical care, religious services, or adult day care are allowed (Medicare.gov).
  2. Confirm the face-to-face visit (an in-person or telehealth visit, meaning by video or other technology, about the main reason for home care): it must happen no more than 90 days before or within 30 days after home health care starts (42 CFR 424.22).
  3. Make sure there is a plan of care (a written plan from the doctor listing the services your parent needs) (42 CFR 409.42).
  4. Don't wait for a hospital stay. Medicare's home health eligibility list doesn't include a prior hospital stay (Medicare.gov).
  5. Choose an agency. Compare agencies on Care Compare (Medicare's tool for comparing providers); your choice should be honored, but a Medicare Advantage Plan may require agencies it contracts with (Medicare.gov, publication 10969).

What does Medicare home health cover, and what does it cost?

Your parent pays $0 for covered home health services: After the Part B deductible ($283 in 2026), they pay 20% of the Medicare-approved amount (the amount Medicare agrees to pay for a service) for medical equipment (Medicare.gov; Medicare.gov).

  1. Covered: part-time or intermittent skilled nursing care; physical, occupational, and speech-language therapy (if conditions are met); medical social services; and home health aide care (help with bathing, walking, and similar tasks) only when your parent also gets skilled care (Medicare.gov).
  2. Not covered: 24-hour care at home, home meal delivery, homemaker services unrelated to the care plan, and custodial care (help with daily activities like bathing or dressing) when that is the only care needed; see also our guide to home health aides (Medicare.gov).
  3. How much care: in most cases, skilled nursing and aide services up to 8 hours a day combined, for a maximum of 28 hours a week (Medicare.gov).
  4. Watch for notices. The agency should give an ABN (Advance Beneficiary Notice: a form saying Medicare may not pay for a service) before services Medicare doesn't cover, and a Home Health Change of Care Notice when the plan of care changes (Medicare.gov; CMS).
  5. Family caregivers: Part B covers caregiver training services if certain conditions apply (Medicare.gov).

What if home health or nursing facility care is ending too soon?

Appeal quickly: Follow the instructions on the Notice of Medicare Non-Coverage no later than noon the day before the termination date listed on the notice (Medicare.gov).

  1. Look for the notice. Home health agencies, SNFs, hospices (care for people who are terminally ill), and CORFs (outpatient rehabilitation facilities) must give a Notice of Medicare Non-Coverage when covered services are ending (CMS).
  2. Call the BFCC-QIO listed on the notice, or find it in the CMS BFCC directory.
  3. Keep notes of dates, names, and what you were told, and call 1-800-MEDICARE (TTY, a text phone line for people who are deaf or hard of hearing: 1-877-486-2048) with your parent if you need help (Medicare.gov).
  4. Plan a backup. Read our respite care guide and in-home care vs. assisted living in case Medicare coverage ends.
