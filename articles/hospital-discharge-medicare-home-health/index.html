Hospital Discharge and Medicare Home Health: What to Ask Before Your Parent Leaves

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 7, 2026

US programs only.

Please note: This post is general information, not legal, financial, or medical advice.

Before your parent leaves the hospital, check the details with the hospital, Medicare.gov, or 1-800-MEDICARE. That is Medicare's official phone line, 1-800-633-4227 (full disclaimer).

In plain words: This guide is about leaving the hospital and getting care at home. Medicare is U.S. government health insurance for older people and some younger people with disabilities. What it pays for depends on a few rules, and you have the right to appeal. First, ask the hospital about the plan for care after your parent leaves.

What should we ask before my parent is discharged from the hospital?

Before your parent leaves, ask where they will get care next. Home health care (nursing or therapy given at home) is one option (Medicare.gov checklist).

  1. Use Medicare's discharge planning checklist (publication 11376) during the stay. It treats your parent and you, the caregiver, as key members of the planning team (Medicare.gov).
  2. Ask whether your parent is an inpatient or an outpatient under observation. An inpatient was admitted by a doctor's formal order. Under observation means the hospital is watching them. The doctor is still deciding whether to admit them. See the next question (Medicare.gov).
  3. Ask for the list of care providers. When follow-up care fits, the hospital must put a list in the discharge plan. It lists Medicare-participating home health agencies and SNFs (skilled nursing facilities: places that give short-term nursing and rehab care) that serve your parent's area. It also lists similar providers. (42 CFR 482.43).
  4. Say which provider you prefer. The hospital must tell your parent or their representative they are free to choose. They can choose among participating Medicare providers. The hospital must not limit the choices (42 CFR 482.43).
  5. Read Medicare's hospital benefits booklet for more on hospital stays (Medicare.gov, publication 11435).

Is my parent an inpatient or under observation, and why does it matter?

It matters for costs: Part B (Medicare's medical insurance for doctor and outpatient care) pays for observation. Part A (hospital insurance) does not, because observation is outpatient care (Medicare.gov).

  1. Ask the status each day. Observation means your parent gets outpatient care for now. Meanwhile, the doctor decides whether to admit them or send them home. Medicare.gov says your parent is still an outpatient. That lasts until the doctor writes an order to admit them. This is true even after a night in the hospital (Medicare.gov).
  2. Look for the MOON (Medicare Outpatient Observation Notice). This form tells your parent they are an outpatient under observation. Hospitals must give it to people under observation for more than 24 hours. They must give it no later than 36 hours after observation begins (CMS (the federal agency that runs Medicare); CMS Medicare Claims Processing Manual, Chapter 30; Medicare.gov).
  3. Ask about drugs. Medicare.gov says Part B generally doesn't cover drugs you get in an outpatient setting. This means prescription and over-the-counter drugs. They are sometimes called "self-administered drugs" (Medicare.gov).
  4. Know the SNF effect. Original Medicare (regular Medicare) requires a 3-day inpatient stay before it covers SNF care. Time under observation doesn't count toward it. See our cost guide (Medicare.gov).
  5. If admitted, then switched to observation: In Original Medicare, people hospitalized on or after February 14, 2025, can ask for a fast appeal (a quick review by an outside reviewer). They should get a Medicare Change of Status Notice. A Medicare Advantage Plan is a Medicare-approved plan from a private company. If your parent has one, ask the plan or call 1-800-MEDICARE (Medicare.gov).

Will Medicare pay for a skilled nursing facility after the hospital?

Maybe. Original Medicare covers SNF care after a medically needed inpatient stay of at least 3 days in a row (Medicare.gov).

  1. Count only inpatient days. The day your parent leaves doesn't count. Time under observation doesn't count. Time in the emergency room before admission doesn't count either, even overnight (Medicare.gov).
  2. Know the 2026 costs: Days 1–20 cost $0 each day after the $1,736 deductible. A deductible is the amount paid before Medicare starts to pay. Days 21–100 cost $217 each day. From day 101, your parent pays all costs (Medicare.gov).
  3. Learn the benefit period. This is how Medicare counts use of hospital and SNF care. It starts the day your parent starts inpatient hospital or SNF care. It ends after 60 days in a row without inpatient hospital or SNF care. Each benefit period has up to 100 SNF days (Medicare.gov, publication 10153; Medicare.gov).
  4. If your parent has a Medicare Advantage Plan, ask the plan. Medicare.gov says these plans may waive the 3-day minimum (Medicare.gov).

What if we think the hospital is discharging my parent too soon?

Ask for a fast appeal by the day your parent is set to leave. While waiting, your parent can stay without paying for the stay, except coinsurance (your share of the cost) or deductibles (Medicare.gov).

  1. Find the Important Message from Medicare. This notice tells your parent their rights in the hospital. Follow its steps to appeal. Hospitals must give the notice within 2 calendar days of admission. They must give it again no more than 2 calendar days before discharge (Medicare.gov; CMS; 42 CFR 405.1205; CMS Medicare Claims Processing Manual, Chapter 30).
  2. Call the BFCC-QIO. That stands for Beneficiary and Family Centered Care Quality Improvement Organization. It is the outside reviewer Medicare uses for these appeals. Commence or Acentra runs it, depending on the state. Find the one for your parent's state in the CMS BFCC directory (Medicare.gov; CMS).
  3. Expect a quick decision. In a hospital, the BFCC-QIO decides within one day of getting the facts it asked for (Medicare.gov).
  4. If you missed the deadline, you can still ask the BFCC-QIO to review the case. But different rules apply. Your parent might have to pay for days past the first discharge date. Medicare Advantage members can ask their plan for an appeal (Medicare.gov).

Does my parent qualify for Medicare home health care?

Maybe, if your parent is homebound (can't leave home without great effort) and needs skilled care (care that needs a nurse or therapist) part-time or now and then. A doctor must see them and order the care (Medicare.gov).

  1. Know the rules. A doctor or other allowed provider must see your parent in person and confirm the need. They must order the care from a Medicare-certified (approved by Medicare) home health agency.
  2. Check homebound status: Leaving home isn't advised, or needs help. Help can mean a cane, wheelchair, walker, special rides, or another person. Your parent is normally unable to leave home, and leaving takes a lot of effort. Short, rare outings are allowed. Examples are medical care, religious services, or adult day care (Medicare.gov).
  3. Check the face-to-face visit. This is a visit about the main reason for home care. It can be in person or by telehealth (video or other technology). It must happen no more than 90 days before home health care starts. Or it can be within 30 days after it starts (42 CFR 424.22). Also make sure there is a plan of care. This is a written plan from the doctor that lists the services your parent needs (42 CFR 409.42).
  4. Don't wait for a hospital stay. Medicare's home health list of rules doesn't include a past hospital stay (Medicare.gov).
  5. Choose an agency. Compare agencies on Care Compare. That is Medicare's tool for comparing providers. Your choice should be honored. But a Medicare Advantage Plan may require agencies it has contracts with (Medicare.gov, publication 10969).

What does Medicare home health cover, and what does it cost?

Your parent pays $0 for covered home health services. After the Part B deductible ($283 in 2026), they pay 20% of the Medicare-approved amount (what Medicare agrees to pay for a service) for medical equipment (Medicare.gov; Medicare.gov).

  1. Covered: Medicare covers part-time or now-and-then skilled nursing care. It covers physical, occupational, and speech therapy if the rules are met. It covers medical social services. It covers home health aide care only when your parent also gets skilled care. Aide care is help with bathing, walking, and similar tasks (Medicare.gov).
  2. Not covered: Care at home 24 hours a day is not covered. Meal delivery is not covered either. Neither are homemaker services not tied to the care plan. Custodial care is help with daily tasks like bathing or dressing. It is not covered if it is the only care needed. See also our guide to home health aides (Medicare.gov).
  3. How much care: Skilled nursing and aide services can be combined. Together they can most often be up to 8 hours a day. The most is 28 hours a week (Medicare.gov).
  4. Watch for notices. Before services Medicare doesn't cover, the agency should give an ABN (Advance Beneficiary Notice). That form says Medicare may not pay for a service. When the plan of care changes, the agency should give a Home Health Change of Care Notice (Medicare.gov; CMS).
  5. Family caregivers: Part B covers training for caregivers if certain conditions apply (Medicare.gov).

What if home health or nursing facility care is ending too soon?

Appeal fast by following the steps on the Notice of Medicare Non-Coverage. Do it by noon the day before the end date on it (Medicare.gov).

  1. Look for the notice. Some providers must give a Notice of Medicare Non-Coverage when covered services are ending. These are home health agencies, SNFs, hospices, and CORFs. Hospices care for people who are terminally ill. CORFs are outpatient rehab facilities (CMS).
  2. Call the BFCC-QIO listed on the notice, or find the one for your parent's state in the CMS BFCC directory.
  3. Keep notes of dates, names, and what you were told. If you need help, call 1-800-MEDICARE with your parent. TTY is a text phone line. It is for people who are deaf or hard of hearing. The TTY number is 1-877-486-2048 (Medicare.gov).
  4. Plan a backup. If Medicare coverage ends, read our respite care guide and in-home care vs. assisted living.
