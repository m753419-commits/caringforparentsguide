Nursing Home Comparison and Visit Checklist

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 7, 2026

US programs only.

In plain words: This guide helps you compare nursing homes and plan a visit. A star rating is a clue, not a promise that a home fits your parent. Look at inspections, staffing, and the visit itself. Start with homes near your parent.

This is general information, not legal, financial, or medical advice. It does not rank homes or name a home to pick.

Ask each home for its written fees and its latest inspection report. Confirm coverage with the program or the plan (full disclaimer).

Medicare is U.S. government health insurance for older people and some younger people with disabilities.

Medicaid (a joint federal and state program that helps with health costs for people with limited income and resources) is a different program.

A hard choice is normal. Compare first, then visit.

Where should I start?

Look up nursing homes near your parent, compare them, and visit before you choose. Do not stop at the overall star.

  1. Ask people you trust, your parent's doctor, and the hospital discharge planner what they know.
  2. Care Compare (Medicare's tool for comparing providers) is one place to search.
  3. Compare health inspection results, staffing, and quality measures. Do not keep only the overall star.
  4. Visit the homes you are considering, or ask someone to visit for you. Use a new checklist for each home. Do not add your own score and call it a government rank.
  5. This research read the choosing page, the ratings page, the September 2026 guide, and the checklist without an account. It saw only the Care Compare page shell, not a home list. That does not mean there are no homes. It also does not mean the page was missing.

What should I do with the star ratings?

Stars run from 1 to 5. They are a quality comparison. In plain terms, they do not promise a fit for your parent, no problems, or that payment is certain.

  1. There is one overall star rating, plus separate ratings. The separate ratings are health inspections, staffing, and quality measures.
  2. CMS (the federal agency that runs Medicare) says no rating system can cover every important point. Read the September 2026 CMS guide. Use the stars with a visit and with other facts.
  3. A closer home may be a better choice than a higher-rated home that is far away. Family visits matter. Special rehab or dementia (a loss of memory and thinking that gets in the way of daily life) care matters too.
  4. The September 2026 guide says high-performing homes will have a trophy icon, besides the usual stars. The icon is not a promise that the home fits your parent. This guide did not open a home's page to check the icon.
  5. The same guide describes a July 2026 change to quality measure star cut points. Each cut point rose by one-half of the average improvement in those scores. A star change across periods may come from a method change. Do not assume the home got better or worse from the stars alone.

The September 2026 guide also revises the long-stay antipsychotic medication measure in its January 2026 and April 2026 notes. The notes cover the data used and who is left out. Write down the data date when you compare. Do not treat numbers from different dates as the same current fact. This guide does not judge whether a drug is right for your parent.

How do the September 2026 inspection ratings work?

The September 2026 CMS guide uses the two most recent standard health inspection surveys. Those are the two most recent standard inspections. Do not use the older three-survey method.

  1. The same guide says this two-survey method began with the July 2025 refresh. It replaced the three most recent standard surveys.
  2. Also count complaint surveys from the most recent 36 months. Also count focused infection control surveys (surveys that look closely at infection control) from those 36 months.
  3. The rating also counts revisits (follow-up inspections) used to confirm that problems were fixed. Points reflect the number, scope (how widespread a problem is), and severity (how serious it is) of deficiencies.
  4. Do not stop at whether the home passed. Ask what the problems were and whether each one was fixed.

What does the staffing rating show?

The September 2026 guide uses six staffing measures. Three are nurse staffing hours, and three are turnover. The hours are not a promise of who is on duty today.

  1. The hours are case-mix adjusted, which means adjusted for how much care the residents need. The hours cover a single quarter.
  2. One hour measure is total nurse hours per resident per day. One is RN (registered nurse) hours per resident per day, and those hours do not include licensed practical nurses. One is total nurse hours on weekends. Weekend hours are averaged across Saturdays and Sundays.
  3. Turnover covers total nursing staff, RNs, and the nursing home administrator. Annual turnover uses six quarters in a row of PBJ (Payroll-Based Journal, staffing reported from payroll records) data. It is not a same-day head count.
  4. On the visit, ask who is there during the day, at night, and on weekends. Also ask whether the same staff give the care. A high staffing star is not a promise that someone will come at once at night.
  5. These hours are for the star rating. This guide did not verify a national minimum staffing law. Do not read the hours as a legal minimum. There is no single 2026 admission price in these sources. There is no single wait time either.

Where can I read inspection reports?

Ask the home to show its most recent state or federal survey report in a public place. You can also use Care Compare, and most state survey agency sites have older reports.

  1. The checklist that says this is CMS-12130, dated November 2022. Medicare.gov still offers that checklist. It is not a 2026 edition.
  2. Certified means a state agency inspected the home and it passed. Ask if the home is Medicare-certified and Medicaid-certified. Ask which state agency licensed the home and the administrator.
  3. Certification is not a promise that Medicare or Medicaid will pay the whole long stay. Medicare and Medicaid cover care only from certified homes, and that is not the same as paying every long-term cost.
  4. This guide did not open a real home's report. Ask the home, and read the report yourself.

What should I ask on a visit?

Use one blank checklist per home, and write the answers, what you see, the date, and what is still open. The questions below follow the November 2022 checklist and the choosing page.

  1. Ask if there is an opening that fits your parent. Ask for a written fee schedule. See what the monthly fee includes, and what costs extra.
  2. Ask which care problems were on the last state inspection. Ask whether each one was corrected, and how the home can show that. Ask what therapy is offered, and whether therapy staff are available every day. Ask if your parent can keep their own doctor, and how rides to that doctor work.
  3. Ask how the home prevents and reports abuse and neglect, and how it responds when a family reports a concern. Notice whether residents are clean and dressed for the time of day. Notice whether staff are warm, polite, and respectful, and notice odor, light, and noise.
  4. Ask which nurses and aides can help during the day, at night, and on weekends. Ask where that staffing list is posted. Ask if staff speak your parent's main language. If not, ask whether an interpreter is available.
  5. Ask if the home can meet a special diet. Ask if someone can help your parent eat and drink when needed. Ask if your parent can choose when to get up, sleep, and bathe. Ask about extra dementia training. Ask if non-medication approaches are the first attempt for behavior symptoms. Ask about staff-to-resident ratios for dementia care, and about safe time outdoors.

Ask if the home is close enough for family to visit often. Ask how visitors get in early or late, after the building locks at night. Ask how residents who have trouble moving can join activities.

Ask how families learn the emergency plan, which hospital the home works with, and which systems the backup generator runs.

Does a high star rating decide it?

No. CMS says no rating can cover every important point. A brochure is not enough either.

  1. Use the stars with a visit, your parent's care needs, and how easy it is for family to visit.
  2. A high staffing star uses quarterly hours and turnover. It is not a promise about a night response. This guide did not find a response time for any one home.
  3. Ask what therapy is actually offered, whether therapy staff are there every day, and how dementia care is given. This guide cannot say a named home meets your parent's medical needs. Confirm that with the medical team.
  4. There is no national 2026 admission price in these sources. Get each home's written fee list. Do not infer all long-term costs from a Medicare certification.
