Sharing Care with Siblings

How to Split Caregiving Responsibilities Fairly Among Siblings

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 7, 2026

This guide is written for families in the United States. Where it mentions FMLA or Medicaid, it means the US law and program. FMLA is the Family and Medical Leave Act. The leave is unpaid, but your job is protected if you qualify. Medicaid is a joint federal and state program that helps with health costs for people with limited income and resources.

In plain words: Fair care among siblings does not have to mean the same hours. List the tasks, then let each person take what fits. Write down who does what and how money works. Start with one family meeting that includes your parent when you can.

How do we split caregiving fairly among siblings?

Fair doesn't have to mean equal hours. List every task. Then let each sibling take what fits their skills, distance, and time. The National Institute on Aging (NIA, a federal research agency) suggests making a list of what needs doing, considering each person's skills and interests, and letting people choose (NIA).

  1. List every task by area: health (appointments, medications, insurance questions). Daily care (meals, bathing, housekeeping). Money (bills, watching for scams, benefits paperwork, taxes). Home (repairs, safety changes, technology). Transportation. Companionship. Coordination. Also cover backup when the main caregiver is sick or away.
  2. Ask each sibling three questions. What am I good at? What can I reliably do, given where I live and what my life is like now? What will I not be able to do?
  3. Give every task a lead, a backup, and a check-in date.
  4. Ask your parent what they want before deciding what they need. NIA says asking directly is the best way to know (NIA).
  5. Keep contributions visible with a shared calendar and a short weekly update. Also use a shared care notebook or document.

How do we run a family meeting about our parent's care?

Include your parent when possible. Send an agenda and the task list ahead of time. Use video so faraway siblings can join. End with names and dates. If things are tense, ask a neutral facilitator. A trusted relative, a faith leader, a social worker, or a geriatric care manager can do this. A geriatric care manager is a professional who helps plan and coordinate care.

  1. Send the agenda and task list before the meeting.
  2. Invite your parent, since it's their life.
  3. Find a facilitator if needed. The Aging Life Care Association lists professional geriatric care managers. NIA also points to them (NIA).
  4. Stick to the present and near future. Leave old family history for another time.
  5. End with a lead, a backup, and a check-in date for every task.

How can a sibling who lives far away help?

They can do a lot. NIA says they can take on specific tasks (NIA). This is for family members who aren't the main caregiver. NIA says they can also stay in contact or arrange regular respite (NIA). Respite is short-term care that gives the main caregiver a break. NIA says they can also travel to stay with the parent for a few days (NIA). That gives the main caregiver time off. Ask the main caregiver what would help most (NIA).

  1. Handle research, insurance, billing, and benefits calls during business hours.
  2. Join medical appointments by phone or video, with your parent's permission.
  3. Pay for or arrange services that lighten the local sibling's load. Examples include respite from a volunteer or an in-home aide. An adult day program is another example (NIA).
  4. Plan visits that give the main caregiver real time off (NIA). See our long-distance caregiving guide.

How should we handle money, and should the sibling who does the most care get paid?

Talk about it openly. Decide who pays for what. Track spending. Make sure whoever handles money has legal authority. Some families decide to pay the sibling who does the most hands-on care. If you do, put it in writing. It may affect taxes and benefits. Check with a tax professional or an elder law attorney before paying a family member. An elder law attorney (a lawyer who helps older adults and their families interpret state laws, plan how wishes will be carried out, and understand financial options) can help.

  1. Decide who pays for what. The money can be your parent's own money, siblings' contributions, or both.
  2. Before paying a sibling for care, ask an elder law attorney. Ask about a written caregiver agreement. See getting paid to care for a parent. Medicaid is a joint federal and state program that helps with health costs for people with limited income and resources. Rules for these agreements under Medicaid vary by state. For example, Nevada's Medicaid manual has a timing rule. It requires the written agreement to be signed before the care is given. North Carolina's Medicaid manual requires the same. Both treat payment for care given in the past as a transfer of assets. A transfer of assets is money given away without fair value in return (Nevada Medicaid manual, section F-415.23, MTL 04/23; North Carolina Medicaid manual MA-2240, revised September 1, 2025).
  3. Talk with a financial professional. NIA notes that some ways of paying for care can affect taxes and inheritance (NIA).
  4. Make sure the sibling handling money has authority. One example is a durable power of attorney for finances. That document names someone to make financial decisions when your parent can't (NIA).

How do we avoid disputes over our parent's money?

Keep records everyone can see. Act only within the authority you legally have. One example is a durable power of attorney for finances (NIA). If a sibling manages a parent's Social Security benefits (monthly payments from the U.S. government to retired workers, people with disabilities, and some of their family members), check one point. Check whether Social Security (the federal agency that pays retirement and disability benefits) requires a representative payee. A representative payee is a person or organization it appoints to manage benefits. See power of attorney and Social Security.

  1. Keep a shared spreadsheet of expenses with receipts.
  2. Agree on who can spend what without checking with the others. Write it in your family care agreement.
  3. Keep your parent's legal documents up to date with our documents checklist.
  4. If you suspect financial exploitation, contact your parent's local Adult Protective Services. That is the state or local agency that investigates abuse and exploitation of older adults. Use NAPSA's Help in Your Area page (NAPSA). NAPSA (National Adult Protective Services Association, a national group for the agencies that look into abuse and neglect of older and disabled adults) lists local offices.
  5. If a sibling becomes the representative payee, Social Security can ask them to complete an annual report. The Representative Payee Report accounts for the benefits. Under a 2018 law, Public Law 115-165, some payees are exempt. SSA no longer requires that report from spouses. It also exempts certain parents and legal guardians who live with the beneficiary. Some state mental institutions are exempt too. Exempt payees can still have site reviews. A site review is a review in which Social Security checks how a payee is managing the benefits (SSA payee FAQ; SSA POMS GN 00605.015, effective May 26, 2023).

What goes in a family care agreement?

A one- or two-page summary that everyone can see can show each person's responsibilities. It can also show how you'll communicate and how money works. It can show what happens in an emergency. It can show when you'll review the plan. It doesn't need to be a legal document. If it includes paying a family member for care, rules differ by state. Check with your parent's state Medicaid office before signing or paying (Medicaid.gov state contact list). Or check with an elder law attorney before signing or paying.

  1. List each person's responsibilities, with a lead and a backup for every area.
  2. Set how you'll communicate. Use a weekly group text, a shared document, or a monthly video call.
  3. Write down how money works. Note what your parent pays, what siblings contribute, and how receipts are shared. Before anyone signs or is paid for care, contact your parent's state Medicaid office. Use the Medicaid.gov state contact list.
  4. Decide what happens in an emergency. Decide who gets called first, who travels, and who calls the others.
  5. Put the next review date on the calendar. Send the agreement to everyone, including your parent if they want a copy.

What if one sibling won't help?

You can't force them. You can ask for something specific and small. Accept that some people will say no, as NIA advises (NIA). Then look for help elsewhere, including local services through the Eldercare Locator (Eldercare Locator).

  1. Ask for one specific task, such as "Could you call Dad every Tuesday?"
  2. Offer options: time, money, or a skill.
  3. Look for other help from friends, neighbors, faith communities, or paid help. Also use local services through the Eldercare Locator (eldercare.acl.gov, 1-800-677-1116).
  4. Consider elder mediation if conflict is affecting your parent's care. A mediator helps families make eldercare decisions together. A mediator is a neutral third party. This person helps people talk through a disagreement (ABA).

What if our parent wants only one of us involved?

Respect your parent's wishes as much as possible. This is especially true about who receives medical information. Under the HIPAA Privacy Rule, providers may share some information. They may share with family involved in care if your parent agrees. They may also share if your parent doesn't object. The HIPAA Privacy Rule is the federal rule that protects health information. They are not required to share otherwise. This changes if the family member is a personal representative. A personal representative is someone with legal authority. That person can make health decisions for them (HHS). You can still support the chosen sibling in other ways.

  1. Ask your parent who they want to share medical information with. Let the doctor's office know.
  2. Support the chosen sibling with emotional support. Also take on specific tasks, as NIA suggests for anyone who isn't the main caregiver.
  3. Agree on how updates will reach the rest of the family.

What if we just can't agree?

Bring in a neutral mediator. Elder mediation is a process that helps families work through eldercare decisions (ABA). Keep the focus on your parent's needs and wishes.

  1. Write down each sibling's main concern. Also write what your parent has said they want.
  2. Consider an elder mediator; elder mediation helps families make eldercare decisions together (ABA).
  3. Keep each discussion focused on your parent's needs and wishes, not past disagreements.

How often should we revisit the plan?

Review the plan every few months. Also review it after any big change such as a hospital stay, a move, or a new diagnosis (NIA). NIA advises being realistic and planning to revisit care decisions as needs change (NIA). Don't wait for the scheduled review if the main caregiver shows signs such as exhaustion, impatience, or trouble sleeping (NIA).

  1. Put the next review on the calendar now.
  2. Watch for warning signs: one person quietly picking up others' tasks. The main caregiver skipping their own appointments, losing sleep, or sounding short-tempered (signs of caregiver stress in NIA's list, NIA; see caregiver burnout warning signs). Updates stopping. Or your parent's needs changing while the plan hasn't.
  3. Call a short family meeting. Ask: "Who is carrying too much right now?"
  4. Update your written family care agreement.
