How to Split Caregiving Responsibilities Fairly Among Siblings

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 3, 2026

This guide is written for families in the United States; where it mentions FMLA or Medicaid, it means the US law and program.

Please note: This post is general information, not legal, financial, or medical advice.

How do we split caregiving fairly among siblings?

Fair doesn't have to mean equal hours: list every task, then let each sibling take what fits their skills, distance, and time. The National Institute on Aging (NIA) suggests making a list of what needs doing, considering each person's skills and interests, and letting people choose (NIA).

  1. List every task by area: health (appointments, medications, insurance questions); daily care (meals, bathing, housekeeping); money (bills, watching for scams, benefits paperwork, taxes); home (repairs, safety changes, technology); transportation; companionship; coordination; and backup when the main caregiver is sick or away.
  2. Ask each sibling three questions: What am I good at? What can I reliably do, given where I live and what my life is like now? What will I not be able to do?
  3. Give every task a lead, a backup, and a check-in date.
  4. Ask your parent what they want before deciding what they need; NIA says asking directly is the best way to know (NIA).
  5. Keep contributions visible with a shared calendar, a short weekly update, and a shared care notebook or document.

How do we run a family meeting about our parent's care?

Include your parent when possible, send an agenda and the task list ahead of time, use video so faraway siblings can join, and end with names and dates. If things are tense, ask a neutral facilitator, such as a trusted relative, a faith leader, a social worker, or a geriatric care manager (a professional who helps plan and coordinate care).

  1. Send the agenda and task list before the meeting.
  2. Invite your parent, since it's their life.
  3. Find a facilitator if needed; the Aging Life Care Association lists professional geriatric care managers, which NIA also points to (NIA).
  4. Stick to the present and near future, and leave old family history for another time.
  5. End with a lead, a backup, and a check-in date for every task.

How can a sibling who lives far away help?

A lot: NIA says family members who aren't the main caregiver can take on specific tasks, stay in contact, arrange regular respite (short-term care that gives the main caregiver a break), or travel to stay with the parent for a few days so the main caregiver can take time off (NIA). Ask the main caregiver what would help most (NIA).

  1. Handle research, insurance, billing, and benefits calls during business hours.
  2. Join medical appointments by phone or video, with your parent's permission.
  3. Pay for or arrange services that lighten the local sibling's load, such as respite from a volunteer, an in-home aide, or an adult day program (NIA).
  4. Plan visits that give the main caregiver real time off (NIA); see our long-distance caregiving guide.

How should we handle money, and should the sibling who does the most care get paid?

Talk about it openly: decide who pays for what, track spending, and make sure whoever handles money has legal authority. Some families decide to pay the sibling who does the most hands-on care; if you do, put it in writing, because it may affect taxes and benefits, and check with a tax professional or elder law attorney (a lawyer who helps older adults and their families interpret state laws, plan how wishes will be carried out, and understand financial options) before paying a family member.

  1. Decide who pays for what: your parent's own money, siblings' contributions, or both.
  2. Before paying a sibling for care, ask an elder law attorney about a written caregiver agreement; see getting paid to care for a parent. Rules for these agreements under Medicaid (health coverage run by each state for people with low income and few savings) vary by state: for example, Nevada's and North Carolina's Medicaid manuals require the written agreement to be signed before the care is given, and treat payment for care given in the past as a transfer of assets (money given away without fair value in return) (Nevada Medicaid manual, section F-415.23, MTL 04/23; North Carolina Medicaid manual MA-2240, revised September 1, 2025).
  3. Talk with a financial professional, since NIA notes that some ways of paying for care can affect taxes and inheritance (NIA).
  4. Make sure the sibling handling money has authority, such as a durable power of attorney for finances (a document naming someone to make financial decisions when your parent can't) (NIA).

How do we avoid disputes over our parent's money?

Keep records everyone can see, and act only within the authority you legally have, such as a durable power of attorney for finances (NIA). If a sibling manages a parent's Social Security, check whether Social Security requires a representative payee (a person or organization it appoints to manage benefits); see power of attorney and Social Security.

  1. Keep a shared spreadsheet of expenses with receipts.
  2. Agree on who can spend what without checking with the others, and write it in your family care agreement.
  3. Keep your parent's legal documents up to date with our documents checklist.
  4. If you suspect financial exploitation, contact your parent's local Adult Protective Services (the state or local agency that investigates abuse and exploitation of older adults) through NAPSA's Help in Your Area page (NAPSA).
  5. If a sibling becomes the representative payee, Social Security can ask them to complete an annual Representative Payee Report accounting for the benefits. Under a 2018 law (Public Law 115-165), SSA no longer requires that report from spouses, certain parents and legal guardians who live with the beneficiary, and some state mental institutions; exempt payees can still have site reviews (reviews in which Social Security checks how a payee is managing the benefits) (SSA payee FAQ; SSA POMS GN 00605.015, effective May 26, 2023).

What goes in a family care agreement?

A one- or two-page summary that everyone can see of each person's responsibilities, how you'll communicate, how money works, what happens in an emergency, and when you'll review the plan; it doesn't need to be a legal document. If it includes paying a family member for care, rules differ by state, so check with your parent's state Medicaid office (Medicaid.gov state contact list) or an elder law attorney before signing or paying.

  1. List each person's responsibilities, with a lead and a backup for every area.
  2. Set how you'll communicate: a weekly group text, a shared document, or a monthly video call.
  3. Write down how money works: what your parent pays, what siblings contribute, and how receipts are shared; before anyone signs or is paid for care, contact your parent's state Medicaid office through the Medicaid.gov state contact list.
  4. Decide what happens in an emergency: who gets called first, who travels, and who calls the others.
  5. Put the next review date on the calendar, and send the agreement to everyone, including your parent if they want a copy.

What if one sibling won't help?

You can't force them, but you can ask for something specific and small and accept that some people will say no, as NIA advises (NIA). Then look for help elsewhere, including local services through the Eldercare Locator (Eldercare Locator).

  1. Ask for one specific task, such as "Could you call Dad every Tuesday?"
  2. Offer options: time, money, or a skill.
  3. Look for other help: friends, neighbors, faith communities, paid help, and local services through the Eldercare Locator (eldercare.acl.gov, 1-800-677-1116).
  4. Consider elder mediation if conflict is affecting your parent's care: a mediator (a neutral third party who helps people talk through a disagreement) helps families make eldercare decisions together (ABA).

What if our parent wants only one of us involved?

Respect your parent's wishes as much as possible, especially about who receives medical information: under the HIPAA Privacy Rule (the federal rule that protects health information), providers may share with family involved in care if your parent agrees or doesn't object, and are not required to share otherwise unless the family member is a personal representative (HHS). You can still support the chosen sibling in other ways.

  1. Ask your parent who they want to share medical information with, and let the doctor's office know.
  2. Support the chosen sibling with emotional support and specific tasks, as NIA suggests for anyone who isn't the main caregiver (NIA).
  3. Agree on how updates will reach the rest of the family.

What if we just can't agree?

Bring in a neutral mediator: elder mediation is a process that helps families work through eldercare decisions (ABA). Keep the focus on your parent's needs and wishes.

  1. Write down each sibling's main concern and what your parent has said they want.
  2. Consider an elder mediator; elder mediation helps families make eldercare decisions together (ABA).
  3. Keep each discussion focused on your parent's needs and wishes, not past disagreements.

How often should we revisit the plan?

Every few months, and after any big change such as a hospital stay, a move, or a new diagnosis, since NIA advises being realistic and planning to revisit care decisions as needs change (NIA). Don't wait for the scheduled review if the main caregiver shows signs of stress that NIA lists, such as exhaustion, impatience, or trouble sleeping (NIA).

  1. Put the next review on the calendar now.
  2. Watch for warning signs: one person quietly picking up others' tasks; the main caregiver skipping their own appointments, losing sleep, or sounding short-tempered (signs of caregiver stress in NIA's list, NIA; see caregiver burnout warning signs); updates stopping; or your parent's needs changing while the plan hasn't.
  3. Call a short family meeting and ask: "Who is carrying too much right now?"
  4. Update your written family care agreement.
