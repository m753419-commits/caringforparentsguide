Talking With Your Parent

How to Talk to Your Aging Parent About Needing Help

By Ellen Carter. Published October 31, 2026. Publisher: Family Caregiver Practical Guide.

This article contains affiliate links. As an Amazon Associate I earn from qualifying purchases. How affiliate links work on this site.

The first time I tried to talk with my mother about getting some help at home, I did everything wrong. I brought it up in the car on the way back from the grocery store, I opened with "Mom, we need to talk about you living alone," and I had a list of things I had already decided. She went quiet, then said she was perfectly fine, and we didn't speak about it again for months.

What I hadn't understood was that she didn't hear "help." She heard "you're losing your independence," "your daughter thinks you're failing," and "decisions are being made without you." Of course she pushed back. I would have too.

The second time went differently. I picked a calm afternoon, I asked more than I told, and I started with what she wanted, which was to stay in her own house as long as possible. That one shift turned the conversation from an argument into a plan we made together. This guide is everything I wish I had known before that first car ride.

Please note: This post is general information, not medical, legal, or financial advice. If you have concerns about a parent's health or safety, talk with their doctor or another qualified professional.

Key takeaways

  • Start with your parent's goals, not your worries, so the conversation is about keeping what they value.
  • Ask open questions and listen far more than you talk.
  • Expect several short conversations, not one big one.
  • Offer choices and small trial steps so your parent stays in control.
  • Agree on one concrete next step and a date to check in.

Why Parents Push Back

Resistance rarely means your parent doesn't see the problem. More often, it's about what accepting help seems to mean. Common reasons include:

  • Fear of losing independence. Help can feel like the first step toward leaving home.
  • Pride and role reversal. They spent decades taking care of you. Being on the other side can feel embarrassing.
  • Worry about cost. Many parents assume any help will be expensive, or don't want to "waste" money meant for the family.
  • Feeling ambushed. A conversation that seems pre-decided, especially with several family members at once, feels like a verdict rather than a discussion.
  • Not being asked. If you jump straight to solutions, your parent has nothing left to decide except "no."

When you understand which of these is in play, you can speak to that fear directly instead of arguing about the dishes piling up.

Before You Talk: Prepare

A little preparation keeps you calm and focused.

  1. Get clear on what you've noticed. Write down specific, recent observations, such as missed bills, an unstocked fridge, or difficulty with stairs. Specifics are easier to discuss than "you're not doing well."
  2. Decide on one goal. For a first conversation, the goal might just be "agree that we'll look at options together," not "get Mom to accept an aide three days a week."
  3. Coordinate with siblings first. Agree on who will start the conversation, so your parent doesn't hear three different messages. If your family needs a plan for sharing the work, see our guide to splitting caregiving responsibilities among siblings.
  4. Know a few options in advance. Read up on what's available locally so you can describe real choices. The Eldercare Locator (eldercare.acl.gov, 1-800-677-1116) can point you to local services such as in-home help and transportation.
  5. Pick the right moment. Choose a quiet, private time when no one is tired, rushed, or upset. Avoid holidays with the whole family watching, and avoid the day after a scare when emotions are high, unless safety requires it.

Conversation Openers That Lower Defensiveness

The first sentence sets the tone. These openers invite your parent in instead of putting them on trial. Adapt the wording so it sounds like you.

Start with their goals

  • "You've always said you want to stay in this house. I want that too. Can we talk about what would make that easier?"
  • "What matters most to you about the next few years?"

Use "I" statements

  • "I've been worrying about you on the stairs, and I'd feel better if we talked about it."
  • "I need some help understanding how things are going day to day, because I'm too far away to see it."

Ask for their advice

  • "A friend of mine is figuring out help for her dad. What do you think she should do?"
  • "If you were in my shoes, how would you want me to bring this up?"

Make it about a small, specific thing

  • "I noticed the yard is getting to be a lot. Would it help to have someone mow every couple of weeks?"
  • "Would you be open to trying grocery delivery for a month, just to see if you like it?"

Give them the door to say not yet

  • "We don't have to decide anything today. I just want to start talking about it."

Instead of Saying This, Try This

Instead of saying... Try... Why it helps
"You can't live alone anymore." "What would help you keep living here safely?" Focuses on their goal, not a verdict
"You need a caregiver." "Would a few hours of help a week make things easier?" Small and specific feels manageable
"We've all decided..." "I'd like us to figure this out together." Keeps your parent in charge of their life
"You forgot to pay the electric bill again." "I saw a late notice. Can we look at the bills together?" Describes, doesn't blame
"It's for your own good." "It would really ease my mind." Honest about your feelings without lecturing

A Question Checklist for the Conversation

Open questions get you real information and show respect. You won't ask all of these at once. Pick a few for each conversation.

Daily life

  • ☐ What part of the day feels hardest right now?
  • ☐ Is there anything around the house that's gotten harder to keep up with?
  • ☐ How are you managing groceries, meals, and errands?
  • ☐ Are you getting out to see friends as much as you'd like?

Home and getting around

  • ☐ Are there places in the house that feel tricky, like stairs, the tub, or dark hallways?
  • ☐ How do you feel about driving these days? Would rides sometimes be nice?

Money and paperwork

  • ☐ Are the bills and mail feeling manageable?
  • ☐ Is there anyone you'd want to help with paperwork if you ever needed it?

Preferences and plans

  • ☐ If you needed some help, what kind would you be comfortable with? From whom?
  • ☐ What would you never want?
  • ☐ Who do you want involved in decisions, and who would you rather leave out?
  • ☐ Where are your important papers, and who should know? (Our documents checklist can help.)

Closing

  • ☐ What's one small thing we could try first?
  • ☐ When would be a good time to talk about this again?

During the Conversation

  • Listen more than you talk. Let silence happen. Your parent may need a moment before saying what really worries them.
  • Reflect back what you hear. "So the biggest thing is not wanting strangers in the house. Did I get that right?"
  • Acknowledge feelings before solving. "That makes sense. I'd feel the same way."
  • Offer choices, not ultimatums. Two or three options let your parent choose, which protects their sense of control.
  • Suggest a trial. "Let's try it for a month and then decide" is far easier to accept than a permanent change.
  • Stop before it becomes a fight. If voices rise, it's fine to say, "Let's take a break and come back to this next week."

After the Talk: Follow Through

End every conversation with one concrete next step: a phone call to make, a service to try, a date to revisit. Then do what you said you'd do. Following through builds trust for the next, harder conversation.

Share a short update with siblings so everyone hears the same thing. The Family Caregiver Alliance suggests keeping a written summary of what each person agreed to, and holding family discussions regularly rather than expecting to settle everything at once. If the conversation grows into a larger family discussion, our guide to sharing care among siblings walks through how to divide tasks.

When Your Parent Still Says No

Sometimes, even with your best approach, the answer is no. A few things to remember:

  • An adult parent who can make their own decisions has the right to make choices you disagree with. Your role is to inform, offer, and stay connected.
  • Keep the door open. "Okay. Can I bring it up again in a month?" often works better than pressing.
  • Try a different messenger. Some parents listen more readily to a trusted friend, a faith leader, or their doctor.
  • Bring in a neutral helper. A social worker or a professional care manager can facilitate a family conversation. The Family Caregiver Alliance notes that an outside facilitator can be helpful when families have trouble coming to agreement.
  • Act when safety is at real risk. If you believe your parent is in immediate danger, call 911. If you're worried about self-neglect or exploitation, contact your local Adult Protective Services or ask the Eldercare Locator where to turn.

And take care of yourself. Repeated hard conversations wear you down. If you notice you're running on empty, read our guide to caregiver burnout warning signs.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the right time to talk to my parent about getting help?

Earlier is usually better, before a fall or crisis forces fast decisions. A calm conversation while your parent is doing fairly well gives them more say and gives everyone more options. Choose a quiet, private moment rather than a holiday gathering.

What if my parent gets angry or shuts down?

Stop pushing and acknowledge the feeling: "I can tell this is upsetting. Let's pause." Come back to it another day, perhaps with a smaller, more specific question. Anger often comes from fear of losing control, so offering choices can help next time.

Should all the siblings be there for the conversation?

Usually not for the first talk. Several adult children at once can feel like an intervention. Agree as siblings beforehand, let one trusted person start the conversation, and include others in later family meetings.

How do I talk about help when I live far away?

Use regular phone or video calls and ask open questions about daily life. Ask your parent what they'd like to do during your next visit, and use visits to see how things are going in person. A local relative, friend, or care manager can also help you understand what's happening.

Can I make my parent accept help?

If your parent is able to make their own decisions, generally no. You can share your concerns, offer options, and keep the conversation going. If you're worried about their ability to make safe decisions, talk with their doctor or an elder law attorney about your options.
