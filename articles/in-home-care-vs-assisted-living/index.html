In-Home Care vs. Assisted Living: How to Decide What's Right

Published October 31, 2026. Publisher: Family Caregiver Practical Guide.

"Should Mom stay home with help, or move somewhere with more support?" It's one of the hardest questions families face. There's rarely one right answer, and the right answer can change over time.

In this post, I'll walk through what each option usually includes, how they compare on cost, safety, and quality of life, and the questions that tend to make the decision clearer.

Please note: This post is general information, not legal, financial, or medical advice. Ask your parent's doctor about their care needs, and verify costs and coverage with providers and agencies.

What "In-Home Care" Usually Means

In-home care covers a wide range of help in your parent's own home:

  • Non-medical help (often called homemaker or personal care): help with bathing, dressing, meals, housekeeping, errands, and companionship.
  • Home health care: skilled services such as nursing or therapy ordered by a doctor. Medicare may cover part-time or intermittent skilled home health care for people who meet its conditions, but it does not cover long-term custodial care.
  • Family and volunteer help, often combined with paid help.

What "Assisted Living" Usually Means

Assisted living communities generally offer housing, meals, housekeeping, activities, and help with personal care, with staff available around the clock. They aren't nursing homes, which provide a higher level of medical care. Rules and services vary by state and by community, so ask each community exactly what they provide.

The Cost Picture

Here are national median costs from the CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey. Actual costs vary widely by location and by how much care your parent needs.

Type of care 2025 national median
Non-medical in-home caregiver $35 per hour ($6,673 per month, based on 44 hours a week)
Assisted living community $6,200 per month ($74,400 per year)
Adult day health care $95 per day
Nursing home, semi-private room $315 per day ($9,581 per month)
Nursing home, private room $355 per day ($10,798 per month)

Source: CareScout Cost of Care Survey, 2025.

Notice that in-home care costs depend heavily on hours. A few hours a week of help may cost far less than assisted living. Round-the-clock in-home care can cost much more.

Also keep in mind: Medicare.gov states that Medicare doesn't pay for long-term custodial care, whether at home, in assisted living, or in a nursing home. Some people qualify for long-term care through Medicaid, depending on their state's rules, and some have private long-term care insurance.

A Side-by-Side Comparison

Factor In-home care Assisted living
Where your parent lives Their own home A private apartment or room in a community
Familiarity Highest: own home, neighbors, routines New surroundings; can bring personal belongings
Supervision Only when a caregiver is there, unless 24-hour care is arranged Staff available 24 hours
Social contact Can be isolating without visits or programs Built-in activities and dining with others
Home safety Family must handle modifications Built for older adults
Cost pattern Rises with hours of help Monthly fee, often plus extra for higher care levels
Family workload Often heavy: scheduling, backup, home upkeep Lighter for daily tasks; still advocacy and visits
Flexibility Easy to add or reduce hours Moving in and out is a bigger step

Questions That Help You Decide

About your parent's needs

  • What help does your parent need with daily tasks such as bathing, dressing, toileting, eating, and moving around?
  • Are there safety concerns, such as falls, wandering, or forgetting the stove?
  • Does your parent need help at night?
  • What does their doctor recommend?

About your parent's wishes

  • Where does your parent want to live? What matters most to them: their garden, their neighbors, their independence, their privacy?
  • How do they feel about having a stranger in the house versus moving?

About the family

  • Who can realistically provide backup when a paid caregiver doesn't show up?
  • Is the main family caregiver close to burnout?

About money

  • What can your parent afford, and for how long?
  • Might they qualify for Medicaid, VA benefits, or other programs? Your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) and the Eldercare Locator can help you find out.

A Simple Way to Score the Options

Imagine a mother who lives alone, needs help bathing twice a week, has had one fall, and feels lonely since her husband died. Her children could rate each option from 1 (poor fit) to 5 (great fit):

Totals don't decide for you, but filling this in together often shows what matters most.

Options in Between (and Beyond)

The choice isn't always "home or assisted living." Other options may fit, alone or in combination:

  • Adult day programs offer supervision, activities, and meals during the day, which can let a parent stay home while giving family caregivers time to work or rest.
  • PACE (Programs of All-Inclusive Care for the Elderly) is a Medicare and Medicaid program available in some areas. According to Medicare.gov, it serves people who are 55 or older, live in a PACE service area, are certified by their state as needing a nursing home level of care, and can live safely in the community with PACE's help.
  • Memory care is a specialized setting, sometimes within assisted living, for people living with dementia. Ask what training staff have and how the space is secured.
  • Nursing homes provide a higher level of care, including 24-hour nursing. Medicare.gov's Care Compare tool lets you look up nursing homes and compare information about them.
  • Moving in with family can work for some families, but it's a big change for everyone. Talk honestly about privacy, workload, and money first.

Planning the Transition

Whatever you choose, the change itself deserves a plan. If you're adding in-home help, introduce the caregiver gradually, ideally while a family member is there, and share a short page about your parent's routines, preferences, and dislikes. If your parent is moving, involve them in choosing what to bring, set up their new room with familiar things before they arrive, and expect an adjustment period that may take weeks or longer. Keep a simple log of how they're eating, sleeping, and feeling, and bring concerns to staff or the agency early and calmly.

When to Revisit the Decision

No decision is permanent. Plan to look at it again after any hospital stay, fall, new diagnosis, or change in who is available to help, and at least every few months even when nothing dramatic happens. A good question to ask each time: "Is this arrangement still keeping Mom safe, and is it still something she would choose?"

Visiting and Vetting

For in-home care agencies, ask: - Are caregivers employees, screened, trained, and insured? - What happens if a caregiver is sick or doesn't show up? - How is the care plan made and updated?

For assisted living communities, ask: - What's included in the base rate, and what costs extra? - How are care levels assessed, and how are price increases handled? - What happens if my parent's needs increase, or if money runs out? - Can we read the contract before signing?

Your state's Long-Term Care Ombudsman Program can tell you about residents' rights and complaints. Use the Eldercare Locator (eldercare.acl.gov or 1-800-677-1116) to find local resources.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is in-home care always cheaper than assisted living?

No. It depends on hours. Part-time help is often less expensive; full-time or 24-hour help can cost more than assisted living.

Does Medicare pay for assisted living?

Medicare doesn't pay for long-term custodial care, including most assisted living costs. Check Medicare.gov and your parent's plan for any services that are covered.

Can we try in-home care first?

Yes, many families do. Set a check-in date to review whether it's working and what would signal a need for change.

What if my parent refuses to move?

Respect their voice, focus on safety, involve their doctor, and revisit the conversation. Adding more help at home is often a reasonable middle step.

Key takeaways

  • In-home care offers familiarity and flexibility; assisted living offers built-in supervision and social life.
  • Costs depend heavily on hours of help and location. Use current local quotes.
  • Medicare doesn't cover long-term custodial care.
  • Start with your parent's needs and wishes, then look at family capacity and money.
