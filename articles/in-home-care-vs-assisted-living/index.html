In-Home Care vs. Assisted Living: How to Decide What's Right

Does Medicare cover assisted living?

No: Medicare doesn't pay for assisted living, and it doesn't pay for long-term care in general (Medicare.gov; NIA).

Assisted living is, in the National Institute on Aging's words, "for people who need help with daily care, but not as much help as a nursing home provides." Most people pay the full cost themselves. To look for help paying:

  1. Ask your parent's state Medicaid office. NIA says Medicaid may cover some aspects of assisted living, depending on the state and whether your parent is eligible. Find your state's office at Medicaid.gov.
  2. Check for PACE. The Program of All-Inclusive Care for the Elderly (PACE) is a Medicare and Medicaid program in some states. Medicare.gov says your parent can join if they are at least 55, live in a PACE service area, need a nursing-home level of care (as certified by the state), and can live safely in the community with PACE's help (Medicare.gov, PACE).
  3. If your parent is a veteran, ask VA. NIA notes that VA covers long-term care for some veterans (NIA, Paying for long-term care).
  4. Read any long-term care insurance policy your parent owns, and call the insurer about what it pays for.
  5. Get free local help. The Eldercare Locator (eldercare.acl.gov, from the Administration for Community Living) connects you to your local Area Agency on Aging.

Does Medicare cover nursing homes?

Only for a short skilled stay after a hospital admission, not for long-term living in a nursing home (Medicare.gov).

  1. Check the 3-day rule. Medicare Part A (hospital insurance) covers care in a skilled nursing facility (SNF: a facility with daily skilled nursing or therapy) generally after a qualifying inpatient hospital stay of at least 3 days. Time under "observation" or in the emergency room before admission doesn't count.
  2. Know the 2026 costs per benefit period: days 1–20, $0 per day after the Part A deductible ($1,736 in 2026); days 21–100, $217 per day; after day 100, your parent pays all costs (Medicare.gov; CMS 2026 fact sheet).
  3. Plan for long-term stays separately. NIA says Medicare generally doesn't cover long-term nursing home stays; Medicaid may cover some costs for people who qualify.
  4. Compare facilities on Medicare Care Compare, and contact the Long-Term Care Ombudsman (ltcombudsman.org) with concerns about care in a facility.

Does Medicare cover home health care?

Only part-time skilled care for a parent who is homebound. Medicare doesn't pay for 24-hour care at home or for personal care when that is the only care needed (Medicare.gov).

  1. Check that your parent is "homebound." Under Medicare.gov's definition, both must be true: leaving home isn't recommended because of their condition, or they need help to leave (such as a cane, walker, wheelchair, special transportation, or another person); and they are normally unable to leave home, and leaving takes a lot of effort.
  2. Get an in-person visit and an order. A doctor or other provider such as a nurse practitioner must see your parent in person, confirm they need home health care, and order it.
  3. Use a Medicare-certified home health agency. Ask the agency to confirm it's Medicare-certified before care starts.
  4. Know the hour limits. "Part-time or intermittent" care usually means skilled nursing and home health aide services up to 8 hours a day (combined), for a maximum of 28 hours a week.
  5. Budget for what isn't covered: 24-hour care, homemaker services unrelated to the care plan, and custodial or personal care (help with bathing, dressing, or using the bathroom) when that's the only care needed.

Is assisted living cheaper than in-home care?

It depends on hours: at CareScout's national medians ($35 an hour in 2025 for in-home care, $6,200 a month in 2025 for assisted living), more than about 41 hours a week of paid help at home costs more than assisted living (our calculation from those 2025 medians: $35 × 41 hours × 52 weeks ÷ 12 months ≈ $6,218 a month); a few hours a week costs much less (CareScout).

  1. Count the hours. Write down how many hours of help your parent needs each week, including nights.
  2. Multiply by a local hourly quote from two or three home care agencies, then add home costs (rent or mortgage, utilities, food, home repairs or safety changes).
  3. Get assisted living quotes in writing, including extra charges. NIA notes that residents pay more if they need extra services or special care.
  4. Look at in-between options. Adult day care can cover weekday hours for less than full-time home help.
  5. Check local prices with the CareScout cost tool and ask your Area Agency on Aging (Eldercare Locator) about low-cost local services.

How do I know whether my loved one needs assisted living or in-home care?

Match the setting to the help needed: in-home care fits when a few hours of help a day keeps your parent safe; assisted living fits when they need help and supervision around the clock but not daily skilled nursing (NIA). In-home care ranges from non-medical help (bathing, dressing, meals, housekeeping, companionship) to home health care (skilled nursing or therapy ordered by a doctor).

  1. List the daily tasks your parent needs help with. Include activities of daily living (ADLs: bathing, dressing, eating, using the bathroom, moving around) and tasks like cooking, shopping, and managing medicines.
  2. Ask whether your parent is safe alone at night and between visits (falls, wandering, missed medicines). NIA says assisted living offers 24-hour supervision, security, and on-site staff (NIA, Long-term care facilities).
  3. Ask your parent's doctor what level of care they need now and what may change.
  4. Ask your parent what they want. Their preferences matter, and NIA suggests talking with family about the pros and cons of each option before deciding (NIA, Aging in place).
  5. Ask the right questions before you sign. Home care agencies: are caregivers employees, screened, trained, and insured; what happens if a caregiver doesn't show up; how is the care plan updated? Assisted living: what's in the base rate and what costs extra; how are care levels and price increases handled; what happens if needs increase or money runs out; can you read the contract first? The Long-Term Care Ombudsman (ltcombudsman.org) can explain residents' rights.

Can we try in-home care first?

Yes: NIA notes that most people prefer to stay in their own home for as long as possible, and that family, friends, and neighbors are the biggest source of help for many older people (NIA, Aging in place).

  1. Talk with your parent and family about what support is needed for your parent to stay at home, as NIA suggests (NIA).
  2. Find local services through your Area Agency on Aging (Eldercare Locator, 1-800-677-1116).
  3. Consider adult day care. NIA says adult day care programs can offer social activities, exercise, meals, and personal care during the day (NIA).
  4. Set a check-in date to review whether the plan is working, and agree in advance on what would signal a need for change (see the next question).

When is it time for assisted living?

When your parent can no longer live at home safely or comfortably, even with the help you can arrange and afford (NIA).

  1. Watch for safety signs: repeated falls, missed or doubled medicines, not eating, getting lost, or needing help at night that no one can provide.
  2. Compare the cost of adding hours at home with the cost of assisted living (see the table above).
  3. Check your own limits. If family caregivers can't keep up, that is part of the decision.
  4. Consider in-between options: adult day programs, PACE (see the first question), memory care for a parent living with dementia (ask about staff training and how the space is secured), or a nursing home if 24-hour nursing is needed.
  5. Set a date to review the plan, for example every few months or after any hospital stay, and change it when needs change.

What if my parent refuses help?

Keep the conversation going and offer specific, practical help; NIA suggests mentioning your concern without sounding critical and trying to fulfill your parent's wishes as far as possible (NIA). If you're worried about their ability to make decisions, talk with their doctor, who can give guidance on it, or an elder law attorney (a lawyer who helps older adults and their families interpret state laws, plan how wishes will be carried out, and understand financial options) (NIA, Legal and Financial Planning).

  1. Name one worry and offer one practical fix. NIA suggests mentioning your concern without sounding critical, trying to meet your parent's wishes as far as possible, and giving specific examples of help, such as arranging regular grocery delivery (NIA).
  2. If your parent gets angry or shuts down, stop pushing, acknowledge the feeling, and come back to it another day with a smaller, more specific question.
  3. Suggest a doctor visit if you're worried about their health, memory, or judgment; NIA suggests offering to make the appointment, give them a ride, or go with them (NIA).
  4. Put what you agree on in writing. The Family Caregiver Alliance suggests a written summary of what each person has agreed to and a schedule for the next family meeting (Family Caregiver Alliance).
  5. Act when safety is at real risk. If you believe your parent is in immediate danger, call 911. If you suspect abuse, neglect, or self-neglect (when a person can't meet their own basic needs, which threatens their health and safety), contact your local Adult Protective Services (APS: the state or local agency whose workers investigate abuse, neglect, self-neglect, and financial exploitation of older adults and adults with disabilities, and arrange services to keep them safe) (NAPSA). Find your state's APS number on NAPSA's Help in Your Area page, or ask the Eldercare Locator (1-800-677-1116) where to turn.

What if my parent refuses to move?

Respect your parent's voice, focus on safety, involve their doctor, and revisit the conversation. Adding more help at home is often a reasonable middle step; NIA notes that most people prefer to stay in their own home for as long as possible (NIA, Aging in place).

  1. Add help at home first (see Can we try in-home care first?). Your Area Agency on Aging can point you to local services (Eldercare Locator, 1-800-677-1116).
  2. Involve their doctor if you're worried about falls, confusion, or other health changes (NIA).
  3. Agree on the signs that would mean it's time to look again, such as the safety signs in When is it time for assisted living?
  4. Set a date to revisit the conversation, and use the steps in What if my parent refuses help? if it gets heated.
