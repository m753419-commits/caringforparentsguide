In-Home Care vs. Assisted Living: How to Decide What's Right

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 7, 2026

US programs only.

In plain words: In-home care fits when a few hours of help a day keeps your parent safe. Assisted living fits when they need help and supervision around the clock but not daily skilled nursing. Medicare is U.S. government health insurance for older people and some younger people with disabilities. It doesn't pay for assisted living, so plan how to pay and ask your parent what they want.

Does Medicare cover assisted living?

No, Medicare doesn't pay for assisted living. It doesn't pay for long-term care in general (Medicare.gov; NIA).

The National Institute on Aging (NIA, a federal research agency) describes assisted living. It is "for people who need help with daily care, but not as much help as a nursing home provides." Most people pay the full cost themselves. Here is how to look for help paying.

  1. Ask your parent's state Medicaid office. Medicaid is a joint federal and state program. It helps with health costs for people with limited income and resources. NIA says Medicaid may cover some aspects of assisted living. It depends on the state and whether your parent is eligible (qualifies). Find your state's office at Medicaid.gov.
  2. Check for PACE. The Program of All-Inclusive Care for the Elderly (PACE) is a Medicare and Medicaid program in some states. Medicare.gov says your parent can join if all of these are true. They are at least 55 and live in a PACE service area. They need a nursing-home level of care (as certified by the state). And they can live safely in the community with PACE's help (Medicare.gov, PACE).
  3. If your parent is a veteran, ask VA. VA is the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs. NIA notes that VA covers long-term care for some veterans (NIA, Paying for long-term care).
  4. Read any long-term care insurance policy your parent owns. Call the insurer about what it pays for.
  5. Get free local help. The Eldercare Locator (eldercare.acl.gov) is from the Administration for Community Living. It connects you to your local Area Agency on Aging. That is a local office that helps older adults find services.

Does Medicare cover nursing homes?

Only for a short skilled stay after a hospital admission. It doesn't cover long-term living in a nursing home (Medicare.gov).

  1. Check the 3-day rule. Medicare Part A (hospital insurance) covers care in a skilled nursing facility (SNF). An SNF is a facility with daily skilled nursing or therapy. This is generally after a qualifying inpatient hospital stay of at least 3 days. Inpatient means admitted to the hospital. Time under "observation" or in the emergency room before admission doesn't count. Observation is outpatient care while doctors decide whether to admit someone.
  2. Know the 2026 costs per benefit period. A benefit period is how Medicare counts use of hospital and SNF care. For days 1–20, your parent pays $0 per day after the Part A deductible ($1,736 in 2026). The deductible is what your parent pays before Medicare pays. For days 21–100, it is $217 per day. After day 100, your parent pays all costs (Medicare.gov; CMS 2026 fact sheet).
  3. Plan for long-term stays separately. NIA says Medicare generally doesn't cover long-term nursing home stays. Medicaid may cover some costs for people who qualify.
  4. Compare facilities on Medicare Care Compare, Medicare's tool for comparing them. Contact the Long-Term Care Ombudsman (ltcombudsman.org) with concerns about care in a facility. An ombudsman speaks up for residents.

Does Medicare cover home health care?

Only part-time skilled care for a parent who is homebound. Medicare doesn't pay for 24-hour care at home or for personal care when that is the only care needed (Medicare.gov).

  1. Check that your parent is "homebound." Under Medicare.gov's definition, two things must both be true. First, leaving home isn't recommended because of their condition, or they need help to leave. Examples of help are a cane, walker, or wheelchair. Special transportation or another person also counts. Second, they are normally unable to leave home, and leaving takes a lot of effort.
  2. Get an in-person visit and an order. A doctor or other provider must see your parent in person. One example is a nurse practitioner, a nurse with advanced training. The provider must also confirm your parent needs home health care and order it.
  3. Use a Medicare-certified home health agency. That means one approved by Medicare. Ask the agency to confirm it's Medicare-certified before care starts.
  4. Know the hour limits. Intermittent means off and on. "Part-time or intermittent" care usually means skilled nursing and home health aide (help with things like walking, bathing, or grooming) services up to 8 hours a day (combined). The maximum is 28 hours a week.
  5. Budget for what isn't covered. That includes 24-hour care and homemaker services (help with chores like cleaning) unrelated to the care plan. It also includes custodial or personal care when that's the only care needed. That is help with bathing, dressing, or using the bathroom.

Is assisted living cheaper than in-home care?

It depends: above about 41 hours a week, paid help at home costs more than assisted living (our calculation from CareScout's national medians) (CareScout). A few hours a week costs much less.

  1. Count the hours. Write down how many hours of help your parent needs each week, including nights.
  2. Multiply by a local hourly quote from two or three home care agencies. Then add home costs. These include rent or mortgage, utilities, food, and home repairs or safety changes.
  3. Get assisted living quotes in writing, including extra charges. NIA notes that residents pay more if they need extra services or special care.
  4. Look at in-between options. Adult day care can cover weekday hours for less than full-time home help.
  5. Check local prices with the CareScout cost tool. Ask your Area Agency on Aging (Eldercare Locator) about low-cost local services.

How do I know whether my loved one needs assisted living or in-home care?

In-home care fits when a few hours of help a day keeps your parent safe. Assisted living fits when they need help and supervision around the clock but not daily skilled nursing (NIA).

Match the setting to the help needed. In-home care ranges from non-medical help to home health care. Non-medical help includes bathing, dressing, meals, housekeeping, and companionship. Home health care is skilled nursing or therapy ordered by a doctor.

  1. List the daily tasks your parent needs help with. Include activities of daily living (ADLs). These are bathing, dressing, eating, using the bathroom, and moving around. Also include tasks like cooking, shopping, and managing medicines.
  2. Ask whether your parent is safe alone at night and between visits. Think about falls, wandering, or missed medicines. NIA says assisted living offers 24-hour supervision, security, and on-site staff (NIA, Long-term care facilities).
  3. Ask your parent's doctor what level of care they need now and what may change.
  4. Ask your parent what they want. Their preferences matter. NIA suggests talking with family about the pros and cons of each option before deciding (NIA, Aging in place).
  5. Ask the right questions before you sign. For home care agencies, ask: Are caregivers employees, screened, trained, and insured? What happens if a caregiver doesn't show up? How is the care plan updated? For assisted living, ask: What's in the base rate, and what costs extra? How are care levels and price increases handled? What happens if needs increase or money runs out? Can you read the contract first? The Long-Term Care Ombudsman (ltcombudsman.org) can explain residents' rights.

Can we try in-home care first?

Yes, and NIA notes that most people prefer to stay in their own home for as long as possible. It also notes that family, friends, and neighbors are the biggest source of help for many older people (NIA, Aging in place).

  1. Talk with your parent and family, as NIA suggests. Talk about what support is needed for your parent to stay at home (NIA).
  2. Find local services through your Area Agency on Aging (Eldercare Locator, 1-800-677-1116).
  3. Consider adult day care. NIA says adult day care programs can offer social activities, exercise, meals, and personal care during the day (NIA).
  4. Set a check-in date to review whether the plan is working. Agree in advance on what would signal a need for change (see the next question).

When is it time for assisted living?

When your parent can no longer live at home safely or comfortably, even with help you can arrange and afford (NIA).

  1. Watch for safety signs. These include repeated falls, missed or doubled medicines, not eating, or getting lost. Another is needing help at night that no one can provide.
  2. Compare the cost of adding hours at home with the cost of assisted living (see the table above).
  3. Check your own limits. If family caregivers can't keep up, that is part of the decision.
  4. Consider in-between options. One is adult day programs. Another is PACE (see the first question). Memory care is care for a parent living with dementia. Ask about staff training and how the space is secured. A nursing home is an option if 24-hour nursing is needed.
  5. Set a date to review the plan, for example every few months or after any hospital stay. Change the plan when needs change.

What if my parent refuses help?

Keep the conversation going and offer specific, practical help (NIA). If you're worried about their ability to make decisions, talk with their doctor or an elder law attorney (NIA, Legal and Financial Planning). An elder law attorney is a lawyer who helps older adults and their families interpret state laws. They also help plan how wishes will be carried out and help families understand financial options.

  1. Name one worry and offer one practical fix. NIA suggests mentioning your concern without sounding critical. It also suggests trying to meet your parent's wishes as far as possible. And it suggests giving specific examples of help, such as arranging regular grocery delivery (NIA).
  2. If your parent gets angry or shuts down, stop pushing and acknowledge the feeling. Come back to it another day with a smaller, more specific question.
  3. Suggest a doctor visit if you're worried about their health, memory, or judgment. Their doctor can give guidance on their ability to make decisions. NIA suggests offering to make the appointment, give them a ride, or go with them (NIA).
  4. Put what you agree on in writing. The Family Caregiver Alliance suggests a written summary of what each person has agreed to. It also suggests a schedule for the next family meeting (Family Caregiver Alliance).
  5. Act when safety is at real risk. If you believe your parent is in immediate danger, call 911. If you suspect abuse, neglect, or self-neglect, contact your local Adult Protective Services (APS). Self-neglect is when a person can't meet their own basic needs. This threatens their health and safety. APS is the state or local agency for this. Its workers investigate abuse, neglect, self-neglect, and financial exploitation. This is for older adults and adults with disabilities. They also arrange services to keep them safe (NAPSA). Find your state's APS number on NAPSA's Help in Your Area page. Or ask the Eldercare Locator (1-800-677-1116) where to turn.

What if my parent refuses to move?

Respect your parent's voice, focus on safety, involve their doctor, and revisit the conversation. Adding more help at home is often a reasonable middle step (NIA, Aging in place).

  1. Add help at home first (see Can we try in-home care first?). NIA notes that most people prefer to stay in their own home for as long as possible. Your Area Agency on Aging can point you to local services (Eldercare Locator, 1-800-677-1116).
  2. Involve their doctor if you're worried about falls, confusion, or other health changes (NIA).
  3. Agree on the signs that would mean it's time to look again. One example is the safety signs in When is it time for assisted living?
  4. Set a date to revisit the conversation. If it gets heated, use the steps in What if my parent refuses help?
