How to Ask for Help as a Caregiver (and Actually Get It)

By Ellen Carter. Published October 31, 2026. Publisher: Family Caregiver Practical Guide.

For the first year I cared for my mother, people kept saying the same thing: "Let me know if you need anything." And I kept saying the same thing back: "Thanks, we're fine." Then I'd go home and fold laundry at midnight.

It wasn't that people didn't mean it. They did. The problem was that "anything" is impossible to answer when you're exhausted. I didn't have the energy to figure out what to hand off, and asking felt like admitting I couldn't cope.

What changed it was a list. A friend sat me down, made me write out every task I did in a week, and then asked, "Which of these could someone else do?" The answer was: more than half. This guide gives you that list, plus the words to ask.

Please note: This post is general information, not medical, legal, or financial advice. If you're feeling overwhelmed, hopeless, or unable to cope, please reach out to your doctor or a mental health professional. In the U.S., you can call or text 988 to reach the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline.

Key takeaways

  • "Let me know if you need anything" works better when you have a specific answer ready.
  • Write down every caregiving task, then sort what only you can do from what others could.
  • Ask for specific, time-limited help: "Could you pick up groceries Thursday?"
  • Match tasks to people's strengths and schedules.
  • Use a simple shared list or calendar so offers don't get lost.

Why Asking Is So Hard

Many caregivers struggle to ask because:

  • It feels like failing. You think you should be able to handle it.
  • You don't want to be a burden. Everyone is busy.
  • It's faster to do it yourself. Explaining feels like more work.
  • Past offers fell through. Someone said yes, then didn't follow up.
  • Your parent prefers you. And you feel guilty letting anyone else in.

All of these are understandable. None of them make the workload smaller. If you're noticing exhaustion or resentment building, our guide to caregiver burnout warning signs can help you recognize when it's time to change something.

Step 1: Make Your Task List

For one week, write down everything you do for your parent. Then sort it.

Task How often Only I can do it? Someone else could...
Groceries Weekly No Shop or set up delivery
Rides to appointments 2x a month Sometimes Drive to routine visits
Bills and paperwork Monthly Maybe Help sort mail
Yard work, snow Weekly in season No Mow, shovel, rake
Laundry Weekly No Take a load home
Meals Daily No Bring a dinner once a week
Company and conversation Daily No Visit, call, play cards
Pharmacy pickups Monthly No Pick up prescriptions
Home repairs As needed No Fix the dripping faucet
Medical decisions As needed Usually (Keep with you and your parent)

You'll likely find, as I did, that many tasks don't need you specifically.

Step 2: Match Tasks to People

Think about who's in your circle and what they're good at:

  • The organizer can manage a shared calendar or coordinate a meal train.
  • The handy neighbor can tackle small repairs or the snow.
  • The talker can call your parent twice a week for a chat.
  • The faraway sibling can handle bills, insurance calls, and online orders. See our guide to long-distance caregiving.
  • The busy friend might not have time but could drop off a meal once a month.
  • The faith community or club may have volunteers for rides or visits.

If siblings are involved, our guide to splitting caregiving responsibilities among siblings explains how to divide the bigger jobs fairly.

Step 3: Ask Clearly

Specific requests get specific answers. Here are scripts you can adapt.

When someone says "Let me know if you need anything"

  • "Thank you, I'd love that. Could you pick up Mom's groceries on Thursday? I'll send you the list."
  • "Actually, yes. Could you sit with Dad for two hours on Saturday so I can go to the dentist?"

Asking a friend directly

  • "I'm stretched pretty thin right now. Would you be willing to bring dinner over one night a week for the next month?"

Asking a sibling

  • "I've been handling the appointments and groceries. Could you take over the bills and insurance calls? I'll show you where everything is."

Asking a neighbor

  • "Would you mind keeping an eye out for Mom's lights in the morning? If the curtains aren't open by ten, could you knock or give me a call?"

Asking for a one-time favor

  • "Could you help me move Mom's bed downstairs this Saturday? It's about an hour's work."

Instead of Saying This, Try This

Instead of saying... Try... Why it helps
"We're fine, thanks." "Actually, could you help with groceries this week?" Turns a vague offer into real help
"Whenever you have time." "Could you do Tuesday at 2?" Specific requests get answered
"It's a lot, but I'll manage." "I could really use a hand with the yard this month." Honest and concrete
"You don't have to." "Thank you, that would make a real difference." Lets people feel useful
"Could you help out more?" "Could you take over the pharmacy pickups?" Clear ownership of a task

Step 4: Make It Easy to Say Yes

  • Make tasks small and time-limited. "Two hours on Saturday" beats "help with Mom."
  • Give clear instructions. A grocery list, the pharmacy address, where the spare key is.
  • Use a shared list or calendar. A simple shared document or calendar lets people sign up and see what's covered.
  • Accept help done differently. If your brother buys a different brand of yogurt, let it go.
  • Say thank you, every time.

Step 5: Bring in Outside Help When Needed

Friends and family can't cover everything. Paid or community services can fill the gaps:

  • Respite care, so you get regular breaks. Our respite care guide explains the options.
  • In-home help, like aides or homemaker services. See our comparison of in-home care vs. assisted living.
  • Local programs like meal delivery and transportation. The Eldercare Locator (eldercare.acl.gov, 1-800-677-1116) can point you to services near your parent.

When People Say No

Not everyone will help, and that's painful. Try not to take it personally; people have limits you may not see. Thank them anyway, move on to the next person, and focus your energy on those who show up.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I ask family members to help with caregiving?

Be specific. Instead of asking for "more help," name a task, a time, and what it involves. Matching tasks to each person's strengths and schedule makes it easier for them to say yes.

What can friends do to help a caregiver?

Friends can bring meals, pick up groceries or prescriptions, give rides, visit or call your parent, help with yard work or repairs, or sit with your parent so you can take a break.

Why is it so hard for caregivers to ask for help?

Many caregivers feel asking means they're failing, worry about being a burden, or find it faster to do things themselves. Recognizing these feelings, and having a ready task list, makes asking easier.

What if no one offers to help?

Start by asking one person for one small, specific thing. Look beyond family to neighbors, faith communities, and local services. Your parent's Area Agency on Aging may know about caregiver support programs.
