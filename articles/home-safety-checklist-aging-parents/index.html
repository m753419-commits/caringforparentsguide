Home Safety Checklist for Aging Parents: A Room-by-Room Guide

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 7, 2026

This checklist is written for families in the United States. The resources it points to, such as the Eldercare Locator, are US services.

In plain words: This guide walks through the house for fall hazards. Many of those hazards can be changed. You do not have to fix everything at once. Start with the walking paths, the stairs, and the bathroom.

What are the most important home safety changes for an aging parent?

Start with the home hazards CDC (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, the U.S. government's main public health agency) lists as fall risks you can change. Those include throw rugs, clutter, and broken or uneven steps (CDC, Facts About Older Adult Falls; CDC, Check for Safety (2017)). Then add good lighting and bathroom grab bars (CDC, Check for Safety (2017)). More than one out of four people age 65 and older falls each year. Falling once doubles the chance of falling again (CDC).

  1. Remove throw rugs, or hold them down with double-sided tape or a non-slip backing (CDC).
  2. Clear the walking paths. Move furniture out of the way. Pick up objects from floors and stairs. Coil or tape cords next to the wall (CDC).
  3. Light the way. Put a light and switch at the top and bottom of the stairs. Put a lamp within reach of the bed. Put a night-light on the path to the bathroom (CDC).
  4. Put grab bars next to and inside the tub and next to the toilet. Add a non-slip mat or strips on the tub or shower floor (CDC).
  5. Fix loose or uneven steps and put handrails on both sides of the stairs, as long as the stairs (CDC).

What should I check in each room?

Walk through seven areas with the checklist below. The areas are entrances, living areas, stairs, kitchen, bathroom, bedroom, and the whole house. The list is built from the CDC's Check for Safety (2017). It also uses the National Institute on Aging (NIA, a federal research agency) home safety worksheet and room-by-room guide (CDC; NIA worksheet; NIA, Room by Room).

Entrances and outside

  • ☐ Front steps are not broken or uneven, and outdoor stairs have non-slip material.
  • ☐ Sturdy handrails on both sides of outdoor steps.
  • ☐ Walkways are clear of hoses, branches, and other things to trip on.
  • ☐ Outdoor lighting is adequate. Lights that turn on automatically can help.
  • ☐ A small bench or table by the door holds bags while unlocking.
  • ☐ Outside doors and windows have secure locks.

Sources: NIA, Worksheet: Home Safety Checklist. NIA, Preventing Falls at Home: Room by Room.

Living areas and hallways

  • ☐ Throw rugs removed, or secured with double-sided tape or a non-slip backing.
  • ☐ Furniture moved so paths are clear.
  • ☐ Cords coiled or taped next to the wall, not across walkways.
  • ☐ Light switches at each end of a long hall.
  • ☐ Sofas and chairs are the right height to get in and out of easily.
  • ☐ Things used often are at waist level or within easy reach.

Sources: CDC, Check for Safety (2017); NIA, Room by Room.

Stairs

  • ☐ Handrails on both sides, secure, and as long as the stairs.
  • ☐ A light and light switch at the top and bottom.
  • ☐ Nothing stored on the steps.
  • ☐ Carpet firmly attached to every step, or non-slip rubber treads on bare steps.
  • ☐ Step edges marked with bright or reflective tape.

Sources: CDC, Check for Safety (2017); NIA worksheet.

Kitchen

  • ☐ Things used often are on lower shelves, about waist high.
  • ☐ If a step stool is needed, it is sturdy and has a bar to hold. Never a chair.
  • ☐ Spills are cleaned up right away.
  • ☐ The stove has safety knobs and an automatic shut-off switch.

Sources: CDC, Check for Safety (2017); NIA, Room by Room; NIA worksheet.

Bathroom

  • ☐ Grab bars next to and inside the tub, and next to the toilet.
  • ☐ A raised (elevated) toilet seat, if getting up is hard.
  • ☐ Non-slip mat or self-stick strips on the tub or shower floor. Also next to the tub, toilet, and sink if the floor is bare.
  • ☐ A shower stool and a hand-held shower head, if bathing is tiring.
  • ☐ Water heater set at 120°F to avoid scalding.
  • ☐ A night-light for trips to the bathroom at night.

Sources: CDC, Check for Safety (2017); NIA worksheet; MedlinePlus, Bathroom safety for adults.

Bedroom

  • ☐ A lamp within easy reach of the bed.
  • ☐ A night-light on the path from bed to bathroom.
  • ☐ A flashlight by the bed in case the power goes out.
  • ☐ A landline or charged phone near the bed.

Sources: CDC, Check for Safety (2017); NIA, Room by Room.

Whole house

  • ☐ Smoke and carbon monoxide alarms near the kitchen and in all bedrooms, with batteries checked.
  • ☐ Emergency numbers in large print near each phone.
  • ☐ No clutter, and no repairs waiting to be done.
  • ☐ You know where pets are, so they don't cause a trip.

Sources: NIA worksheet; NIA, Room by Room.

  1. Print this list and walk through the house with your parent, ticking each item and circling anything to fix.
  2. Fix immediate dangers first, such as loose stair railings and poor lighting (NIA, Aging in Place).
  3. Sort the circled items with the quick fixes vs. bigger projects table.

Which fixes are quick, and which need a professional?

Moving rugs, cords, and everyday items and plugging in night-lights are usually quick jobs. Grab bars, extra handrails, new light switches, and repairs to steps may need a handyperson, contractor, or electrician. Grab bars must be attached securely into wall studs, or to a wood board that is attached to studs (Alaska Department of Health, "Grab Bar Basics").

  1. Do the left-column fixes on your next visit.
  2. Have an electrician add stair lights and switches, or an outlet so cords don't cross the floor (CDC).
  3. Have grab bars installed into studs. Don't mount them over a fiberglass tub surround, which has an air gap behind it (Alaska Department of Health).
  4. Ask about help paying. Call the Area Agency on Aging through the Eldercare Locator (eldercare.acl.gov, 1-800-677-1116). Many state and local governments run home modification programs (NIA).
  5. Write down what you changed and when, and keep it with your parent's other papers (see our documents checklist).

Does Medicare pay for grab bars or a bath seat?

Original Medicare is regular Medicare and doesn't pay for these items, and Medicare is U.S. government health insurance for older people and some younger people with disabilities. Its equipment list denies grab bars, bathtub seats, and raised toilet seats as not primarily medical (CMS NCD 280.1, version effective June 9, 2025). A Medicare Advantage plan is a Medicare-approved plan from a private company, and it may choose to offer bathroom safety devices as an extra benefit. That benefit can include installation and an in-home bathroom safety inspection (CMS Medicare Managed Care Manual, Ch. 4, checked November 2026). Medicaid is a joint federal and state program that helps with health costs for people with limited income and resources.

  1. If your parent has a Medicare Advantage plan, call the number on the plan card. Ask whether it offers a "bathroom safety devices" benefit.
  2. If your parent has Medicaid, ask the state Medicaid office whether its home and community-based services waiver covers home modifications.
  3. Call the Area Agency on Aging through the Eldercare Locator about local home modification programs (NIA).
  4. Check your state housing finance agency, social services department, and community development groups for repair aid or discounts (NIA, Aging in Place).

How do I get my parent to agree to home safety changes?

Make it their project. Do the walk-through together with the CDC's checklist, and let your parent choose which fixes come first. That checklist is written for older adults to use in their own homes (CDC, Check for Safety (2017)). NIA notes that it may not be necessary to make every suggested change (NIA worksheet).

  1. Print the CDC checklist and ask your parent where they feel least steady before you point anything out.
  2. Agree on the immediate dangers first, such as loose stair railings and poor lighting (NIA). Let your parent order the rest.
  3. Offer one change as a trial, such as using a shower chair for a month.
  4. If you live far away, do the walk-through on a video call. Ask a nearby relative or neighbor to handle small fixes.

Who can do a professional home safety assessment?

An occupational therapist (a licensed clinician who helps people do everyday activities safely) can assess the home. The CDC's fall-prevention guide tells doctors to refer patients to one when home hazards are likely (CDC STEADI Pocket Guide). After a fall, a doctor might also suggest a physical therapist or nurse visit (NIA).

  1. Ask your parent's doctor for a referral to an occupational therapist for a home safety evaluation.
  2. If your parent has a Medicare Advantage plan, ask about an in-home bathroom safety inspection. Ask whether that comes with its bathroom safety devices benefit (CMS).
  3. Call the Area Agency on Aging (Eldercare Locator, 1-800-677-1116) about local home modification programs (NIA).
  4. Bring your completed checklist to the visit so the therapist sees what you have already found.

How often should we review home safety?

We suggest a walk-through every six months. Do it sooner after a fall, a hospital stay, a new medicine, or new trouble walking or seeing. NIA says to reevaluate home safety every so often as needs change (NIA worksheet).

  1. Put a reminder on your calendar six months from today.
  2. Redo the checklist and compare it with your written record of past changes.
  3. After a fall, tell your parent's doctor. The CDC says health care providers can help reduce fall risks such as medicines, vision problems, and weakness (CDC).
  4. If the house is getting hard to manage even with changes, compare options. See our guides to in-home care vs. assisted living and what care costs.

Are towel bars safe to use as grab bars?

No. Towel racks can't support a person's weight and may come off the wall (MedlinePlus; AHRQ). MedlinePlus (a free health information website from the U.S. National Library of Medicine) is cited here. AHRQ (Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, a U.S. government agency that works to make health care safer and better) is cited here.

  1. Install real grab bars next to and inside the tub and next to the toilet (CDC).
  2. Attach them into wall studs, or have a professional do it (Alaska Department of Health).
  3. Skip suction-cup grab bars, which can release without warning (Alaska Department of Health).
  4. Check installed grab bars now and then to make sure they are not loosening (Alaska Department of Health).
