Home and Safety

Home Safety Checklist for Aging Parents: A Room-by-Room Guide

By Ellen Carter. Published October 31, 2026. Publisher: Family Caregiver Practical Guide.

After my mother finally agreed to "look at a few things" around her house, I showed up with a notepad and every intention of being helpful. Within ten minutes I had pointed out the loose hallway runner, the dim stairwell, the bath mat that slid when you stepped on it, and the extension cord snaking across the living room. She folded her arms and said, "So the house is a disaster now?"

It wasn't a disaster. It was the house she had lived in for forty years, set up for the person she had been at fifty. What changed my approach was walking through it with her, one room at a time, and asking where she felt unsteady instead of announcing what I'd spotted. She knew things I didn't: which step creaked, which light switch was too far from the door, which cabinet she'd stopped using because it meant standing on a chair.

This guide is the checklist we ended up building together. Most of the fixes are small, inexpensive, and quick, and many of them simply make the house easier for anyone to live in.

Please note: This post is general information, not medical, legal, or financial advice. If your parent has had a fall, has balance or vision changes, or takes medicines that make them dizzy, talk with their doctor. An occupational therapist can also do a professional home safety assessment.

Key takeaways

  • Walk through the house together and ask where your parent feels unsteady; they know the trouble spots.
  • Start with the big three: clear walkways, good lighting, and support in the bathroom.
  • Most fixes are small and cheap, such as removing throw rugs, adding night-lights, and moving everyday items within reach.
  • Fix one or two things at a time so the house still feels like home.
  • Write down what you changed and check again in six months.

Why Home Safety Is Worth a Weekend

Falls are a common reason older adults lose independence, and the home is where many of them happen. According to the CDC, more than one out of four people age 65 and older falls each year, but less than half tell their doctor, and falling once doubles the chance of falling again. The CDC lists home hazards such as broken or uneven steps, throw rugs, and clutter among the risk factors that can be changed (CDC, Facts About Older Adult Falls).

That last point is the encouraging part. You can't change your parent's age, but you can change the stairs, the lighting, and the bathroom.

Before You Start: How to Bring It Up

If you read our guide on how to talk to your aging parent about needing help, the same principles apply here:

  • Lead with their goal. "You want to stay in this house. Let's make it easier to do that."
  • Make it a walk-through, not an inspection. Ask, "Where do you feel least steady?" before you point anything out.
  • Offer choices. "Would you rather have a grab bar or a sturdy shower chair first?"
  • Keep it about the house, not about them. "These stairs are steep for anyone" lands better than "You're unsteady on the stairs."

The Room-by-Room Checklist

Print this or copy it into a notebook. Tick each item as you check it, and circle anything to fix.

Entrances and Outside

  • ☐ Steps are even and in good repair, with no loose bricks or boards
  • ☐ Sturdy handrails on both sides of outdoor steps, if possible
  • ☐ Walkways are clear of hoses, planters, and wet leaves
  • ☐ Outdoor lights work, ideally on a timer or motion sensor
  • ☐ House number is easy to read from the street (useful for deliveries and emergency services)
  • ☐ A place to set down bags while unlocking the door

Living Areas and Hallways

  • ☐ Throw rugs removed, or secured flat with non-slip backing
  • ☐ Clear paths through each room, with furniture moved out of walkways
  • ☐ Electrical and phone cords run along walls, not across floors
  • ☐ Light switches reachable at the entrance to each room
  • ☐ Favorite chair is stable, a comfortable height, and has armrests
  • ☐ Frequently used items are on low shelves or tables, not on the floor

Stairs

  • ☐ Handrails on both sides, running the full length of the stairs
  • ☐ Light switches at both the top and bottom
  • ☐ Nothing stored on the steps, not even "just for now"
  • ☐ Carpet is tight, with no loose edges; bare steps have non-slip treads
  • ☐ Edges of steps are easy to see (a strip of contrasting tape can help)

Kitchen

  • ☐ Everyday dishes, pots, and food are between waist and shoulder height
  • ☐ A sturdy step stool with a handle, if one is needed at all, instead of a chair
  • ☐ Spills are easy to see and wipe up; no floor wax that leaves a slick surface
  • ☐ Stove knobs are easy to read; a timer is handy
  • ☐ Smoke detector nearby works (test it)

Bathroom

  • ☐ Grab bars by the toilet and in the tub or shower (towel racks are not grab bars)
  • ☐ Non-slip mat or adhesive strips inside the tub or shower
  • ☐ Non-slip bath mat outside the tub
  • ☐ A night-light so the path from bed to bathroom is lit
  • ☐ Shower chair or tub bench, and a handheld shower head, if standing is tiring
  • ☐ Raised toilet seat, if getting up is hard
  • ☐ Door can be unlocked from outside in an emergency

Bedroom

  • ☐ A lamp or touch light within reach of the bed
  • ☐ Clear path from bed to bathroom, with night-lights along the way
  • ☐ Bed is a comfortable height for sitting down and standing up
  • ☐ Phone within reach of the bed
  • ☐ Clothes they wear most are in easy-to-reach drawers and closet rods

Whole House

  • ☐ Smoke and carbon monoxide detectors on every level, tested
  • ☐ Emergency numbers written in large print near the phone
  • ☐ A working flashlight in the bedroom and kitchen
  • ☐ Thermostat and water heater set to comfortable, safe temperatures
  • ☐ Pets' bowls, toys, and beds out of walking paths

Quick Fixes vs. Bigger Projects

Not everything has to happen at once. This table helps you sort the list.

Usually quick and inexpensive May need a handyperson or contractor
Removing or securing throw rugs Installing grab bars into wall studs
Plug-in night-lights Adding a second stair handrail
Moving everyday items within reach Repairing uneven outdoor steps
Taping down or rerouting cords Walk-in shower or tub conversion
Non-slip mats in the tub Adding wiring for new light switches
Brighter bulbs Ramps at entrances

Grab bars in particular should be anchored properly. If you're not confident doing it yourself, hire someone who is. Your local Area Agency on Aging may know about home repair or modification programs for older adults; the Eldercare Locator (eldercare.acl.gov, 1-800-677-1116) can connect you.

Making Changes Your Parent Will Accept

The best safety change is the one that stays in place. A few things that helped us:

  • Fix one or two things per visit. A whole-house makeover can feel like the house is being taken away.
  • Let your parent choose the order. My mother picked the stair light first because the dark stairwell had been bothering her for years.
  • Pick items that look like home. Grab bars now come in finishes that match ordinary bathroom fixtures.
  • Respect what matters to them. If a rug was a wedding gift, find a non-slip pad rather than rolling it up and taking it away.
  • Try it for a month. "Let's try the shower chair for a month" is easier to say yes to than "forever."

What If You Live Far Away?

You can still help. Ask your parent to walk through the house with you on a video call, room by room, using this checklist. A neighbor, local relative, or friend can help with small fixes. If you can, schedule the bigger projects around a visit so you can see the work in person. Our guide to splitting caregiving responsibilities among siblings can help if a brother or sister nearby can take on the hands-on part.

Keep a Simple Record

Write down what you changed and when, and keep it with your parent's other important papers (our documents checklist has a list of what to gather). Walk through the house again every six months or after any change, such as a new medication, a hospital stay, or a fall. Needs shift over time, and so should the house.

If the checklist keeps getting longer and the house is becoming hard to manage even with changes, that is useful information too. Our comparison of in-home care vs. assisted living can help you think through next steps, and our guide to what it costs to care for an aging parent can help you plan.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the most important home safety changes for an aging parent?

Start with clear walkways, good lighting, and support in the bathroom. Removing throw rugs and clutter, adding night-lights, and installing properly anchored grab bars address hazards that come up again and again. Then work through the rest of the checklist at your parent's pace.

How do I get my parent to agree to home safety changes?

Frame the changes as a way to help them stay in their own home, which is usually what they want. Walk through the house together, ask where they feel unsteady, and let them choose what to fix first. Small trials, like using a shower chair for a month, are easier to accept.

Who can do a professional home safety assessment?

An occupational therapist can assess the home and suggest changes based on your parent's abilities. Ask your parent's doctor about a referral. Your local Area Agency on Aging may also know about home safety or modification programs.

How often should we review home safety?

A walk-through every six months is a reasonable habit. Do it sooner after any change, such as a fall, a hospital stay, a new medication, or new trouble with walking or vision.

Are towel bars safe to use as grab bars?

No. Towel bars aren't designed to hold a person's weight and can pull out of the wall. Install real grab bars anchored into wall studs or with appropriate anchors, or have a professional do it.
