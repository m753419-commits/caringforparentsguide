Moving an Aging Parent Into Your Home: Questions to Ask First

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 3, 2026

This guide is written for families in the United States; the resources it points to, such as the Eldercare Locator, are US services.

Please note: This post is general information, not medical, legal, or financial advice. Moving a parent in can affect taxes, benefits, and legal arrangements. Talk with a qualified financial professional or elder law attorney in your state before making major money or property decisions.

Questions in this guide

  1. Should my elderly parent move in with me?
  2. How do I prepare my home for an elderly parent?
  3. What should we agree on in writing?

Should my elderly parent move in with me?

It depends on your parent's wishes and care needs, your household, your work, and your finances. The National Institute on Aging (NIA) lists living with a family member as one housing option and suggests talking with family about the pros and cons of each option before deciding (NIA; NIA).

  1. Ask your parent directly what they want, since NIA says asking directly is the best way to know what someone needs (NIA).
  2. Talk with everyone who lives in your home about who will help day to day, whether your parent would be alone during the day, and whether there is a private room, ideally on the ground floor near a bathroom.
  3. Check what it means for your job; see taking leave to care for a parent.
  4. Compare the alternatives in in-home care vs. assisted living, and talk with siblings about how they'll contribute (splitting caregiving among siblings).
  5. Consider a trial, such as a longer visit of a few weeks, to see what living together is really like.

How do I prepare my home for an elderly parent?

Start with safety: go room by room and fix immediate dangers, such as loose stair railings and poor lighting, first (NIA). Common priorities include a main-floor bedroom, anchored bathroom grab bars, night-lights, handrails, and clear walkways.

  1. Walk through your home with our room-by-room home safety checklist.
  2. Make these changes before moving day: a bedroom on the main floor if possible; grab bars in the bathroom, properly anchored (see why towel bars aren't grab bars); night-lights from bedroom to bathroom; walkways clear, with throw rugs removed or secured; handrails on stairs; and a chair with armrests in shared spaces.
  3. Ask about a professional assessment if your parent has trouble moving around; see who can do a home safety assessment.
  4. Look for help paying for repairs through your state housing finance agency, social services department, or community development groups, as NIA suggests (NIA).

What should we agree on in writing?

Write down how money, chores, meals, privacy, guests, breaks, and check-ins will work. If money changes hands, or your parent is giving or selling property to family, talk with an elder law attorney or financial professional first.

Topic Questions to settle
Money Will your parent contribute to rent, food, or utilities? How much and how often?
Chores What will your parent do? What will others do?
Meals Eat together every night, or separately sometimes?
Privacy When is the door closed? Who can enter whose room?
Guests Can your parent have visitors? Can you still host friends?
Breaks When do you get time off, and who covers?
Review When will you check in on how it's going?
  1. Draft the agreement together with your parent and everyone in your household, using the table above.
  2. Decide what happens to your parent's home: sold, rented, or kept.
  3. Before money or property changes hands, see an elder law attorney (a lawyer who focuses on older adults' legal issues); the National Academy of Elder Law Attorneys has a find-a-lawyer tool (NAELA).
  4. Put a review date on the calendar.

Should my parent pay rent if they live with me?

There's no single right answer, and many families agree on some contribution to household costs. Because money arrangements can affect taxes and benefits, write down what you agree and talk with a financial professional or elder law attorney first.

  1. Agree on the amount and what it covers, and record it in your written agreement.
  2. Ask a tax professional about credits, such as the child and dependent care credit, which can apply to care for a person who can't care for themselves and lived with you for more than half the year (IRS Publication 503, 2025); see whether caregiving expenses are tax-deductible.
  3. If your parent gets Supplemental Security Income (SSI, a monthly Social Security payment for people with little income and resources), know that Social Security says paying part of the rent (called rental liability) puts your parent in their own household. When your parent lives with you, that only works if your parent and your household function as separate economic units (SSA POMS SI 00835.120).
  4. Know how SSI counts help with housing. SSA can lower SSI when others pay your parent's shelter costs; it calls this in-kind support and maintenance (ISM: shelter, and in some cases meals, that others pay for or provide) (SSA Spotlight on Living Arrangements, 2026 edition). For someone in their own household, SSA values ISM at no more than $351.33 a month in 2026, which is one-third of the 2026 federal SSI maximum of $994 plus $20 (SSA POMS SI 00835.901, 2026 table); the federal maximum is $1,029 a month in 2027 (SSA, 2027 SSI amounts). Ask Social Security how your arrangement will be counted.
  5. If your parent has or may need Medicaid (health coverage run by each state for people with low income and few savings), ask your parent's state Medicaid office how rent payments would affect eligibility. Also know that states must seek repayment from the estates of enrollees age 55 or older for nursing facility and home and community-based services (Medicaid.gov, Estate Recovery), and ask an elder law attorney how that applies to your parent's home.

What happens to my parent's Medicare plan or Medicaid if they move in with me?

If your parent has a Medicare Advantage Plan (a private plan offered instead of Original Medicare) or a Medicare drug plan and moves out of the plan's service area, they get a Special Enrollment Period (SEP, a special chance to switch plans because of a life event such as a move) that lasts until 2 full months after the move (Medicare.gov). If your parent has Medicaid, apply with or ask the new state's Medicaid office.

  1. Tell the plan before the move if you can. Medicare.gov says the chance to switch then begins the month before the month of the move and continues for 2 full months after it (Medicare.gov).
  2. Choose a new plan in time. During the SEP, your parent can switch to a new Medicare Advantage Plan (with or without drug coverage) or a Medicare drug plan; if they move out of their old Medicare Advantage Plan's area and don't join a new one, they'll be enrolled in Original Medicare when the old plan drops them (Medicare.gov).
  3. Apply with or ask the new state's Medicaid office (find it through Medicaid.gov). Federal rules say a state can't deny Medicaid because someone hasn't lived there for a set length of time (42 CFR 435.403).

Can I get paid to care for my parent if they live with me?

Possibly: NIA says many states offer some form of pay for family caregivers, most often through Medicaid programs, and some long-term care insurance (private insurance that pays for ongoing daily care) policies pay a family member (NIA). Rules vary by state.

  1. Read our guide to getting paid to care for a parent and the section on Medicaid.
  2. Call the Eldercare Locator (eldercare.acl.gov, 1-800-677-1116) to ask about programs in your parent's area (NIA).
  3. Check your parent's long-term care insurance policy to see who can be paid to provide care (NIA).
  4. If your parent will pay you from their own money, ask an elder law attorney about a written personal care agreement (a contract that describes the care and the pay). Medicaid rules on these agreements vary by state: for example, Nevada's and North Carolina's Medicaid manuals require the written agreement to be signed before the care is given, and treat payment for care given in the past as a transfer of assets (money given away without fair value in return) (Nevada Medicaid manual, section F-415.23, MTL 04/23; North Carolina Medicaid manual MA-2240, revised September 1, 2025).

What if living together stops working?

Agree from the start on what you'll do if your parent needs more care than you can give, such as more in-home help, adult day programs, or assisted living (housing with help with daily activities). NIA advises being realistic and revisiting the decision as needs change (NIA).

  1. Set a regular review date for the arrangement.
  2. Schedule breaks: see how to find respite care, and call the Eldercare Locator at 1-800-677-1116 to find local programs (Eldercare Locator).
  3. Watch for burnout with our caregiver burnout warning signs, and see how to ask for help.
  4. Plan for the costs and paperwork with what care costs and our documents checklist.
