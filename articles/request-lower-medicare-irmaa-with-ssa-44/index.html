Request a Lower Medicare IRMAA With SSA-44

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 9, 2026

Maybe your parent stopped working, and now the premium notice, Social Security's letter showing what your parent pays for Medicare each month, has an extra charge you didn't expect. If the income drop came from a change on Social Security's list, your parent can ask Social Security to use a newer year, when income was lower. The request goes on Form SSA-44. It isn't an appeal, and sending it doesn't by itself remove the charge.

Last checked: November 2026 · US programs only.

This is general information, not advice for one person's plan. Check Medicare.gov or call 1-800-MEDICARE before you act. 1-800-MEDICARE is Medicare's official phone line, 1-800-633-4227.

You can also ask Social Security (the federal agency that pays retirement and disability benefits). Or ask SHIP (the State Health Insurance Assistance Program) for free, fair Medicare help (full disclaimer).

In plain words: IRMAA is an extra monthly Medicare charge for people whose income is higher. Medicare is U.S. government health insurance for older people and some younger people with disabilities. SSA-44 is the Social Security form for a listed life change that lowered income. Start with the premium notice.

The extra charge can land on Part B, which is Medicare's medical insurance for doctor visits, outpatient care, medical equipment and many preventive services. It can also land on a Medicare drug plan, if your parent has one. That's Medicare Part D: private insurance, approved by Medicare, that helps pay for prescription drugs.

It isn't a charge from a private supplement policy.

What is this extra charge?

IRMAA is added when income is higher. It can sit on Part B, on a drug plan, or on both, but only on coverage your parent actually has. A drug plan still charges its own premium, and IRMAA comes on top of it. Social Security's Medicare premiums page spells this out.

The income behind it isn't this month's paycheck. It isn't a bank balance, either. Social Security uses modified adjusted gross income: the adjusted gross income on the federal tax return, plus tax-exempt interest, meaning interest that isn't taxed.

What to do first: read the premium notice. It shows the year of the charge and the tax year Social Security used, and you'll need both later.

When not to file: if the notice shows no extra charge, don't send SSA-44. Retirement alone isn't a reason to file.

Can retirement lead to a new decision?

Sometimes. It can, if your parent or their spouse stopped work or cut back hours, and that change lowered the income Social Security counts. Not every drop in income counts.

Social Security calls these life-changing events, and Form SSA-44 lists exactly which ones it accepts:

  • Marriage, divorce, annulment (a legal end of a marriage), or a spouse's death.
  • Stopped work or fewer hours, for your parent or for the spouse.
  • Some property losses. A sale or transfer your parent chose doesn't count.
  • Some pension losses, where an employer pension stopped, ended, or was reorganized. The form calls that a scheduled change.
  • Some employer settlements tied to bankruptcy or reorganization. Don't treat every employer payment as this event.

Whatever the event, the income counted has to go down because of it. The event date must also fall in the tax year you ask Social Security to use, or in an earlier year.

Which income year can we ask Social Security to use?

Don't assume it's the retirement year. The notice shows which tax year Social Security used, and the form explains which newer year your parent can ask for. Social Security's premiums page says that for 2026 premiums, it generally used the 2024 tax year. Sometimes it used 2023.

Here's how the examples in Form SSA-44 work for a 2026 premium. Treat them as examples, not as a rule for every year.

If the following year will be lower still, your parent can add an estimate for that year too. In the form's example, a 2026 main year is followed by 2027.

An estimate is the income your parent expects on that year's tax return, plus tax-exempt interest, along with the filing status they expect to use. After they file, send a signed copy of the return. If the estimate changes, tell Social Security. Later corrections can include back charges or refunds. This guide doesn't give a refund date or a dollar result.

What papers should we include?

Social Security needs two kinds of proof: proof of the life change, and proof of income for the year you're asking it to use. Your parent signs the form. Never type a Social Security number into this website. The checklist below follows Form SSA-44.

  1. Income for the year you ask for. If that tax return is already filed, send a signed copy. You can also send a transcript from the IRS, the U.S. tax agency; a transcript is an IRS printout of the return. If you send an estimate instead, send the signed return once it's filed. Social Security says it will check the estimate with the IRS later.
  2. Stopped work or fewer hours. The form asks for an original signed statement from the employer, or copies of pay stubs. Original papers or certified copies that show a business transfer also work. A certified copy is one an official office has confirmed.
  3. Missing work papers. Ask Social Security what else it can accept. Don't assume no proof is needed.
  4. Other events. The form names a marriage record, a divorce or annulment decree, a death record, a letter from the pension plan, or a letter about the employer settlement.

Two situations look similar but aren't SSA-44 cases:

  • An amended tax return is a different update. Social Security needs the amended return and the IRS receipt. If the IRS income figure itself is wrong, fix it with the IRS.
  • Married filing separately, and living apart all year, is different too. Call Social Security and explain. Don't use SSA-44 only for that change.

Keep a copy of the notice and everything you send. The form also asks for a phone number and the current mailing address.

How do we send the request?

There are three routes, according to Social Security's Request to lower IRMAA page:

  • Sign in online. The online path says Sign in, but you don't need an account just to read the form.
  • Fax or mail the completed form and the proof to a Social Security office.
  • Show original papers, or certified copies, to a Social Security employee. Ask which office can take them.

Skip the Baltimore address printed on the form. It's for comments about how long the form takes, not for filing.

Questions about this request go to Social Security at 1-800-772-1213, not to 1-800-MEDICARE. The TTY line, a text phone line, is 1-800-325-0778. The official pages don't agree on the opening hour, so this guide doesn't state one. It also didn't sign in or file a form.

Is this the same as an appeal?

No. Social Security's premiums page says you don't need to file an appeal when you're asking for a new decision because a listed event made your parent's income go down. This guide found no single 60-day deadline for SSA-44. That doesn't mean timing never matters: go by the dates and instructions in your parent's notice, and ask Social Security if anything is unclear.

Common mistake: Reaching for the appeal form when the only thing that changed is your parent's income.

If your parent does disagree with the decision, the written appeal is Form SSA-561-U2, a request for Social Security to look again; the premiums page names it as the appeal form. Social Security's reconsideration page, a page about a second look at a decision, says to ask within 60 days after you receive the decision. The 60 days aren't counted from the retirement date.

When does your parent “receive” it? Social Security's IRMAA rules send reconsideration requests to its general appeal rules (20 CFR 418.1325 and 20 CFR 418.1340). Under those rules, the 60 days run from the date your parent receives the notice (20 CFR 404.909), and Social Security's appeal definitions (20 CFR 404.901) treat the notice as received 5 days after the date on it, unless your parent shows it arrived later.

Read the decision letter for the timing, and read it again before you ask about a late request. This guide doesn't state a processing time or a refund amount.

One more check: if Social Security says your parent has a drug plan and they don't, call CMS, the federal agency that runs Medicare, at 1-800-MEDICARE. Social Security's premiums page says it gets those coverage facts from CMS.

For current-year premium amounts, use the official premiums page. This guide doesn't copy the bracket table, because one top amount doesn't match across official versions. It's the highest Part B line for someone who is married, lived with their spouse at some time during the tax year, and filed a separate return: the premiums page shows “Standard premium + $487.90,” while Form SSA-44 shows a Part B monthly adjustment of $487.00 for the same line.

Medigap, a private policy that helps pay some costs in Original Medicare (regular Medicare), is a different charge. The guides below are about that policy, not this extra charge.
