Talking With Your Parent

How to Talk to an Aging Parent About Driving

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 3, 2026

This guide is written for families in the United States, where driver's licenses and DMV (Department of Motor Vehicles, the state office that issues licenses) rules are set by each state.

Please note: This post is general information, not medical, legal, or financial advice. Only a qualified professional can assess whether someone is safe to drive. If you have concerns about a parent's vision, memory, or health, talk with their doctor. If there's an immediate danger, call 911.

How do I know if my elderly parent should stop driving?

The National Institute on Aging (NIA) lists eight signs that a loved one should no longer be driving, including crashes, near misses, or new dents on the car, and a doctor recommending they change their driving or stop (NIA, content reviewed December 20, 2022). Only a qualified professional can assess whether someone is safe to drive.

  1. Watch for NIA's eight warning signs (NIA, content reviewed December 20, 2022): multiple vehicle crashes, near misses, or new dents or scrapes on the car; two or more traffic tickets or warnings within the last two years; increases in car insurance premiums because of driving issues; comments from neighbors or friends about erratic, unsafe, or aggressive driving; anxiety about driving at night; health issues that might affect driving, including problems with vision, hearing, and movement; complaints about the speed, sudden lane changes, or actions of other drivers; and recommendations from a doctor to modify driving habits or quit driving entirely.
  2. Write down what you notice and when, since specific, recent examples are easier to discuss than "you're not driving well."
  3. Suggest a visit to your parent's doctor if you notice health or memory changes, and offer to make the appointment, give a ride, or go along, as NIA suggests (NIA).
  4. Call 911 if you believe your parent or others are in immediate danger.

How do I start the conversation about driving?

Talk about how your parent will keep getting to the places they care about, not about taking the keys, and mention your worry without sounding critical (NIA). Have ride options ready before you ask them to drive less.

  1. Gather your observations: dates, places, and what happened.
  2. Research rides first, through the Eldercare Locator (eldercare.acl.gov, 1-800-677-1116), which can point you to local transportation services (Eldercare Locator).
  3. Agree with siblings on one calm message, delivered by the person your parent trusts most; see splitting caregiving responsibilities among siblings.
  4. Pick a private, calm time, not right after an incident unless safety requires it, and offer practical help that respects your parent's wishes (NIA).
  5. If your parent says no, keep the conversation going, bring in the doctor or a trusted friend, and see what to do if your parent refuses help.

Can my parent drive less instead of stopping completely?

Often: many families cut back in stages, such as no night or highway driving, before stopping completely, and NIA suggests cutting back on or stopping driving at night (NIA). Each stage gives your parent time to adjust and shows you how the ride options are working.

  1. Stop driving at night or in bad weather (NIA).
  2. Skip highways and unfamiliar routes.
  3. Keep to shorter trips close to home.
  4. Drive only with a passenger.
  5. Stop driving, with a full ride plan in place (see transportation options).

Who can evaluate an older person's driving?

A driver rehabilitation specialist, often an occupational therapist (a health professional who helps people do everyday tasks safely), can give an in-depth evaluation of a person's driving and may suggest training or special equipment for the car (NHTSA). NIA notes there may be fees for these assessments (NIA), so ask about the cost and whether Medicare or other insurance covers it before you book.

  1. Ask your parent to raise driving with their doctor at the next appointment, and offer to go along (NIA).
  2. Ask the doctor about a referral for a professional driving evaluation. NHTSA says the specialist can check whether a condition such as Parkinson's, stroke, or diabetes is affecting your parent's driving (NHTSA).
  3. Look into a driving refresher course for older drivers. NHTSA says AARP's Driver Safety Program is the largest national program, and AAA and the National Safety Council also offer courses through many local offices. Insurers in most states offer a car insurance discount for completing one, sometimes for several years after the course (NHTSA); ask your parent's insurer whether it does.

What transportation options are there for seniors who don't drive?

Options may include family and friends, volunteer drivers, senior center or community shuttles, public transit, taxis, rideshare apps, and delivery services. The Eldercare Locator can help you find local transportation services (Eldercare Locator).

  1. List the places your parent goes each week, such as the grocery store, doctor, place of worship, and friends.
  2. Call the Eldercare Locator at 1-800-677-1116 and ask your parent's Area Agency on Aging (the local public or nonprofit office for older-adult services), which may have lists of local services (NIA).
  3. Match each place to a ride or delivery option, and ask family, friends, and neighbors who could drive to appointments or errands (NIA).
  4. Budget with the money the car cost, since insurance, gas, and repairs saved can help pay for rides; see what it costs to care for an aging parent.

Can I report an unsafe elderly driver?

Usually: NHTSA says that in almost every state, a family member can report a driver by writing a letter to the DMV in the state where the older driver lives, and the DMV is required to examine the claims carefully (NHTSA). In an emergency, call 911.

  1. Find your parent's state DMV through USA.gov's list of state motor vehicle services (USA.gov), then look for how that state handles driver reports.
  2. Write down your observations with dates and places, and ask whether your letter will be kept confidential; NHTSA says that depends on the state (NHTSA).
  3. Expect follow-up. NHTSA says that even if your parent is re-examined and passes, the DMV may still require future periodic reviews (NHTSA).
  4. Tell your parent's doctor about your concerns, since only a qualified professional can assess whether someone is safe to drive.
