Talking With Your Parent

How to Talk to an Aging Parent About Driving

By Ellen Carter. Published October 31, 2026. Publisher: Family Caregiver Practical Guide.

This article contains affiliate links. As an Amazon Associate I earn from qualifying purchases. How affiliate links work on this site.

My mother's car was her freedom. It took her to church, to the hairdresser, to Thursday lunch with the same three friends she'd known since the 1970s. So when I noticed fresh scrapes on the bumper and she mentioned, almost proudly, that she "only drives on back roads now," I knew this conversation would be the hardest one yet.

I put it off for months. When I finally brought it up, I made the mistake of leading with the keys. She heard "I'm taking away your life," and in a way, she wasn't wrong. What eventually worked was talking about how she would keep getting to the places she loved, not about the car itself.

If you're worried about a parent's driving, this guide will help you notice what's happening, start the conversation gently, and plan for a life with fewer miles behind the wheel.

Please note: This post is general information, not medical, legal, or financial advice. Only a qualified professional can assess whether someone is safe to drive. If you have concerns about a parent's vision, memory, or health, talk with their doctor. If there's an immediate danger, call 911.

Key takeaways

  • Watch for patterns over time, not a single bad moment.
  • Talk about getting places, not about taking the keys.
  • Small steps, like no night or highway driving, can come before stopping entirely.
  • A doctor or professional driving evaluation can provide a neutral opinion.
  • Line up rides and alternatives before the car goes away.

Signs It May Be Time to Talk

No single moment proves anything; everyone has an off day. Look for patterns. Ride along with your parent when you can, and notice:

  • ☐ New dents, scrapes, or damage on the car or garage
  • ☐ Getting lost on familiar routes
  • ☐ Trouble staying in the lane, or drifting
  • ☐ Missing stop signs or traffic lights
  • ☐ Confusing the gas and brake pedals
  • ☐ Driving much slower or faster than traffic
  • ☐ Other drivers honking often
  • ☐ Close calls or near-misses your parent mentions
  • ☐ Traffic tickets or warnings
  • ☐ Friends or neighbors quietly mentioning concerns
  • ☐ Your parent seeming nervous or exhausted after driving

Write down what you notice and when. Specific, recent examples are much easier to discuss than "you're not driving well."

Why This Conversation Is So Hard

For many older adults, driving means independence, identity, and social connection. Giving it up can mean missing friends, depending on others, and feeling old. Expect strong feelings, and remember they're about losing much more than a car.

Our guide on how to talk to your aging parent about needing help covers the general approach. Here's how to apply it to driving.

Before the Conversation

  1. Gather your observations. Dates, places, what happened.
  2. Research alternatives first. Know what rides exist in your parent's area before you ask them to drive less. The Eldercare Locator (eldercare.acl.gov, 1-800-677-1116) can point you to local transportation services.
  3. Agree with siblings. One calm message, delivered by the person your parent trusts most. See our guide to splitting caregiving responsibilities among siblings if this becomes a family discussion.
  4. Pick the right time. Private, calm, and not right after an incident when everyone is upset, unless safety requires it.

Conversation Openers

Start with their goals

  • "I know how much your Thursday lunches mean to you. I want to make sure you can keep going."
  • "What would help you feel confident getting around this winter?"

Use "I" statements

  • "I've been worried since the scrape on the car, and I'd feel better if we talked about it."

Suggest small changes first

  • "Would you be open to skipping night driving for a while?"
  • "How about I drive when we go on the highway?"

Invite a neutral opinion

  • "Would you be willing to ask your doctor at your next appointment whether driving is still a good idea?"

Instead of Saying This, Try This

Instead of saying... Try... Why it helps
"You have to stop driving." "How can we make sure you keep getting where you want to go?" Focuses on what they keep, not what they lose
"You're a danger on the road." "I noticed the scrape on the bumper. What happened?" Describes, doesn't accuse
"Give me the keys." "Would you try skipping night driving for a month?" A small, reversible step
"We've decided..." "I'd like us to figure this out together." Keeps your parent in control
"You'll just have to ask for rides." "Let's set up a ride plan before anything changes." Shows you've thought about their life

Gradual Steps Before Stopping

Many families move through stages:

  1. No driving at night or in bad weather
  2. No highways or unfamiliar routes
  3. Shorter trips close to home
  4. Driving only with a passenger
  5. Stopping, with a full ride plan in place

Each step gives your parent time to adjust and lets you see how alternatives are working.

Getting a Neutral Opinion

A parent may listen to a professional more readily than to their child. Options include:

  • Their doctor, who can review health and medication factors.
  • A driving evaluation, sometimes done by an occupational therapist trained as a driver rehabilitation specialist. Ask the doctor about a referral.
  • A driving refresher course for older drivers, offered by some organizations. These can help a parent feel more confident, or show them it may be time to stop.

If you have serious safety concerns and your parent won't listen, some states allow family members to ask the motor vehicle agency to review someone's driving. Check your parent's state DMV website for how this works there.

Build the Ride Plan

Giving up the car only works if your parent can still get where they want to go. Map out the places they go each week and how they'll get there:

Destination How often Ride option
Grocery store Weekly Delivery, family, or senior shuttle
Doctor appointments Monthly Family member or community transport
Place of worship Weekly Friend from the congregation
Friends or clubs Weekly Friends, rideshare, or senior center van
Hair salon, errands Every few weeks Family or rideshare

Options may include family and friends, volunteer driver programs, senior center or community shuttles, public transit with reduced fares, taxis or rideshare apps, and delivery services. If the car is sold, the money saved on insurance, gas, and repairs can help pay for rides. Our guide to what it costs to care for an aging parent can help you budget.

When Your Parent Still Says No

  • Keep the conversation going. "Can we talk about it again next month?"
  • Bring in the doctor or a trusted friend.
  • Stay connected. The goal is your parent's safety and wellbeing, not winning an argument.
  • Act if there's immediate danger. If you believe your parent or others are at immediate risk, call 911.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my elderly parent should stop driving?

Look for patterns, such as new dents, getting lost on familiar routes, close calls, or tickets. A single incident isn't proof. If you're concerned, ask your parent's doctor or look into a professional driving evaluation.

How do I convince my parent to stop driving?

Start with their goals, like staying connected to friends, and focus on how they'll keep getting places. Suggest small steps first, such as no night driving, and line up ride options before asking them to stop.

Who can evaluate an older person's driving?

Their doctor can review health factors, and a driver rehabilitation specialist, often an occupational therapist, can do a formal driving evaluation. Ask the doctor about a referral.

What transportation options are there for seniors who don't drive?

Options may include family and friends, volunteer drivers, senior center shuttles, public transit, taxis, rideshare apps, and delivery services. The Eldercare Locator can help you find local transportation services.

Can I report an unsafe elderly driver?

In some states, family members can ask the motor vehicle agency to review a driver. Check your parent's state DMV website. In an emergency, call 911.
