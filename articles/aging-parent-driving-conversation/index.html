Talking With Your Parent

How to Talk to an Aging Parent About Driving

Family Caregiver Practical Guide · Updated November 7, 2026.

This guide is written for families in the United States. Driver's licenses and DMV (Department of Motor Vehicles, the state office that issues licenses) rules are set by each state.

Please note: This guide is general information, not medical, legal, or financial advice. Only a qualified professional can assess whether someone is safe to drive. If you have concerns about a parent's vision, memory, or health, talk with their doctor. If there's an immediate danger, call 911.

In plain words: This guide is about talking with a parent who still drives. You may see warning signs, but only a qualified professional can say if they are safe. Start by writing down what you saw. Then talk with them, with ride options ready.

How do I know if my elderly parent should stop driving?

The National Institute on Aging (NIA, a federal research agency) lists eight signs that a loved one should no longer be driving (NIA, content reviewed December 20, 2022). Only a qualified professional can assess whether someone is safe to drive.

  1. Watch for NIA's eight warning signs (NIA, content reviewed December 20, 2022). Multiple vehicle crashes, near misses, or new dents or scrapes on the car. Two or more traffic tickets or warnings within the last two years. Increases in car insurance premiums because of driving issues. Comments from neighbors or friends about erratic, unsafe, or aggressive driving. Anxiety about driving at night. Health issues that might affect driving, including problems with vision, hearing, and movement. Complaints about the speed, sudden lane changes, or actions of other drivers. Recommendations from a doctor to modify driving habits or quit driving entirely.
  2. Write down what you notice and when. Specific, recent examples are easier to discuss than "you're not driving well."
  3. Suggest a visit to your parent's doctor if you notice health or memory changes. Offer to make the appointment, give a ride, or go along, as NIA suggests (NIA).
  4. Call 911 if you believe your parent or others are in immediate danger.

How do I start the conversation about driving?

Talk about how your parent will keep getting to the places they care about. Don't make it about taking the keys. Mention your worry without sounding critical (NIA). Have ride options ready before you ask them to drive less.

  1. Gather your observations: dates, places, and what happened.
  2. Research rides first, through the Eldercare Locator (eldercare.acl.gov, 1-800-677-1116), which can point you to local transportation services (Eldercare Locator).
  3. Agree with siblings on one calm message. The person your parent trusts most should say it. See splitting caregiving responsibilities among siblings.
  4. Pick a private, calm time. Don't start right after an incident unless safety requires it. Offer practical help that respects your parent's wishes (NIA).
  5. If your parent says no, keep the conversation going. Bring in the doctor or a trusted friend. See what to do if your parent refuses help.

Can my parent drive less instead of stopping completely?

Possibly, NIA suggests cutting back on or stopping driving at night for people who have trouble seeing in the dark. NIA also suggests routes that avoid highways, and staying off the road in bad weather ("When in doubt, don't go out") (NIA).

  1. Stop driving at night or in bad weather. NIA says to cut back on or stop driving at night if you have trouble seeing in the dark. Bad weather, such as rain, ice, or snow, can make it hard for anyone to drive. NIA says: "When in doubt, don't go out" (NIA).
  2. Skip highways. NIA suggests choosing a route that avoids highways or other high-speed roadways (NIA).
  3. Stop driving, with a full ride plan in place (see transportation options).

Who can evaluate an older person's driving?

A driver rehabilitation specialist, often an occupational therapist (a health professional who helps people do everyday tasks safely), can give an in-depth evaluation of a person's driving. They may suggest training or special equipment for the car. National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA, the U.S. government agency for road safety) gives this advice (NHTSA). NIA notes there may be fees for these assessments (NIA). Ask about the cost before you book. Also ask whether Medicare (U.S. government health insurance for older people and some younger people with disabilities) or other insurance covers it.

  1. Ask your parent to raise driving with their doctor at the next appointment, and offer to go along (NIA).
  2. Ask the doctor about a referral for a professional driving evaluation. NHTSA says the specialist can check whether a condition such as Parkinson's, stroke, or diabetes is affecting your parent's driving (NHTSA).
  3. Look into a driving refresher course for older drivers. NHTSA says AARP's Driver Safety Program is the largest national program. AARP is a large nonprofit group for people age 50 and older. AAA (a large U.S. club that helps drivers) also offers courses through many local offices. The National Safety Council (a U.S. nonprofit group that works to prevent injuries and deaths) does too. Insurers in most states offer a car insurance discount for completing one. The discount is sometimes for several years after the course (NHTSA). Ask your parent's insurer whether it does.

What transportation options are there for seniors who don't drive?

Options may include family and friends, volunteer drivers, and senior center or community shuttles. They may also include public transit, taxis, rideshare apps, and delivery services. The Eldercare Locator can help you find local transportation services (Eldercare Locator).

  1. List the places your parent goes each week. Examples are the grocery store, the doctor, a place of worship, and friends.
  2. Call the Eldercare Locator at 1-800-677-1116. Ask your parent's Area Agency on Aging. That is the local public or nonprofit office for older-adult services. It may have lists of local services (NIA).
  3. Match each place to a ride or delivery option. Ask family, friends, and neighbors who could drive to appointments or errands (NIA).
  4. Budget with the money the car cost. Insurance, gas, and repairs you no longer pay can help pay for rides. See what it costs to care for an aging parent.

Can I report an unsafe elderly driver?

Usually, in almost every state, a family member can report a driver by writing a letter to the DMV where the older driver lives. NHTSA says the DMV is required to examine the claims carefully (NHTSA). In an emergency, call 911.

  1. Find your parent's state DMV through USA.gov's list of state motor vehicle services (USA.gov). Then look for how that state handles driver reports.
  2. Write down your observations with dates and places. Ask whether your letter will be kept confidential. NHTSA says that depends on the state (NHTSA).
  3. Expect follow-up. NHTSA says that even if your parent is re-examined and passes, the DMV may still require future periodic reviews (NHTSA).
  4. Tell your parent's doctor about your concerns. Only a qualified professional can assess whether someone is safe to drive.
