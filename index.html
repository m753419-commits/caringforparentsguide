Practical help for adult children caring for an aging parent

Family Caregiver Practical Guide publishes plain-language guides for families in the United States. Use them to get organized, talk with your parent and your siblings, and prepare questions for a doctor, an elder law attorney, or a financial professional.

Each guide is general information. It is not medical, legal, or financial advice. The disclaimer explains those limits, and every guide says so again before the details.

How to use a guide

  1. Choose the guide that matches the decision in front of you. You do not need to read them in order.
  2. Copy the checklist or table into a notebook or a shared document. The goal is to see what is missing, not to finish it in one sitting.
  3. Use it as a conversation with your parent, if they are able to take part. Confirm anything legal, medical, or financial with a qualified professional in your parent's state.

Guides

  1. Paperwork & Legal

    What Documents Do You Need When Caring for an Aging Parent? (Checklist)

    If you've ever stood in a hospital hallway trying to remember whether your mom ever signed a health care proxy, you know the feeling. Paperwork isn't the most important part of caring for a parent. Your relationship is. But missing paperwork can turn a hard week into an impossible one.

  2. Sharing Care with Siblings

    How to Split Caregiving Responsibilities Fairly Among Siblings

    In many families, caregiving isn't divided. It lands. It lands on whoever lives closest, whoever has the most flexible job, whoever is the daughter, or whoever said yes first and never got the chance to say "wait." Over time, that person gets tired and resentful, and the other siblings feel shut out or judged.

  3. Finding Care

    In-Home Care vs. Assisted Living: How to Decide What's Right

    "Should Mom stay home with help, or move somewhere with more support?" It's one of the hardest questions families face. There's rarely one right answer, and the right answer can change over time.

  4. Money & Costs

    How Much Does It Cost to Care for an Aging Parent?

    When families first ask "how much will this cost?", they're usually hoping for one number. The honest answer is that it depends: on where your parent lives, how much help they need, what kind of care they choose, and what benefits they qualify for.

  5. Caregiver Self-Care

    Caregiver Burnout: Warning Signs and What to Do About It

    Caring for a parent can be meaningful. It can also be exhausting in a way that sneaks up on you. One week you're managing fine. A few months later you're snapping at your kids, lying awake at 3 a.m., and can't remember the last time you did something just for yourself.

  6. Talking With Your Parent

    How to Talk to Your Aging Parent About Needing Help

    The first time I tried to talk with my mother about getting some help at home, I did everything wrong. I brought it up in the car on the way back from the grocery store, I opened with "Mom, we need to talk about you living alone," and I had a list of things I had already decided. She went quiet, then said she was perfectly fine, and we didn't speak about it again for months.

  7. Home and Safety

    Home Safety Checklist for Aging Parents: A Room-by-Room Guide

    After my mother finally agreed to "look at a few things" around her house, I showed up with a notepad and every intention of being helpful. Within ten minutes I had pointed out the loose hallway runner, the dim stairwell, the bath mat that slid when you stepped on it, and the extension cord snaking across the living room. She folded her arms and said, "So the house is a disaster now?"

  8. Long-Distance Care

    Long-Distance Caregiving: How to Help a Parent From Far Away

    For two years, I lived a four-hour drive from my mother. Every phone call ended with "I'm fine, don't worry," and every visit showed me something the calls hadn't: a stack of unopened mail, a fridge with three cartons of expired milk, a new dent in the car she swore was "from the parking lot."

  9. Caring for Yourself

    Respite Care for Family Caregivers: How to Take a Real Break

    The first time someone suggested respite care to me, I nodded politely and changed the subject. A break sounded lovely and completely impossible. Who would make my mother's lunch the way she liked it? Who would know that she got anxious around four in the afternoon? And honestly, what kind of daughter needs a break from her own mother?

  10. Work and Caregiving

    Caring for a Parent While Working: FMLA Basics and Practical Options

    I remember the exact meeting. My phone buzzed three times in a row with my mother's number, and I stepped out of a budget review to learn she had been taken to the emergency room. She was okay. I was not. Sitting in my car afterward, I realized I had no idea what I was allowed to do. Could I take a week off? Would I lose my job? Did I have to tell my manager why?

  11. Talking With Your Parent

    How to Talk to an Aging Parent About Driving

    My mother's car was her freedom. It took her to church, to the hairdresser, to Thursday lunch with the same three friends she'd known since the 1970s. So when I noticed fresh scrapes on the bumper and she mentioned, almost proudly, that she "only drives on back roads now," I knew this conversation would be the hardest one yet.

  12. Talking With Your Parent

    Holiday Visit Checklist: What to Notice About Your Aging Parents

    The Thanksgiving I remember most wasn't the one with the burned pie. It was the year I opened my mother's refrigerator to get the cream and found almost nothing in it: a few condiments, half a loaf of bread, and a casserole dish I'd seen there in August. She'd told me on the phone every week that she was "eating fine."

  13. Caring for Yourself

    How to Ask for Help as a Caregiver (and Actually Get It)

    For the first year I cared for my mother, people kept saying the same thing: "Let me know if you need anything." And I kept saying the same thing back: "Thanks, we're fine." Then I'd go home and fold laundry at midnight.

  14. Living Arrangements

    Moving an Aging Parent Into Your Home: Questions to Ask First

    When my mother's house became too much for her, my first instinct was simple: she'll move in with us. We had a spare room. I loved her. How hard could it be?
